# Patterns

## Scope Before Search
**When to use**: The topic has too many populations, factors, settings, or time periods.
**How**: Choose simple, applied, or academic review purpose; draft the main question and subquestions; narrow population, setting, time, factors, and literature type until thorough treatment is possible.
**Trade-offs**: Narrow scope improves depth and objectivity, but may leave out peripheral context. Archive excluded material for a later question.

## Free Writing Then Clustering
**When to use**: Relevant factors are unclear or the first interpretation feels too obvious.
**How**: Free-write every connected idea for roughly 3-20 minutes without editing. Later put the abbreviated question at the center of a cluster, add spokes, and add layers. Select factors by relevance, purpose, evidence, and workability.
**Trade-offs**: Divergence exposes hidden dimensions; too much clustering delays scope control. A line in a cluster is a planning association, not evidence or causation.

## Abstract-Based Relevance Triage
**When to use**: A database search produces more articles than can be read fully.
**How**: Screen each abstract for population, factors, perspective, method, and results. Read direct matches, park loose matches, and mine related work only with an explicit bounded connection.
**Trade-offs**: Fast triage protects focus but can miss useful detail hidden in an abstract. Full reading still decides method quality, limitations, and final use.

## Living, Source-Marked Outline
**When to use**: Preliminary reading has revealed major dimensions but the evidence is still changing the argument.
**How**: Start with question and importance; add hierarchical headers, source slots, quotations, paraphrases, counterevidence, and gaps. Put author/date beside claims, mark exact words immediately, and reorder as the literature dictates.
**Trade-offs**: Updating costs time but exposes missing logic and prevents article-dump drafting. A frozen first outline preserves assumptions rather than knowledge.

## Six-Lens Critical Thinking Chain
**When to use**: Several sources address the same question and a summary is not enough.
**How**: Analyze integrity; compare similarities; contrast differences; evaluate against the specific target; synthesize compatible strands; integrate apparent contradictions. Record each move in the Critical Thinking Chart.
**Trade-offs**: The chain is slower than summarizing, but it produces traceable judgments. It does not require six separate sections; lenses can operate within topic-centered sections.

## Analysis Paragraph With Selective Minimalism
**When to use**: An article needs a defensible evidentiary role.
**How**: State only the relevant finding, then test theory, question, design, sample, evidence, interpretation, conclusion, and limitations. Explain how the weaknesses affect the topic and assign the source a bounded use: evidence, lead, perspective, or exclusion.
**Trade-offs**: Brevity protects the signal; omitting a detail that changes integrity or applicability is not minimalism but concealment.

## Strong/Weak Comparison-Contrast Test
**When to use**: Sources share a theme but may not share a defensible relationship.
**How**: Check environment, procedures, participants, evaluation method, purpose, timing, and relevant goals. Classify direct matches as strong; disclose inferred links as weak; apply the Narrow-Strand Rule when many differences compete.
**Trade-offs**: Weak links can generate hypotheses or synthesis, but should not be labeled direct evidence. More differences improve diagnosis only until they obscure which condition matters.

## Evaluation Point and Placement
**When to use**: External literature must inform a particular organization, program, population, or event.
**How**: Choose the nearest useful analogue by explanatory variables, not name. Use subquestions to compare roles, procedures, interactions, outcomes, and wild cards. Place evaluation after each major comparison/contrast and carry its essential implication into synthesis.
**Trade-offs**: Repeated evaluation improves traceability; weak or purely superficial points dilute the framework and should be qualified or removed.

## Spider-Web Synthesis and Conditional Integration
**When to use**: Compatible findings need a framework, or contrasting findings need explanation.
**How**: Sort compatible articles by the dimension, group, and setting each illuminates. Weave strands into a bounded common picture. For contradiction, inspect population, context, procedure, measure, timing, and limits; write a both-and-under-conditions account and test every link.
**Trade-offs**: A framework enables a research or action step, but the more links it adds, the greater the risk of overgeneralization, faulty logic, or an uncited hypothesis.

## Organization and If-Then Mapping
**When to use**: The evidence is understood but the reader cannot yet follow its relationships.
**How**: Choose higher-order-skill, time, group, trend, theme, method, researcher-centered, or topic-centered organization according to the evidence. Use a cluster-style if-then diagram for multiple possible paths and state every intermediate link.
**Trade-offs**: Topic-centered organization keeps analysis near the issue it explains; researcher-centered organization preserves intellectual development. Method-centered organization helps only when method explains result variation.

## Citation-as-Boundary Writing
**When to use**: A paragraph shifts from an author's finding to the reviewer's comparison, evaluation, or inference.
**How**: Attribute and cite the source claim where it ends. Start the reviewer's move after the citation with a transition such as "however" or "based on this information"; use cautious verbs for possibilities. Quote only when wording itself matters.
**Trade-offs**: More boundaries add words but prevent misattribution and accidental plagiarism. One citation at the end of a mixed paragraph is concise but ambiguous.

## COLR and Flow-Sheet Critique
**When to use**: A draft or completed publication needs an objective audit of logic, evidence, and presentation.
**How**: Outline the question, subquestions, headings, source claims, evidence quality, comparisons, examination points, conclusions, and transitions. For each article ask whether it is appropriate, accurate to its use, concise, integrated, and effective. Revisit notes after distance.
**Trade-offs**: Detailed mapping takes longer than a global impression, but reveals small evidence-to-claim errors that large conclusions hide.

## Diagnosis-Driven Revision and Editing
**When to use**: Peer or self-critique has identified weak support, sequence, interpretation, or conclusions.
**How**: Use an Expansion and Revision Plan: research more, strengthen, qualify, reorder, cut, or rewrite. Check the "so what?" link. Review and revise at least twice for a substantial review, then edit clarity, directness, voice, grammar, punctuation, lists, citations, quotations, and references; maintain a personalized error list.
**Trade-offs**: Editing before substantive revision polishes broken logic. Automated spelling/grammar checks find patterns, but reader judgment decides accuracy and fit.
