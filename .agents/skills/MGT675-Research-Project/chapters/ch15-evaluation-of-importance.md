# Chapter 15: Evaluation of Importance of the Literature to a Topic of Interest

## Core Idea

Evaluation asks what the literature means for the specific topic, situation, event, organization, or program being studied. It is not another source-quality check. It is the bridge from research conducted elsewhere to the environment in which the reviewer must make a decision, justify a study, or design an action.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Evaluation of Importance**: Assess external literature against the actual topic.
  - When to use: Use after analyzing, comparing, and contrasting sources, whenever the review must support a local conclusion, proposal, or research design.
  - How: Select an evaluation point or subgroup directly related to the topic. Compare the literature's characteristics, programs, variables, and outcomes with the specific situation. Identify what transfers, what does not, what may help or harm the situation, and which factors remain wild cards. Repeat for each important factor, then weave the smaller evaluations into a larger framework.
- **Evaluation Point Questions**: Make relevance concrete rather than intuitive.
  - When to use: Use to choose among several related programs, groups, or articles.
  - How: Ask what program or characteristic differences matter, how the cases are similar or different, whether one contains a variable absent in the other, whether human performance could change the result, which external characteristics could help or hurt, and what remains unknown.
- **Subquestion-Based Evaluation**: Link literature to a research agenda.
  - When to use: Use when the main question contains several dimensions that require separate judgments.
  - How: Turn the main question into subquestions about what is known, how the reference setting compares with the target setting, what current practice does, how effective it is, and what differences might affect adaptation.

## Key Concepts

- **Evaluation**: Assessment of literature's importance and applicability to an outside topic.
- **Evaluation point**: A specific factor, subgroup, program, or characteristic used to test relevance.
- **Context**: The environment and conditions in which a finding was produced or will be applied.
- **Subgroup**: A narrowed population or circumstance to which an evaluation applies.
- **Generalizability**: The evidence-based potential for prediction in an appropriate larger population.
- **Wild card**: A relevant characteristic whose effect is unknown or insufficiently studied.
- **Framework**: The larger explanation produced by weaving smaller evaluation results together.
- **Applicability**: The degree to which a finding can inform the target situation without exceeding its evidence.

## Mental Models

- Treat evaluation as a **bridge**, not a verdict: it connects general research to a particular setting while showing where the bridge is weak.
- Think of relevance as **relationship**, not proximity. A nearby or similarly named program may provide little useful information; a different program may reveal the key variable.
- Use subquestions as **evaluation instruments**. They prevent a broad claim that hides several unresolved comparisons.
- Build the framework from **small evaluations upward**. A local insight is useful when its place in the larger logic is explicit.

## Anti-patterns

- **Evaluating only whether an article is good**: Quality does not prove that the finding fits the target organization or population.
- **Choosing the closest label**: Superficial similarity can be less valuable than a source with the right role, variable, or outcome.
- **Jumping from literature to recommendation**: The missing comparison between research context and target context makes the recommendation speculative.
- **Ignoring wild cards**: Unknown variables can change whether an apparently useful program transfers.
- **Keeping evaluations disconnected**: A set of local observations without a framework does not answer the main question.

## Worked Example

For the question, "What impact would a Fortune 500 management training program have on teachers' classroom management effectiveness?", useful evaluation subquestions include: What is known about teachers as managers? What similarities and differences exist between teachers and business mid-level managers? What training do teachers currently receive? How effective is it? How do teacher-training and Fortune 500 manager-training programs differ?

Possible evaluation points include comparing teaching management with Wal-Mart management, Fortune 500 training with S.C.O.R.E. training, parenting skills with management skills, or elementary classroom management with college classroom management. The reviewer should not choose one because it sounds familiar. The choice depends on which comparison reveals variables that could positively or negatively affect classroom management and which remain unknown. Each small evaluation then contributes to the framework for whether adaptation is justified and what the next study should examine.

## Key Takeaways

1. Evaluate evidence against the target situation, not only against methodological standards.
2. Choose evaluation points by direct explanatory value and available evidence.
3. Use subquestions to make transfer conditions visible.
4. Identify positive, negative, and unknown contextual variables.
5. Weave individual evaluations into a framework before making a recommendation or research claim.
