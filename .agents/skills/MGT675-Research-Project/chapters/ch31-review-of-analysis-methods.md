# Chapter 31: Review of Analysis Methods

## Core Idea
The final chapter is a compact audit of the methods used to build a literature review: analysis, comparison, contrast, evaluation, synthesis, and integration. Treat them as a constructivist chain in which each systematic step builds on the last, and inspect the logic links closely enough to support a conclusion, action plan, or research design.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Analysis**: Use first for every article. Check the soundness of the theoretical framework and source interpretation, whether the question is narrow and testable, whether design and methodology fit it, whether the sample is adequate and unbiased, whether data are interpreted correctly, whether conclusions are limited and clear, and whether the contributors and sources are credible. For nonresearch articles, inspect the quality, sufficiency, and balance of the sources used.
- **Comparison and Contrast**: Use after analysis to examine similarities and differences in theoretical frameworks, limitations, assumptions, research designs, samples, results, and conclusions. Treat these dimensions as structural when they concern design or logic and substantive when they concern findings or conclusions.
- **Evaluation**: Use to assess the literature against an outside element, normally the specific office, classroom, program, event, population, or situation the review must explain. Prefer evaluation points that are closely linked, useful for insight, and supported by comparable information about programs, interactions, chain of command, support, or other relevant dynamics.
- **Synthesis and Integration**: Use synthesis to combine conclusions that do not contradict each other into a comprehensive framework. Use integration to explain differing conclusions by examining the situations studied, their limiting factors, and the conditions that could produce the variation.

## Key Concepts
- **Internal integrity**: Whether an article's logic, method, evidence, interpretation, and conclusion hold together.
- **Theoretical framework**: The logic and literature base that grounds a study or argument.
- **Research design fit**: The appropriateness of the design for the question, including experimental or path-analysis designs for causal questions.
- **Sample adequacy**: Whether sample size, type, and selection support the claims made.
- **Structural comparison**: Comparison of frameworks, assumptions, designs, limitations, or other research architecture.
- **Substantive comparison**: Comparison of results or conclusions.
- **Outside element**: The specific situation against which the literature is evaluated.
- **Comprehensive framework**: The overall picture produced by synthesis and integration.
- **Critical Thinking Chart**: The open working table that links article summaries to comparison, contrast, evaluation, synthesis, and integration points.
- **Logic link**: A defensible connection between successive assessment steps or claims.

## Mental Models
- **Use the chain as a gate.** An article that fails analysis should not silently carry the same weight into comparison, evaluation, or synthesis.
- **Think in two axes for every source.** Ask whether the article is internally sound and whether it is relevant to the external situation; strong performance on one axis does not guarantee the other.
- **Treat disagreement as information.** Contrast identifies variation, evaluation identifies local relevance, and integration explains the conditions under which different findings can coexist.
- **Use the Critical Thinking Chart as a living map.** Add relationships when new articles make an earlier point significant; do not assume entries must remain beside the source that first suggested them.

## Anti-patterns
- Accepting a study's conclusion without checking question, design, sample, method, interpretation, and limitations.
- Calling two articles similar or different without specifying whether the comparison is structural or substantive.
- Evaluating a source against a vague general topic rather than the specific situation that matters.
- Treating synthesis as a list of common findings or integration as an unsupported explanation for disagreement.
- Allowing a missing logic link or an overgeneralized conclusion to survive because the overall argument sounds persuasive.

## Worked Example
To audit an article about whether a management program improves employee performance, first ask whether its theoretical framework is sound, the question is sufficiently narrow, the design measures performance rather than only attitudes, and the sample is large and unbiased enough for the conclusion. Compare it with another article on framework, sample, design, and results; contrast the different follow-up periods and measures. Evaluate both against the focal organization, whose employees and implementation period may differ. Synthesize the evidence that the program improves performance when reinforcement is present, then integrate a weaker or null result by noting its short follow-up and self-report measure. The final conclusion is conditional and traceable to the six methods.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start with internal article analysis before assigning weight to any source.
2. Compare and contrast frameworks, limitations, assumptions, designs, samples, results, and conclusions.
3. Evaluate relevance against the specific situation, not only the broad topic.
4. Use synthesis for compatible evidence and integration for context-bound variation.
5. Preserve a visible logic link at every step of the constructivist process.
6. Use the Article Assessment Form, Article Analysis Form, and Critical Thinking Chart as adaptable working tools when a more systematic audit is needed.
