# Chapter 7: Brainstorming: Examples of Factors to Consider

## Core Idea

Brainstorming expands a research question into possible factors before the writer commits to an outline. The goal is not to include every variable; it is to expose the dimensions that might matter, then select a manageable set based on the question, purpose, available literature, and time.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Free Writing**: Generate possibilities without prematurely judging them.
  - When to use: Use at the start of planning, especially when the question feels abstract or the relevant factors are unclear.
  - How: Find a quiet setting, set aside a defined period of roughly 3 to 20 minutes, and write every thought connected to the question. Fragments and abbreviations are acceptable. Do not stop to organize, correct, or decide whether an idea is important. Review the output later and develop promising directions further.
- **Clustering**: Organize and extend the factors produced by brainstorming.
  - When to use: Use after free writing, or use it alone when visual relationships help reveal subtopics.
  - How: Put a short version of the main question in a central circle. Draw spokes for first-level ideas. Add further lines for the dimensions of each spoke. Continue while the expansion helps make the topic narrower and more workable. A time break between free writing and clustering can reveal additional relationships.
- **Factor Selection and Scope Control**: Move from possibility to a defensible review plan.
  - When to use: Apply after generating a broad field of variables.
  - How: Ask which factors matter to the specific population, location, situation, and purpose. Consider the review type and the amount of literature available. Select the factors that can produce an objective, useful answer; do not give every listed factor equal treatment.

## Key Concepts

- **Factor/variable**: A dimension, condition, characteristic, or influence that may matter to the topic.
- **Free writing**: Timed, unrestricted generation of ideas related to a question.
- **Clustering**: A visual arrangement of a central question, its spokes, and deeper layers of subtopics.
- **Central question**: The abbreviated main issue placed at the center of a cluster.
- **Spoke point**: A first-level factor extending from the central question.
- **Layer**: A further level of detail attached to a factor or subfactor.
- **Scope**: The practical boundaries of what the review will examine.
- **Objective review**: A review whose selected dimensions are broad enough to avoid one-sided conclusions but narrow enough for thorough treatment.

## Mental Models

- Use **divergence before convergence**: first create options, then judge and narrow them.
- Think of a cluster as a **map of possible routes**, not a causal model. A line between ideas indicates a question to investigate, not proof that one causes another.
- Use the **manageable strand rule**: continue narrowing until one person can examine the selected factors carefully within the available time and document.
- Treat a break between planning stages as a **fresh-eyes control**. Distance helps expose missing factors and reduce attachment to the first idea.

## Anti-patterns

- **Outlining before exploring**: Early headings can lock the review into the first interpretation of the problem.
- **Stopping at the first plausible factor**: A narrow list created without alternatives is likely to reproduce the writer's assumptions.
- **Treating every variable as equally important**: Exhaustive inclusion creates an unfinishable review and weakens the argument.
- **Brainstorming forever**: More layers are useful only while they help narrow the topic or clarify its dimensions.
- **Mistaking a cluster for evidence**: A visual association still requires literature and analysis.

## Worked Example

For the question, "What impact would adapting a Fortune 500 management training program have on teacher training?", free writing might produce ideas about students resembling unmotivated employees, teacher self-monitoring, age of students, differences in measures of success, and the work environment. Do not discard the rough fragments at this stage.

Next, cluster the ideas around several spokes: teacher and manager roles, student or employee motivation, classroom or organizational environment, age and population, management behaviors, and definitions of success. Add layers such as caregiver versus mentor, small classroom versus large organization, self-motivation versus supervision, and academic performance versus business performance. The review might ultimately select role similarities, management behaviors, and measures of effectiveness, while reserving unrelated age or industry variables for later work. The cluster has generated an objective scope without pretending that every possible factor will be studied.

## Key Takeaways

1. Use free writing to expose possibilities and clustering to make relationships visible.
2. Delay judgment until after the first round of idea generation.
3. Select factors by relevance, purpose, evidence availability, and workability.
4. Keep the distinction between a planning association and a tested relationship.
5. Narrow the cluster until it can support a thorough and objective review.
