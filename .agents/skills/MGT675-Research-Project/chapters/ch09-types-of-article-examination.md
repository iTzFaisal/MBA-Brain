# Chapter 9: Types of Article Examination Used in a Literature Review

## Core Idea

The literature review uses six related higher-order thinking skills, each with a distinct job: analysis, comparison, contrast, evaluation, synthesis, and integration. Reexamining an article through different lenses is not pointless repetition; it builds an increasingly defensible picture of what the literature means for the topic.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Six Higher-Order Thinking Skills**: Preserve the distinctions between the complementary methods.
  - **Analysis**: Examine an article's integrity, assumptions, structure, evidence, method, interpretation, and conclusions.
  - **Comparison**: Identify similarities across two or more articles, studies, or phenomena.
  - **Contrast**: Identify differences across them.
  - **Evaluation**: Judge the importance and applicability of the literature in relation to the specific topic, situation, program, or event under study.
  - **Synthesis**: Combine sound, compatible parts of different sources to create a more complete picture.
  - **Integration**: Explain how apparently incompatible findings can fit together by identifying relevant differences or conditions.
  - When to use: Use all six in a substantial review, but do not force each into every paragraph.
  - How: Let analysis establish whether an article has integrity and value. Use the valuable articles for comparisons and contrasts. Use those relationships to create syntheses and integrations. Evaluate individual sources and the combined framework against the specific topic.
- **Critical Thinking Chart**: Keep the six assessments connected.
  - When to use: Use while reading and revising, especially when several articles address the same factor.
  - How: Record each article's assumptions, design, sample, evidence, results, conclusions, strengths, weaknesses, similarities, differences, relevance, and possible framework contribution.

## Key Concepts

- **Analysis**: Examination of the integrity and internal soundness of an article.
- **Comparison**: Discovery of meaningful similarities.
- **Contrast**: Discovery of meaningful differences.
- **Evaluation**: Assessment of literature against an outside topic or situation.
- **Synthesis**: Weaving compatible findings into a larger picture.
- **Integration**: Reconciling apparently contradictory findings through their conditions and differences.
- **Integrity**: The degree to which an article's logic, method, evidence, and conclusions can be trusted.
- **Framework**: An organized explanation of how the literature's pieces relate to the topic.

## Mental Models

- Use six **lenses on the same evidence**: each lens asks a different question and should produce a different kind of statement.
- Think of analysis as the **value gate**. Do not build a framework from an article whose evidence or logic has not been examined.
- Treat comparison and contrast as a **paired diagnostic**: similarities show what may be stable; differences show what may be conditional.
- Think of synthesis and integration as **construction and reconciliation**. One combines compatible pieces; the other explains apparent conflict.

## Anti-patterns

- **Using analysis as a synonym for everything**: Vague language hides whether the writer checked integrity, similarity, difference, or applicability.
- **Collapsing comparison and contrast**: Readers lose the distinction between reinforcement and divergence.
- **Synthesizing before analyzing**: A neat framework can magnify a weak or misinterpreted article.
- **Evaluating only source quality**: An article can be rigorous yet poorly suited to the specific situation.
- **Treating the skills as independent**: The review then lacks the logic chain that turns articles into a framework.

## Worked Example

Suppose one article reports that managers' expectations affect employees and another reports that management style changes under stress. Analyze each article first for its method, evidence, limitations, and conclusion. If both are sound and address complementary aspects of management, compare their compatible elements and synthesize them into a broader picture of the management environment. If a third article reports that mentoring reduces stress during audits while another reports heightened stress under the same apparent condition, contrast them and integrate the difference by examining whether one organization had active mentoring. Finally, evaluate whether these findings are useful for the organization or management problem in the current review.

## Key Takeaways

1. Name the thinking operation being performed; precision improves the logic of the review.
2. Analyze integrity before using an article as a building block.
3. Compare similarities, contrast differences, and evaluate applicability separately.
4. Use synthesis for compatible evidence and integration for apparently incompatible evidence.
5. Keep the six operations linked in a Critical Thinking Chart and in the outline.
