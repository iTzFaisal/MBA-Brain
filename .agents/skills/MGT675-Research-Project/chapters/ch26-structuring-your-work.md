# Chapter 26: Structuring Your Work

## Core Idea
Structure is the visible form of the review's reasoning. The right design lets readers tell when evidence is being analyzed, compared, contrasted, evaluated against the focal situation, synthesized, or integrated, and it makes every transition and possible link explicit without pretending that a complex literature review has one simple causal chain.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Researcher-centered structure**: Use when the literature clusters naturally around theorists or researchers. Present each researcher's contribution and analysis before moving to comparisons, contrasts, and the culminating synthesis and integration.
- **Topic-centered structure**: Use when the literature clusters around dimensions of a topic, events, or situations. Combine article summaries, analyses, comparisons, and contrasts under each topic heading; place the overall synthesis and integration before the conclusions.
- **Cluster-style if-then diagram**: Use to map multiple possible relationships in a literature review. Draw direct if-then links, include alternative paths where no direct causal relationship can be claimed, and document every intermediate step.
- **Transition and logic-link check**: Use while revising structure. Make clear which articles are being compared or contrasted, when evaluation shifts to the specific question or circumstance, and how the capstone synthesis follows from the preceding work.

## Key Concepts
- **Structure**: The organization that carries the review's reasoning to the reader.
- **Researcher-centered review**: A design organized around authors, theorists, or researchers.
- **Topic-centered review**: A design organized around dimensions, events, groups, or situations.
- **Capstone**: The synthesis and integration placed near the end to show the overall picture before conclusions.
- **Logic link**: A stated connection between one idea or condition and the next.
- **Cluster-style if-then diagram**: A multi-path map suited to multiple possible causes and correlations.
- **Direct if-then link**: A relationship explicitly supported or considered in the literature.
- **Causal overclaim**: Treating a possible or correlational link as proof of causation.
- **Transition**: A sentence or phrase that signals a change in evidence, lens, topic, or organizational level.

## Mental Models
- **Structure is a navigation system.** Readers should always know where they are, what question the section answers, and why the next section follows.
- **Use topic organization when relationships matter more than authorship.** Keep source analysis close to the topic it explains, then synthesize across topics.
- **Map uncertainty rather than hiding it.** A cluster-style if-then diagram can display multiple plausible paths while preserving objectivity about causation.

## Anti-patterns
- Mixing researcher and topic organization without a reason the reader can follow.
- Hiding comparisons and evaluations in long article summaries so the shift in reasoning is invisible.
- Drawing a traditional single-chain causal diagram for a multi-cause literature review.
- Skipping intermediate links in an if-then argument because they seem obvious to the writer.
- Treating the placement of synthesis and integration as optional when readers need a capstone before conclusions.

## Worked Example
Suppose a review examines trauma related to family and no-family settings. A cluster-style if-then diagram can show one path from family trauma to possible abuse or neglect and another from external trauma to different forms of stress, while allowing multiple outcomes and intermediate conditions. The diagram does not prove that every trauma causes abuse, neglect, or a specific outcome; it records the relationships that the literature permits the writer to consider. The review then chooses topic-centered sections for family and external settings, states each article's evidence and limits within the relevant section, and brings the paths together in synthesis and integration before the conclusion.

## Key Takeaways
1. Choose researcher-centered or topic-centered structure based on how the evidence clusters.
2. Use topic-centered organization when it keeps related analysis and comparisons together.
3. Place synthesis and integration as a capstone before conclusions.
4. Use the cluster-style if-then diagram for multiple possible relationships, not as proof of causation.
5. State every intermediate link and use transitions to expose shifts in reasoning.
