# Chapter 21: Organizing Your Assessment of the Literature

## Core Idea
The outline is a living organism: research findings and newly identified syntheses may require a different order than the initial plan. Choose an organization that makes the question, evidence, relationships, and conclusions easiest for the reader to follow rather than defaulting to article-by-article order.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Organizational Plans for Literature Reviews (Table 21.1)**: Use **Table of Contents 1** - Introduction, Analysis of Literature, Comparison and Contrast, Evaluation Against My Special Topic, Synthesis and Integration of Literature, Conclusions - when separating higher order thinking tasks is the clearest basic structure. Use **Table of Contents 2** - Introduction, age/time periods, Synthesis and Integration, Conclusions - when chronology drives the question. Use **Table of Contents 3** - Introduction, Parents' Roles, Teachers' Roles, Community's Roles, Students' Roles, Synthesis and Integration, Conclusions - when roles or groups are the important comparison. Test each plan against the evidence before committing.
- **Organization by trends**: Use when the literature shows an evolving management, behavioral, response, or analysis trend. Arrange sources and relationships around the trend rather than around individual authors.
- **Organization by themes**: Use when recurring topic dimensions, groups, or system levels explain the question. Change themes if the research shows that the initial categories do not differentiate findings.
- **Organization by research methods**: Use when statistical analysis, interviews/questionnaires/observations, quasi-experiments, or experiments account for meaningful differences in results. Otherwise, nest method under a more useful group or topic structure.

## Key Concepts
- **Living outline**: A plan that changes as evidence, relationships, and syntheses become clearer.
- **Higher order thinking organization**: A sequence that separates analysis, comparison/contrast, evaluation, synthesis/integration, and conclusions.
- **Time-frame organization**: Arrangement by age, developmental period, historical period, or another meaningful timeline.
- **Group organization**: Arrangement by the people, roles, populations, or units being examined.
- **Trend**: A pattern of change identified across the literature.
- **Theme**: A recurring topic dimension that helps readers understand the evidence.
- **Method-based organization**: Arrangement by research design or analytic method when method explains variation.
- **Transition**: Wording that makes the movement between organizational units and logic steps visible.

## Mental Models
- **Let the evidence choose the shelf.** If findings cluster around theorists, topics, events, groups, or methods, use that natural clustering unless it obscures the question.
- **Optimize reader attention.** Organize so readers can understand one group or period fully before shifting to another, rather than making them repeatedly switch lenses.
- **Treat headings as promises.** A heading should tell the reader what evidence and reasoning belong there; transitions should show why the next unit follows.

## Anti-patterns
- Keeping the original outline unchanged after the research reveals a clearer structure.
- Presenting every article first, every comparison next, and every conclusion last when that arrangement hides the topic's important relationships.
- Choosing themes because they are available rather than because they differentiate results or answer the question.
- Making research method the primary structure when method differences do not explain the findings.
- Moving between groups, time periods, and authors without explicit transitions.

## Worked Example
Imagine a review of how family, school, community, and peer influences shape adolescent outcomes. A skill-based table of contents would place all analyses together, then all comparisons, but readers would repeatedly shift among the four groups. A group-based plan instead gives each group its own section, with the relevant analysis, comparisons, and evaluations nested inside it, followed by a synthesis across groups. If the question is developmental, a time-frame plan may be clearer: early adolescence, middle adolescence, late adolescence, then adulthood. If results differ mainly by method, each group can contain statistical, interview/observation, and experimental subsections. The final choice follows the question and the evidence, not a universal template.

## Key Takeaways
1. Revisit the outline after analysis, comparison, evaluation, synthesis, and integration are visible.
2. Use higher-order-skill order as a sound basic plan, but change it when time, groups, themes, trends, or methods better explain the question.
3. Organize by the dimension that helps readers understand relationships without unnecessary attention shifts.
4. Change themes when they do not distinguish findings or contribute to the argument.
5. Use headings and transitions to expose the logic of the review.
