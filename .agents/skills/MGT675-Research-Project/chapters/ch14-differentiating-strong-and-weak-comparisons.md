# Chapter 14: Differentiating Between Strong and Weak Comparisons and Contrasts

## Core Idea

A comparison or contrast is strong when the relationship between the sources is direct and clearly relevant. It is weak when the writer must infer a connection across mismatched topics, populations, conditions, or outcomes. Weak relationships can misrepresent authors' evidence and conclusions, so narrow the focus and state limitations rather than treating every plausible link as equal.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Strong and Weak Comparison-Contrast Test**: Judge the relationship before writing it as evidence.
  - When to use: Apply whenever two sources share only a broad topic or when a result seems useful by analogy.
  - How: Ask whether the circumstances are similar, including environment, procedures, participant characteristics, evaluation method, and timing. Ask whether the purposes and goals are relevant and similarly perceived. Look for differences in organizational mindset, phases, patterns, and other variables. Direct matching relationships are strong; relationships requiring a partial inferred connection are weak.
- **Comparison and Contrast Chart**: Make direct and inferential relationships visible.
  - When to use: Use while sorting multiple articles on one topic.
  - How: Place articles with directly similar conclusions in a comparison column and directly opposing conclusions in a contrast column. Mark relationships that require inference as faulty or weak unless the review can explain and bound the inference. Keep synthesis separate from the initial comparison decision.
- **Narrow-Strand Rule**: Reduce the number of differences considered at one time.
  - When to use: Use when many variables make it impossible to assign relative importance.
  - How: Identify the smallest meaningful strand of similarity or difference. Remove weak paragraphs or label their weakness. A narrower relationship permits more careful analysis of why it matters.

## Key Concepts

- **Direct association**: A relationship supported by closely matching topics, conditions, outcomes, or groups.
- **Inference**: A connection the writer must supply because the sources do not match directly.
- **Strong comparison**: A similarity whose relevance and evidentiary relationship are clear.
- **Weak comparison**: A similarity dependent on assumptions or partial connections.
- **Strong contrast**: A difference between sufficiently comparable sources.
- **Relevance**: The degree to which the relationship informs the current question.
- **Evaluation method**: The way outcomes or claims were assessed in a source.
- **Narrow strand**: A focused variable or difference that can be interpreted without overwhelming confounds.

## Mental Models

- Treat an inferred connection as carrying **relationship debt**: the more explanation you must add, the less directly the source supports the comparison.
- Think of **more differences as weaker similarity certainty**. A contrast may benefit from differences, but too many differences make it unclear what produced the divergence.
- Use comparability as a **precondition for interpretation**, not an editorial preference.
- Keep the question "What would have to be true for this link to hold?" visible whenever a source comes from an adjacent topic.

## Anti-patterns

- **Comparing anything that mentions the same theme**: Shared vocabulary can conceal incompatible populations or outcomes.
- **Quietly filling gaps with assumptions**: The writer may misrepresent what an author actually found.
- **Calling a weak link strong**: Readers cannot judge the reliability of an inference that is not disclosed.
- **Using too many differences at once**: The review loses the ability to identify which variable matters.
- **Confusing synthesis with comparison**: Two partial pictures may be useful together without being direct evidence of similarity.

## Worked Example

The globalization example sorts seven article summaries. Articles 1 and 7 form a strong comparison: both concern jobs being exported, with one specifying factory and high-technology work and the other emphasizing the loss of good-paying jobs affecting financially constrained senior citizens. Articles 2 and 5 form a strong contrast because one says high-level jobs will remain and service jobs will sustain workers, while the other says overseas job loss has reduced national self-sufficiency.

Articles 2 and 6 create a weak comparison: both suggest that some U.S. jobs will remain, but one refers to high-level jobs and the other to hardworking people in jobs at any level. Articles 5 and 6 create a weak contrast for similar reasons. Article 4 and Article 6 may help create a synthesis about education and globalization, but that does not make them a direct comparison. Labeling these distinctions prevents the writer from turning loose thematic proximity into evidence.

## Key Takeaways

1. Test directness and relevance before presenting a comparison or contrast.
2. Inspect environment, procedure, participants, evaluation, purpose, and time frame.
3. Disclose inference and weaken the claim when the relationship is indirect.
4. Narrow the relationship to one interpretable strand.
5. Keep synthesis available for partial pictures that should not be labeled direct comparisons.
