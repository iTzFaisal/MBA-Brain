# Chapter 3: Objective Research

## Core Idea

Objective research examines a circumstance through multiple relevant parameters instead of allowing a preferred answer to determine which evidence is noticed. A literature review becomes objective through a structured process: narrow the question, identify the factors that may affect it, learn what research has found, and deliberately look for confirmations, denials, competing explanations, and modifications before drawing a conclusion.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Objective Research**: Build a literature review around an accurate account of what is known rather than a summary that merely repeats sources or an argument that defends one side.
  - When to use: Use for academic, applied, and simple reviews whenever the result will inform further research, a program, a proposal, or a decision.
  - How: Consider the relevant parameters of the circumstance, inspect what researchers have concluded about them, and evaluate the quality and balance of those conclusions. Apply the same objectivity requirement even when the review uses sources other than peer-reviewed articles.
- **Parameters of a Circumstance**: Define the factors that can explain a topic's characteristics, development, maintenance, conflict, or change.
  - When to use: Use at the beginning of a review, before searching becomes a collection of disconnected articles.
  - How: Identify an initial set of factors, then let early reading reveal which have actually been studied and which additional factors or perspectives need examination. Treat the list as a starting boundary, not as proof that the factors cause the outcome.
- **Structured Process**: Use an explicit sequence to make objectivity observable.
  - When to use: Use from question formation through the final analysis of the selected literature.
  - How: (1) identify and narrow the topic; (2) identify initial parameters or factors; (3) search for articles; (4) create an outline after enough reading to understand the research and results; (5) search for pros and cons, confirmations and denials, and modifications of the initial findings; and (6) analyze, compare, contrast, evaluate, synthesize, and integrate the selected articles in relation to the question and circumstances.

## Key Concepts

- **Objectivity**: Deliberate consideration of relevant parameters and perspectives without letting personal interest control selection or interpretation.
- **Bias**: A perspective, interest, omission, or imbalance that distorts the evidence or conclusion.
- **Parameter**: A factor or dimension that helps describe or explain the circumstance under review.
- **Body of research**: The existing studies, theories, methods, data, and conclusions that a review examines.
- **Peer-reviewed article**: A source assessed by field reviewers and used as a major foundation for academic objectivity.
- **Initial parameter**: A factor selected before the literature search and then tested against what the research actually addresses.
- **Confirmation and denial**: Evidence that supports or challenges an early finding or assumption.
- **Modification**: A qualification or changed interpretation revealed by later research.
- **Research argument**: The evidence-based explanation a literature review develops, rather than a sequence of summaries.

## Mental Models

- Treat objectivity as a **process you can audit**, not as a personality trait or claim of perfect neutrality.
- Use initial factors as **search hypotheses**, not commitments. The literature is allowed to remove, add, or reshape them.
- Think of the outline as a **pivot point**: it records what the first search taught you and directs the second search toward counterevidence.
- Use the six operations as a **reasoning chain**. Later conclusions should inherit the limits found during earlier examination.

## Anti-patterns

- **Searching only for confirmation**: The review reproduces the writer's first impression instead of testing it.
- **Treating the first factor list as complete**: Unexamined parameters can produce a one-sided explanation.
- **Writing a source-by-source summary**: The reader cannot see the argument, conflicts, or implications for the question.
- **Calling a source objective because of its format**: Peer review or professional status does not eliminate the need to inspect evidence and bias.
- **Skipping the second search**: Without pros, cons, denials, and modifications, the review cannot demonstrate a counterweight to initial findings.

## Worked Example

Imagine a researcher notices that women hold few leadership positions in a small woman-owned business and asks whether this pattern is common. The researcher narrows the question, identifies possible factors such as ownership structure, communication, organizational size, and leadership pathways, and searches for relevant studies. After the initial outline, the researcher deliberately seeks evidence that confirms, disputes, or qualifies the first pattern. The final review can then distinguish a recurring finding from a local observation and show which factors remain uncertain.

## Key Takeaways

1. Make objectivity visible through the choices and sequence of the research process.
2. Start with relevant parameters, but let the literature revise the initial factor list.
3. Use peer-reviewed research as a foundation while evaluating every source for balance and quality.
4. Search deliberately for competing findings and modifications after the first evidence appears.
5. Connect analysis, comparison, contrast, evaluation, synthesis, and integration back to the specific question.
