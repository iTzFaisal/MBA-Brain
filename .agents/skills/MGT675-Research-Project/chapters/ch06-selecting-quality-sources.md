# Chapter 6: Selecting Quality Sources for Your Review

## Core Idea

Source quality depends on the kind of review, the question being asked, the source's direct relevance, and the information it can actually support. Peer review is a strong quality signal, but a highly reputable article that does not address the question is still a poor source for that particular argument.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Source Quality by Review Type**: Scale rigor to purpose without abandoning objectivity.
  - When to use: Use at the beginning of source collection and whenever a source mix starts to drift.
  - How: For an academic review, make peer-reviewed or refereed journal articles the core. For an applied review, prioritize peer-reviewed work but add statutes, legal precedents, government or military reports, and local sources when the situation requires them. For a simple review, popular sources may form a larger share, but label their type and assess them carefully. In every case, weigh peer-reviewed work more heavily when drawing research conclusions.
- **Abstract-Based Relevance Triage**: Screen a large search result set before committing to full reading.
  - When to use: Apply when a database search returns too many articles or when the topic is still being refined.
  - How: Read abstracts for the population, factors, perspective, method, and results. Put directly relevant articles on the reading list. Set loosely related articles aside in a separate file for later or follow-on work. Use related-topic research through **mining the data**, but do not invent a speculative chain of connections.
- **Timeliness of Articles**: Decide what counts as current in context.
  - When to use: Apply when the review claims to describe the current state of knowledge or when a field changes quickly.
  - How: Consider the expectations of the audience, the amount of recent literature, the age of foundational work, and whether older sources are needed to explain the framework. If direct current research is scarce, use older or related research openly and explain its role.
- **Article Analysis Form and Critical Thinking Chart**: Externalize source decisions.
  - When to use: Use from the first abstract review through drafting so judgments are not reconstructed from memory.
  - How: Record the article's topic, method, evidence, conclusions, relevance, strengths, weaknesses, comparisons, contrasts, and possible evaluation points in one organized place.

## Key Concepts

- **Peer-reviewed/refereed journal**: A journal that subjects an article to field review, commonly using reviewers who judge the work without relying on the author's name.
- **Blind review**: Evaluation of an article without the reviewers being given the author's identity.
- **Research-based source**: Literature built on research, analysis, or a documented review rather than unsupported opinion.
- **Local source**: Material tied to the event, organization, population, or environment under examination.
- **Relevance**: The degree to which a source directly informs the question and factors in the review.
- **Timeliness**: The appropriateness of a source's age for the question and audience.
- **Abstract screening**: Using article summaries to decide which sources deserve full reading.
- **Mining the data**: Deliberately seeking evidence from a related topic when direct evidence is limited.

## Mental Models

- Think of source value as a **fit problem**, not a prestige contest: rigor, direct relevance, context, and timeliness must work together.
- Use peer review as a **quality floor**, not a guarantee that the article answers your question or supports your inference.
- Treat the abstract as a **triage filter**, not a substitute for reading the method, evidence, limitations, and conclusion.
- Keep a **parking lot for peripheral sources**. Preserving a source without forcing it into the current review protects focus and future usefulness.

## Anti-patterns

- **Using an expert book because of the author's reputation**: Books may be insightful but do not receive the same blind peer review as journal articles.
- **Equating relevance with a shared keyword**: A source can mention the topic while offering no evidence about the factor being examined.
- **Using only current sources**: Removing older foundational work can leave the review unable to explain how the topic developed.
- **Filling a thin evidence base with speculation**: Related research is useful only when the relationship can be explained and bounded.
- **Treating local opinion as objective fact**: Local reports can reveal reactions and details while still reflecting interests or incomplete information.

## Worked Example

For a review of whether volunteers or paid professionals are more effective at teaching HIV prevention to high school students, imagine three articles. One finds that students respond better to instructors near their own age; it directly answers who may be accepted as an instructor. A second finds that volunteers prefer attentive students over disruptive groups; it is relevant because provider behavior could influence the apparent effectiveness of volunteers, but it raises a question about whether paid professionals behave similarly. A third finds that students attend extracurricular sessions less often than in-school sessions; it is only weakly related unless the review explicitly studies delivery location.

Use the first article, examine the second conditionally, and park the third rather than padding the review. If direct evidence is scarce, mine studies of other volunteer-led student programs, but state that the evidence informs participation or instructor response rather than proving HIV-prevention effectiveness.

## Key Takeaways

1. Let purpose determine the acceptable source mix, but never let purpose excuse biased interpretation.
2. Screen abstracts for direct relevance before spending time on full articles.
3. Use peer-reviewed sources as the evidentiary center of academic work.
4. Keep peripheral sources available without allowing them to weaken the current question.
5. Judge timeliness in relation to the audience, field, and role of the source.
