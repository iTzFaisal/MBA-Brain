# Chapter 8: Creating an Initial Outline for Your Literature Review

## Core Idea

An outline is a living document that turns a question, selected factors, and early source knowledge into a navigable plan. It should change as the literature changes the writer's understanding. Its value is not merely formatting; it exposes missing logic, unsupported sections, and the place where each source will contribute.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Living Outline**: Treat the plan as an evolving research instrument.
  - When to use: Start after preliminary reading, abstract review, free writing, or clustering, and revise throughout research.
  - How: Begin with the question and why it matters. Add major headers for the main dimensions of the review, subheaders for factors and relationships, and lower levels for evidence, quotations, paraphrases, or unresolved questions. Add, remove, and reorder sections as the literature reveals a better logic.
- **Hierarchical Outline with APA-Style Levels**: Match the outline's levels to the depth of the argument.
  - When to use: Use when a review contains several factors, populations, or comparisons that readers must follow.
  - How: Use a main header for a major section, a subheader for its first subdivision, a further level for a narrower point, and paragraph-level details for individual evidence. APA is the book's example, not a universal requirement; use the style required by the audience.
- **Source-Marked Planning**: Preserve attribution while ideas are fresh.
  - When to use: Apply while reading and populating the outline.
  - How: Record authors and dates beside claims, place quotation marks around exact wording immediately, and mark your own observations separately. If a potentially relevant point has no source, label it as an unanswered question or future research need rather than silently treating it as established.

## Key Concepts

- **Living document**: A plan deliberately revised as new information changes the review.
- **Main header**: A first-level division corresponding to a major point or factor.
- **Subheader**: A lower-level division that develops a main header.
- **Preliminary outline**: An early map formed from brainstorming and initial source review.
- **Topic question**: The central question that anchors the outline.
- **Source slot**: A location in the outline where a particular article, evidence, or perspective will be used.
- **Research gap**: A relevant point for which adequate research has not yet been found.
- **Topic sentence**: A sentence that states a paragraph's main point and guides its transition.

## Mental Models

- Use the outline as a **coverage test**: if a factor cannot be placed clearly, the question or factor may be underdeveloped.
- Think of headers as **navigation for the reader**, not labels added after writing. A reader should see why one section follows another.
- Treat a source-marked outline as a **provenance system**. It prevents later confusion about whether a claim came from an author, a paraphrase, or your own inference.
- Regard missing research as **information about the field**. A relevant point with no evidence can become a bounded limitation or future research question.

## Anti-patterns

- **Writing without a plan**: The review becomes a sequence of remembered articles rather than an answer to a question.
- **Freezing the first outline**: Early assumptions then control the review even when the literature points elsewhere.
- **Using headings without hierarchy**: Equal-looking sections conceal which ideas are central and which are supporting details.
- **Leaving quotations unmarked**: Later paraphrasing can accidentally become plagiarism or lose the source's exact meaning.
- **Inventing support for an empty section**: A missing source should not be replaced by an unsupported assertion.

## Worked Example

For the teacher-management question, an initial outline can be organized as follows:

1. Introduction: topic question and why the question matters.
2. Comparison of roles: the nature of teaching, the nature of management, similarities, and differences.
3. Training: current teacher-management training and Fortune 500 manager training.
4. Effectiveness: how each setting defines and measures successful management.
5. Synthesis and integration: what the combined literature suggests for adapting training.

Under "Comparison of roles," place source notes such as a study describing the teacher as caregiver, another describing the manager as mentor, and an observation that both manage an environment and model behavior. If no research addresses office-school differences, mark that point as an evidence gap or future research issue. As articles are read, add limitations, alternate perspectives, and possible comparisons rather than simply adding more citations to fixed headings.

## Key Takeaways

1. Build the outline after enough preliminary reading to know the field's main dimensions.
2. Revise it whenever evidence changes the most logical organization.
3. Use hierarchical levels to show the relationship between questions, factors, evidence, and implications.
4. Mark sources and quotations immediately to protect attribution and streamline drafting.
5. Use empty or weak sections as prompts to narrow, research, or remove a point.
