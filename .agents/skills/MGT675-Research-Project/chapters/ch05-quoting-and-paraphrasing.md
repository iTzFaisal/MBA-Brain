# Chapter 5: Quoting and Paraphrasing

## Core Idea

Quoting and paraphrasing are ways to use source information while keeping the reviewer's understanding and ownership visible. A literature review should normally paraphrase because the writer must select what matters to the question and explain it in a coherent voice. A quotation is appropriate only when the author's exact wording is unusually precise or valuable. Both forms require accurate attribution, and neither permits the writer to change the source's meaning.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Quoting**: Reserve exact source language for wording that cannot be improved without losing its value or precision.
  - When to use: Use sparingly when the formulation itself is important, not merely because the source expresses a point clearly.
  - How: Reproduce the exact words, mark them as a quotation, and apply the required citation style. Include the author, publication date, and page or paragraph locator when the style requires it. Remove irrelevant material rather than importing it into the argument.
- **Paraphrasing**: Re-express relevant source information in the writer's own language after understanding it in the context of the review question.
  - When to use: Use for most source findings and explanations in a literature review.
  - How: Read the passage and identify the part relevant to the question; restate the meaning fully in new wording; compare the paraphrase with the source; preserve qualifiers, population, degree of support, and limitations; and cite the author actually read.
- **Quoting and Paraphrasing**: Choose the form that preserves meaning while keeping the review's logic readable and attributable.
  - When to use: Decide during note-taking and again during drafting whenever source language enters the review.
  - How: Ask whether the exact words are essential and whether they contain unnecessary noise. If not, paraphrase and keep a clear boundary between the source's claim and the reviewer's analysis.

## Key Concepts

- **Quotation**: The author's exact words, marked and cited as required by the applicable style.
- **Paraphrase**: A faithful restatement in the reviewer's own language that still requires a citation.
- **Source meaning**: The claim, scope, qualifiers, and degree of certainty that must remain intact.
- **Relevant information**: The portion of a source selected because it answers the review's particular question.
- **Citation boundary**: The point where responsibility for a statement moves from the source to the reviewer.
- **Author actually read**: The source writer to cite when information was obtained directly or through another author's paraphrase.
- **Quotation noise**: Extra wording carried over from a source that distracts from the review's specific point.
- **Attribution**: Language and citation that identify whose idea, finding, or wording is being used.

## Mental Models

- Treat paraphrasing as an **understanding test**. If the point cannot be explained independently, it may not yet be understood.
- Think of quotations as **precision tools**, not evidence substitutes. Their value must come from the wording itself.
- Use a paraphrase as a **controlled translation**: change the language, preserve the claim, scope, qualifiers, and uncertainty.
- Treat citations as **ownership boundaries**. The reader should know where the author's evidence ends and the reviewer's interpretation begins.

## Anti-patterns

- **Patchwriting**: Rearranging a few source words while retaining the original structure and calling it a paraphrase.
- **Quotation overload**: Letting copied language replace the reviewer's analysis and make the paragraph noisy or awkward.
- **Meaning inflation**: Removing qualifiers, changing "some" to "all," or adding an implication the source did not establish.
- **Citing an unread original**: Naming an author found in a secondary source as if that original work had been examined.
- **Uncited paraphrase**: Treating new wording as common knowledge and omitting credit because no exact words were copied.

## Worked Example

Suppose several studies report that rural and urban students described college as a chance to experience new challenges, while another study found that some urban low-income students associated college with escaping poverty. A review about motivations for higher education can paraphrase only the motivation relevant to its question, preserve the population attached to each finding, and cite each source read. It should not turn a reported perception into a proven cause of college success, combine the groups into one universal claim, or borrow a sentence's wording merely by changing a few verbs.

## Key Takeaways

1. Prefer paraphrase when the review needs the idea rather than the author's exact wording.
2. Quote only when the wording is uniquely precise, valuable, or necessary.
3. Read source material in the context of the question before selecting what to use.
4. Preserve meaning, qualifiers, population, limitations, and degree of support.
5. Cite the author actually read and separate source claims from the reviewer's interpretation.
