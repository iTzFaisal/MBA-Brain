# Chapter 1: Introduction

## Core Idea

The introduction presents the book as a practical, visual guide to producing strong literature reviews. It focuses on the writing process rather than a discipline, research philosophy, or topic-specific theory, and it follows the work from planning and source gathering through analysis and a finished review. The central promise is that objective information, clear logic, examples, checklists, and organizing tools help a writer build a review that can support academic work, a dissertation, a business proposal, a grant, or a professional decision.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Practicalities of the Writing Process**: Treat literature-review writing as a sequence of usable activities rather than as an abstract theory.
  - When to use: Use from the first question and planning decisions through the final written product.
  - How: Move deliberately from planning to research, organization, analysis, writing, and review. Use the book's examples and questions to make each step visible.
- **Objective Literature Review**: Build the review on accurate, objective information instead of speculation or a preferred perspective.
  - When to use: Use whenever the review will support a study, proposal, program, or decision.
  - How: Prefer appropriate research-based sources, examine what is known, and keep the review's conclusions tied to the evidence and its limits.
- **Visual Review-Building Tools**: Externalize the work with lists, forms, checklists, examples, and organized source notes.
  - When to use: Use while planning, collecting sources, analyzing articles, and drafting.
  - How: Record important source information and decisions so the writer can develop the argument without relying on memory or losing the logic of the review.

## Key Concepts

- **Literature review**: A structured written examination of knowledge available on a defined topic.
- **Writing process**: The connected planning, research, organization, analysis, drafting, and revision activities that produce the review.
- **Objective information**: Evidence selected and interpreted without allowing personal interests or an agenda to control the result.
- **Research-based source**: Information grounded in research, documented analysis, or an appropriate scholarly review.
- **Consumer**: The reader, committee, client, supervisor, or organization whose needs shape the review's acceptable form and style.
- **Review tools**: Forms, checklists, questions, examples, and source records that make the work easier to manage.

## Mental Models

- Treat the review as a **route from reality to action**: accurate knowledge comes before a defensible plan or conclusion.
- Think of the book as a **workbench**, not a theory survey. Each checklist or form should reduce a concrete writing or reasoning problem.
- Use **visible process over hidden effort**. If the source decisions and reasoning are recorded, gaps and unsupported assumptions are easier to find.
- Match the review to its **consumer**. The content is the priority, but the required presentation style depends on who will use it.

## Anti-patterns

- **Starting with a preferred conclusion**: The review becomes a search for support rather than an examination of what is known.
- **Treating a literature review as a summary**: Listing what authors said does not show how the information answers the question.
- **Relying on memory**: Unrecorded source decisions, quotations, and relationships are easily confused during drafting.
- **Using the wrong level of guidance**: A style manual cannot replace a sound evidence and reasoning process.
- **Ignoring the consumer**: A review can contain useful information and still fail if its scope, depth, or format does not serve its reader.

## Worked Example

Suppose an MBA student is preparing a review to support a proposal about management training. The student first defines the practical question, identifies the intended decision maker, and determines what evidence is needed. During research, the student records source claims, methods, limitations, and useful relationships instead of collecting disconnected quotations. The final review can then show what the literature establishes, where it is uncertain, and how the evidence should shape the proposal.

## Key Takeaways

1. Use the introduction's practical orientation to connect literature-review work to a real study, proposal, or decision.
2. Treat objective, research-based information as the foundation of the review.
3. Follow a visible process from planning through the finished product.
4. Use forms, checklists, examples, and organized notes to protect the logic of the work.
5. Adapt presentation style to the review's consumer without weakening the evidence standard.
