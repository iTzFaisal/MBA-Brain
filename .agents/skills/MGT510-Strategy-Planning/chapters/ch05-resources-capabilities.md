# Chapter 5: Analyzing Resources and Capabilities

## Core Idea
The resource-based view treats the firm as a collection of resources and capabilities. Resources are productive assets; capabilities are coordinated things the firm can do. Advantage arises when capabilities match industry key success factors and are difficult to transfer, replicate, or appropriate.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Resource-capability-advantage chain**: Trace tangible, intangible, and human resources into organizational capabilities, strategy, advantage, and profit. Individual assets rarely create advantage alone.
- **Capability identification**: Combine functional analysis, Porter's value chain, and historical analysis of repeated successes and failures. Look for routines, processes, and cross-functional integration.
- **Strategic importance tests**:
  - **Relevance**: Does the resource contribute to customer value and KSFs?
  - **Scarcity**: Is it uncommon among rivals, rather than merely necessary to play?
  - **Durability**: How quickly will its value erode?
  - **Transferability**: Can rivals buy or acquire it?
  - **Replicability**: Can rivals build or copy it?
  - **Appropriability**: Who captures the returns - the firm, employees, founders, suppliers, or partners?
- **Relative strength and strategy-implication matrix**: Separate strategic importance from relative strength. Exploit key strengths, repair or avoid key weaknesses, redeploy superfluous strengths, and do not overinvest in irrelevant gaps.
- **Benchmarking**: Compare functional processes and performance with rivals, but supplement numerical comparisons with historical and reflective analysis of idiosyncratic capabilities.

## Key Concepts
- **Resource-based view**: Strategy begins with the firm's distinctive resource and capability base.
- **Organizational capability**: Coordinated capacity to deploy resources for a desired result.
- **Core competence**: An integrated, higher-level capability fundamental to strategy and performance.
- **Resource complementarity**: Advantage from mutually reinforcing resources rather than isolated assets.
- **Ricardian rent**: Return from a scarce or superior resource or capability.
- **Capability hierarchy**: Routines and processes combine into increasingly integrated capabilities.
- **Dynamic capability**: The ability to modify and reconfigure operating capabilities.
- **Appropriability**: The firm's ability to capture returns generated by an advantage.

## Mental Models
- **Three gates to profit**: Establish advantage, sustain advantage, and appropriate the resulting returns.
- **Capability hierarchy**: Resources become routines, routines become processes, and processes become integrated capabilities.
- **Importance x strength**: Prioritize what matters strategically and where the firm is relatively strong or weak.
- **David versus Goliath**: Exploit distinctive strengths and avoid competing directly on a rival's strengths.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating the balance sheet as a complete resource inventory.
- Assuming a valuable individual asset automatically constitutes a capability.
- Calling a capability core without tracing the routines and complementary resources behind it.
- Relying on benchmarking alone to identify distinctive capabilities.
- Mistaking past success, luck, or managerial confidence for capability.
- Trying to fix every weakness internally when outsourcing or strategic avoidance is better.
- Discarding a superfluous strength without testing whether a contrarian strategy could make it valuable.

## Worked Example
Icelandair's location and Reykjavik stopover offer a relatively immobile resource, while its service, operational efficiency, and hands-on management are stronger relative capabilities. Its small domestic market and route constraints are weaknesses. A coherent strategy exploits the location and tourism appeal, uses efficiency for price competition, differentiates through service and the stopover, and uses partners to overcome the limits of the domestic market.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start with an honest inventory of what the firm owns and can reliably do.
2. Test relevance and scarcity before calling a resource a source of advantage.
3. Test durability, transferability, and replicability before calling advantage sustainable.
4. Examine who captures the returns, especially when capability depends on mobile people.
5. Connect internal analysis to industry KSFs and actual strategic choices.
