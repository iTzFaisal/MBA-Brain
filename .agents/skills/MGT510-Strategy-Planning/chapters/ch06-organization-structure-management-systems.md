# Chapter 6: Organization Structure and Management Systems

## Core Idea
Strategy formulation and implementation are interdependent. Implementation converts specialized effort into organizational capability through processes, motivation, structure, and management systems. A strategy that cannot be implemented was incompletely formulated.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Formulation-implementation relationship**: Test every strategic choice against people, resources, processes, structure, incentives, and learning capacity. Use implementation feedback to adapt strategy.
- **Strategic planning cycle**: Establish context and assumptions; develop unit plans; integrate a corporate plan; allocate capital; translate choices into operating plans, owners, KPIs, and budgets; review variances and feed learning into the next cycle.
- **Specialization, cooperation, and coordination**: Division of labor raises productivity but creates cooperation problems (aligning goals) and coordination problems (harmonizing actions).
- **Cooperation mechanisms**: Use hierarchy, incentives, shared values, persuasion, and leadership according to measurability, uncertainty, and team dependence.
- **Coordination mechanisms**: Use rules for standardized work, routines for repeated interdependence, and mutual adjustment for novel, ambiguous work.
- **Organizational capability model**: Integrate resources, processes, motivation, and structure; place tightly interdependent activities together or create explicit boundary-spanning mechanisms.
- **Organization forms**: Use functional structures for one-business scale and learning; multidivisional structures for multiple businesses; matrix structures when several dimensions are deeply interdependent; project, network, or adhocratic forms for creative and boundary-spanning work.
- **Mechanistic versus organic contingency**: Match formal rules, vertical control, and specialization to stable environments; match horizontal communication, autonomy, and mutual adjustment to dynamic environments.

## Key Concepts
- **Agency problem**: Individuals or units pursue personal or departmental interests over organizational goals.
- **Organizational process**: A planned, coordinated sequence of actions for a productive task.
- **Modularity**: Dividing a complex organization into partly independent units.
- **Loose coupling**: Autonomous units connected through standardized interfaces.
- **Organizational culture**: Shared beliefs and norms shaping cooperation and initiative.
- **Contingency theory**: There is no universally best structure; fit depends on strategy, technology, people, and environment.

## Mental Models
- **Intention-action-learning loop**: Strategy guides action; action generates learning; learning modifies strategy.
- **Specialization tax**: Each division-of-labor gain creates additional integration requirements.
- **Capability multiplier**: Resources become capability only when processes, motivation, and structure integrate them.
- **Coordination-fit rule**: Match rules, routines, or mutual adjustment to task standardization and interdependence.

## Anti-patterns
- Blaming poor execution when the strategy ignored implementation constraints.
- Producing a plan without budgets, owners, operating actions, targets, or review.
- Optimizing departments while neglecting end-to-end processes.
- Using individual incentives for outcomes produced jointly by teams.
- Applying rigid rules to ambiguous innovation work or informal coordination to safety-critical routine work.
- Expecting culture to change rapidly by executive decree.
- Building a formal matrix with overlapping authority and weak accountability.

## Worked Example
A medical-device company launching connected home monitoring uses a cross-functional project team while retaining functional departments for specialist capability. Mutual adjustment governs novel design problems, routines govern testing and handoffs, and rules govern regulatory compliance. Team rewards and a shared launch objective support cooperation. The innovation team operates organically while manufacturing and compliance remain mechanistic. Review feedback may require revising strategy rather than merely blaming execution.

## Key Takeaways
1. Implementation is part of strategy-making.
2. Planning systems connect intent to commitment, resources, action, and review.
3. Specialization creates value only when cooperation and coordination are solved.
4. Structure should follow interdependence and process requirements.
5. Organizational form and management style must fit the environment and task.
