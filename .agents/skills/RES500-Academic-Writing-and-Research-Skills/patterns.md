# Research Patterns

## Question Hierarchy
**When to use**: A manager presents a symptom, broad request, or preferred method.
**How**: Translate dilemma -> management question -> research question -> investigative questions -> measurement questions; reject measurements with no upward link.
**Trade-offs**: Clarifies scope and decision value, but exploration may revise the original question.

## Value-of-Information Check
**When to use**: Before approving new research or adding requested variables.
**How**: List action options, decision variables, an unbiased decision rule, expected result without research, expected result with research, and research plus opportunity cost.
**Trade-offs**: Prevents low-value studies, but estimates are uncertain; evaluate after the decision when necessary.

## Source Ladder and Exploration
**When to use**: The dilemma is poorly understood.
**How**: Combine primary records and knowledgeable people with evaluated secondary sources and tertiary search aids; compare findings across sources.
**Trade-offs**: Low-cost uncertainty reduction, but internal sources can carry political or historical bias.

## Design-Dimensions Pass
**When to use**: Several methods could answer the question.
**How**: Classify objective, manipulation, scope, measurement emphasis, complexity, collection method, environment, time, and participant awareness before selecting a design.
**Trade-offs**: Makes assumptions visible, but a multi-method design costs more and needs tighter coordination.

## Frame-to-Inference Sampling
**When to use**: A result may be generalized beyond observed cases.
**How**: Define target population and case, parameters, frame, count, selection method, and recruitment; audit coverage before calculating n.
**Trade-offs**: Probability methods support precision, while nonprobability methods improve access or exploratory depth but limit inference.

## Stratify, Cluster, or Sample Systematically
**When to use**: Simple random sampling is inefficient or costly.
**How**: Stratify for subgroup precision, cluster for geographic/access economy, or use a random start plus skip interval for an ordered frame; inspect periodicity.
**Trade-offs**: Efficiency in one dimension can reduce statistical efficiency or introduce design effects in another.

## Qualitative Saturation and Triangulation
**When to use**: Meaning, motivation, process, or hidden experience matters.
**How**: Recruit relevant and contrasting cases, probe with interviews/groups/pretasks, code and debrief during collection, stop at saturation, and compare methods or cases.
**Trade-offs**: Produces depth and discovery, not automatic population estimates; groups can suppress sensitive views.

## Observation Facts-First Protocol
**When to use**: Actual behavior or context is more reliable than recall.
**How**: Define event or time sampling, operationalize mutually exclusive/exhaustive codes, record facts separately from inferences, train observers, and monitor drift.
**Trade-offs**: Preserves natural behavior, but cannot directly reveal private motives and may create reactivity or privacy risk.

## Causal-Claim Checklist
**When to use**: Considering an experimental or causal conclusion.
**How**: Verify covariation, temporal precedence, and elimination of alternative causes; control the environment, assign or match cases, pilot, and assess internal and external validity.
**Trade-offs**: Stronger control supports causal inference but can reduce naturalness and generalizability.

## Three-Phase Instrument Build
**When to use**: Questions exist but the instrument is not field-ready.
**How**: Refine measurement questions; add introduction, transitions, instructions, screens, filters, and conclusion; order and physically design the instrument; test every branch and mode.
**Trade-offs**: Pretesting costs time, but untested wording, order, and layout create measurement and nonresponse error.

## Clean-File Pipeline
**When to use**: Responses have been collected.
**How**: Preserve CaseID, apply a codebook, post-code open text, edit for completeness/accuracy/coding, diagnose MCAR/MAR/NMAR, inspect visually, then cross-tabulate.
**Trade-offs**: Transparent correction is slower than automatic replacement; guessing or deleting can silently bias results.

## Hypothesis-Test Tree
**When to use**: A sample difference or relationship needs inferential support.
**How**: State H0/HA, branch on one/two/k samples, independent/related cases, scale, assumptions, and power; set alpha, compute, compare p or critical value, and report practical meaning.
**Trade-offs**: Parametric tests have more power under assumptions; nonparametric tests sacrifice some efficiency for robustness.

## Plot, Coefficient, Decision
**When to use**: Measuring association or prediction.
**How**: Inspect scatterplots or tables, select the coefficient by scale and table shape, test it, inspect residuals or ties, and interpret magnitude, uncertainty, practical value, and noncausal alternatives.
**Trade-offs**: Compact coefficients aid comparison, but can conceal curvature, subgroups, outliers, and aggregation artifacts.

## Audience-Effect Report
**When to use**: Analysis must become a management decision.
**How**: Profile the audience, define what it should think/feel/do, choose a report framework, map each recommendation to minimum supporting evidence, visualize, rehearse, deliver, and disclose limits.
**Trade-offs**: Selectivity improves actionability but requires disciplined omission of interesting, unsupported, or out-of-scope results.

## Integrated Project Control
**When to use**: A multi-stage project involves clients, vendors, deadlines, and changing information.
**How**: Screen the RFP, assign roles, control files and scope, link questions to analysis, separate exploratory outputs from final reports, debrief, and design follow-up measurement.
**Trade-offs**: Operational discipline consumes project time but protects methodological validity and continuity.
