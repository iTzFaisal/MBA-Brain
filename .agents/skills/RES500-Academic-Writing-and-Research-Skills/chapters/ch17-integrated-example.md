# Chapter 17: An Integrated Example

## Core Idea

The BrainSavvy/Visionary Insights project demonstrates that a research project is a chain of connected decisions, not a sequence of isolated techniques. A vague management concern becomes a bounded management question; exploration turns that question into investigative and research questions; the design determines who and what to measure; measurement is tested before fielding; analysis is planned before data collection; and reporting turns the strongest supported findings into decisions and next steps.

The chapter's distinctive contribution is its behind-the-scenes view. It shows RFP screening, conflict and capacity checks, proposal scoring, team formation, file organization, assignment of work, client conversations, scope control, instrument debate, pilot decisions, analysis triage, presentation logistics, and post-project learning. Methodological quality and project operations reinforce each other: the quality of the final recommendation depends on both the formal design and the work that makes the design executable.

## Frameworks Introduced (exact names, when/how)

### Integrated Research Process

The project is organized into five named stages:

- **Stage 1: Clarify the Research Question**: move from the management dilemma through exploration to a prioritized question.
- **Stage 2: Research Design**: settle the target population, sampling frame and procedure, measurement instrument, and data collection plan.
- **Stage 3: Data Collection, Preparation, and Examination**: activate the survey, recruit and remind participants, clean and link data, code comments, and conduct exploratory examination.
- **Stage 4: Analyze the Data**: apply planned and additional analyses to identify patterns, drivers, and decision-relevant segments.
- **Stage 5: Report the Research**: prepare the topline and technical products, deliver the oral briefing, handle questions, and support implementation.

Use the stages iteratively. Later findings can expose a need to revisit an earlier assumption, but the team should not allow an unexamined information wish to expand the project without regard to payoff, time, and respondent burden.

### Management-Research Question Hierarchy

The chapter's question path is **Management Dilemma -> Management Question -> Investigative Questions -> Research Questions**.

- The **Management Dilemma** is the increasing number of ServiceCentral complaints, especially unresolved repairs and damaged returned laptops.
- The **Management Question** is what BrainSavvy should do to improve ServiceCentral's repair and servicing program.
- **Investigative Questions** ask how specific components work: call-center assistance, courier performance, repair sequencing, elapsed time, condition at transfer points, and overall satisfaction.
- **Research Questions** translate those investigations into choices such as more operator training, replacing FastDelivery, modifying diagnostics or repair sequencing, changing packaging, or adding metropolitan repair centers.

Use the hierarchy to distinguish an information need from a researchable question and to prevent the questionnaire from becoming a list of everything a manager wants to know.

### Exploration Strategy

The named **Exploration Strategy for BrainSavvy** combines pre- and post-client-visit work:

- Secondary searches on technology service, repair, and customer satisfaction.
- A Dallas visit to understand production, distribution, and ServiceCentral's process.
- Review of customer letters, call transcripts, existing business data, warranty records, and prior studies.
- Expert interviews with the ServiceCentral manager, call-center manager, and FastDelivery account executive.
- Process modeling, data-file analysis, focus groups, and team brainstorming.

Use the strategy to move from anecdotal complaints to a system-level account of where service breaks down.

### Pareto Diagram, Proximity Plot, and Process Modeling

- A **Pareto Diagram** ranks complaint categories and adds a cumulative percentage line. In the ServiceCentral file, unresolved problems and multiple repairs accounted for about 80 percent of perceived inadequate repair service, giving the team a defensible way to prioritize.
- A **Proximity Plot** displays the relationships among the full set of problems, preserving issues that a simple ranking might hide, such as shipping, warranty, parts, phone hold time, and follow-up.
- **Process Modeling** maps the phone-only and phone-plus-physical-repair workflows, including handoffs, deadlines, data entries, courier movement, repair assignment, and delay points. Use it to connect a complaint to the operational event that could produce it.

Together, these tools prevent the team from treating a complaint count as an explanation. Ranking shows concentration, proximity shows the problem field, and process modeling shows where intervention could occur.

### Research Design Phases

The proposal names six phases:

- **Phase 1, Exploration**: interviews, content analysis of logs and complaint letters, and an inventory of internal and external processes.
- **Phase 2, Qualitative**: focus groups with dissatisfied customers to identify component-satisfaction elements.
- **Phase 3, Sampling Design**: draw a probability sample from the ServiceCentral log.
- **Phase 4, Quantitative**: field a customized, pilot-tested online satisfaction survey.
- **Phase 5, Data Analysis**: relate overall satisfaction to component satisfaction and build a performance grid.
- **Phase 6, Non-Response Assessment, Qualitative**: interview a small sample of invited nonparticipants and compare their responses with participants.

This design is sequential and cumulative. Exploration informs the instrument; the instrument and frame support the quantitative benchmark; nonresponse work qualifies the benchmark's representativeness.

### Systematic Probability Sample

The **Systematic Probability Sample** is chosen after the frame is cleaned and randomized. The target population is BrainSavvy laptop owners over 18 who had a ServiceCentral repair experience on a personal laptop in the prior three months. Chardon builds the frame from the ServiceCentral database, removes people whose original repaired laptop is no longer the relevant device unless the new device was also repaired, and randomizes cases to avoid concentration by model or technician. Systematic sampling is selected for lower cost and easier implementation, with perceived service quality and likelihood to repurchase as parameters of interest and a goal of at least 100 cases per month.

### Custom-Designed Expectation Scale

The **Hybrid Expectation Scale**, also called the custom-designed expectation scale, is created because customers and BrainSavvy's credo use the language of meeting or exceeding expectations. The five response points are numerically coded and verbally anchored as **Met Few Expectations**, **Met Some Expectations**, **Met Most Expectations**, **Met All Expectations**, and **Exceeded Expectations**.

The team compares this scale with a Likert scale and BrainSavvy's existing numerical satisfaction scale. They reject a naive off-the-shelf choice because a generic satisfaction scale does not capture the construct that matters to the service operation and its intended decisions.

### Delayed Equivalent Forms and Convergent Reliability

**Delayed Equivalent Forms** tests whether two alternative scales produce equivalent and stable results. Thirty recent ServiceCentral users are split into two groups. One group receives the expectation scale and then the numerical scale; the other receives them in reverse order, one week apart.

**Convergent Reliability** is assessed by comparing the new scale with the existing benchmark measure. On the problem-resolution item, the scales correlate at .90. The expectation scale's one-week stability is .93, compared with .75 for the existing numerical scale. The responses are linear in the comparison plot, and no order effect is found. The team therefore adopts the expectation scale.

### Analysis Plan, Automatic Interaction Detection, and Service Improvement Grid

The **Analysis Plan** selects overall impression of ServiceCentral's effectiveness as the initial dependent variable and uses component ratings, likely reuse, and repurchase measures to examine patterns and drivers. The team prepares cross-tabulations before fielding so the analysis can connect component performance to outcomes rather than merely list frequencies.

**Automatic Interaction Detection (AID)** recursively splits the sample on the component that best differentiates levels of overall satisfaction. In the ServiceCentral analysis, resolution of the problem is the first important split; condition on arrival and speed of repair or technical competence further distinguish subgroups. AID exposes best- and worst-case combinations and points resources toward drivers of the service experience.

The **Service Improvement Grid** plots importance against satisfaction and labels four action zones:

- **Concentrate Efforts**: important issues with marginal satisfaction.
- **Maintain Performance**: important issues already performing well.
- **Low Priority**: less important and less satisfactory issues.
- **Additional Effort Offers Minimal ROI**: relatively satisfactory or less important issues where extra investment has limited return.

Use the grid to convert a collection of means into a prioritization argument. It is a decision aid, not proof that every issue in a quadrant has the same operational solution.

### Top-Box Method of Reporting and Topline Management Report

The **Top-Box Method of Reporting** combines the top response categories to make a management-facing result easier to read. In the monthly example, responses meeting or exceeding expectations are combined for selected measures, while the full distribution remains available in the detailed findings.

A **Topline Management Report** is a concise management product. The BrainSavvy monthly report uses an executive summary, methodology, service improvement grid, findings, and open-ended responses. A parallel technical report remains available for detailed questions and statistical support.

## Key Concepts

- **RFP screening is a research decision.** Bedford Chance checks conflicts of interest, workload, capability, facilities, and delivery timing before VI commits. A prestigious client is not automatically a feasible client.
- **Proposal design is evidence of fit.** VI combines Celina Gamble's qualitative leadership and technology knowledge with Dayton Wager's quantitative and methodological expertise. The proposal covers the problem, technical design, team and outsourcing, schedule, and price.
- **Client selection can be structured.** BrainSavvy defines ten selection criteria before reading proposals, has reviewers score each independently from 1 to 10, interviews the three highest-scoring firms, and then uses separate criteria for the oral presentation.
- **Information needs are not research questions.** Zane Bet's requests for demographics and broad perceptions are tested against expected payoff, cost, researchability, and respondent burden. A long survey lowers completion rates and can reduce the value of the study.
- **Exploration is triangulation.** Customer letters and call transcripts reveal experience; interviews reveal operations; secondary studies provide context and benchmarks; the database provides scale; process models reveal handoffs; focus groups reveal meanings and language.
- **The complaint file needs interpretation.** The Pareto result prioritizes unresolved and repeated repairs, but the proximity plot and process models keep courier, parts, phone access, packaging, and follow-up in view.
- **The target population must match the decision.** Recent repair users are more likely to remember the experience. The case definition excludes irrelevant or stale cases while retaining location and demographic diversity unless those variables become justified segmentation variables.
- **Sampling frame maintenance matters.** A clean frame, randomization, and probability selection support defensible inference. The team explicitly considers outdated contact information, memory decay, model mix, technician mix, and monthly coverage.
- **Measurement language should fit the construct.** Focus groups establish that expectations are central to ServiceCentral's service promise. The team therefore tests a custom expectation scale instead of forcing a generic satisfaction scale.
- **Reliability is multidimensional.** Equivalent forms address convergence with an existing scale; delayed administration addresses stability; counterbalancing addresses order effects; the plot checks whether the relationship is sensible.
- **The instrument is deliberately lean.** It measures the service components, overall impression, likelihood of using ServiceCentral again, likelihood of repurchasing BrainSavvy based on service and product performance, open comments, and follow-up contact. It does not add demographic questions without a clear payoff.
- **A disguised Service Code enables linkage.** The code connects survey responses and comments to call-center and repair-log details for deeper analysis, while the instrument avoids unnecessary identifying descriptors and allows follow-up contact.
- **Analysis begins before fielding.** Overall impression is chosen as the initial dependent variable; dummy cross-tabs and the planned driver analyses give the team a route from component performance to use and repurchase outcomes.
- **Exploratory analysis and report analysis are different.** The team may generate nearly fifty tables and charts to discover patterns, but the management topline receives only the displays that support decisions. A boxplot or AID tree can be valuable internally without belonging in the final topline.
- **Nonresponse remains a live limitation.** The proposal includes phone interviews with invited nonparticipants. The later monthly report states its response rate and explicitly notes that participant/nonparticipant differences are not yet known.
- **Reporting is an intervention.** The oral briefing combines in-room and Web delivery, uses Q&A and a poll to reveal and align priorities, and gives BrainSavvy an interactive way to move from evidence to action.
- **The project has a lifecycle after the presentation.** VI responds to follow-up questions, debriefs the team, and continues with a monthly survey and log assessment. The continuation contract turns the benchmark into a tracking system.

## Mental Models

- **The RFP is a hypothesis, not a complete problem definition.** It contains a starting account and requested methods, but exploration tests what is actually wrong and what can be measured.
- **The question hierarchy is a narrowing funnel.** Move from business tension to decision, from decision to mechanisms, and from mechanisms to measurable variables.
- **The organization is a service system.** A customer outcome is produced by connected employees, software, parts, packaging, courier handoffs, timing, and communication. A satisfaction score without the process map is a symptom, not a diagnosis.
- **Exploration is an evidence braid.** Different sources answer different questions; agreement across them increases confidence, while disagreement identifies what needs investigation.
- **A measurement scale is a contract with respondents and managers.** Its words define what counts as performance, its spacing determines interpretation, and its reliability determines whether comparisons can support decisions.
- **The analysis is a driver tree.** Overall impression is an outcome; component ratings split the outcome into more actionable branches; the best branches identify where corrective resources may matter most.
- **The final report is layered.** The oral briefing creates alignment, the topline creates action memory, the technical report supports scrutiny, and the underlying data and appendices support later questions.
- **Behind-the-scenes infrastructure is part of validity.** Roles, file naming, shared folders, recording, backups, vendor choices, and schedules determine whether the formal design can be executed consistently.
- **A research project is also a relationship.** Scope discipline, transparent limitations, responsive follow-up, and a useful continuation design create value beyond one set of findings.

## Anti-patterns

- Accepting the RFP's requested survey and information list without examining the management decision or operational context.
- Treating a prestigious client as a reason to ignore conflicts, capacity, capability gaps, or an unrealistic schedule.
- Letting every manager request become a questionnaire item, especially demographic items without an identifiable decision payoff.
- Relying on complaint anecdotes without checking their prevalence in the database or tracing them through the service process.
- Using a stale five-year frame when recent experience is required, failing to clean eligibility, or allowing the sample to cluster by model, month, or technician.
- Choosing a generic satisfaction scale despite evidence that the sponsor's construct is expectation-based.
- Launching an untested scale without checking equivalent forms, stability, order effects, or verbal-anchor interpretation.
- Designing the survey first and the analysis later, so the instrument cannot support the intended driver and outcome questions.
- Treating exploratory charts as final findings or showing managers a complex display simply because it was analytically interesting.
- Reporting a response rate without acknowledging uncertainty about nonresponse bias.
- Making a recommendation from one chart, one complaint category, or one subgroup without triangulating the operational evidence.
- Presenting a remote oral report as a passive slide broadcast instead of using questions, polls, follow-up, and an archive to maintain engagement.
- Arriving without backups, prepared answers, a technical report, or a plan for an accelerated deadline.
- Ending the project at the presentation and failing to capture lessons, monitor implementation, or design the next measurement cycle.

## Worked Example

### BrainSavvy ServiceCentral: From Complaint Pattern to Action System

1. **Screen and bid.** BrainSavvy's RFP reports rising calls and letters about its 24-hour ServiceCentral call center and repair facility. VI screens for conflicts, workload, capabilities, and timing. Chance forwards the RFP to Gamble and Wager because the project needs both qualitative and quantitative strength. The proposal promises exploratory work, dissatisfied-customer focus groups, a probability survey, a custom instrument, regression/performance-grid analysis, and nonresponse assessment.
2. **Clarify the problem.** The Dallas visit shows that management has many information wishes, but VI insists on testing their payoff and respondent burden. Letters, transcripts, manager interviews, a FastDelivery interview, secondary research, a Best Buy visit, disguised service calls, loaner-laptop testing, and database review are organized in a shared Dropbox with a file-naming protocol.
3. **Prioritize mechanisms.** Chardon's Pareto diagram shows unresolved problems and repeat repairs account for about 80 percent of perceived inadequate repair service. Process models reveal the ideal 48-hour path and its failure points: technician diagnosis, parts availability, courier pickup and packaging, repair assignment, delay communication, and return delivery. Focus groups are split by laptop model, with a severe-damage replacement group, so the team can identify component satisfaction and customer language.
4. **Define the design.** The target population is adult personal-laptop users repaired by ServiceCentral in the previous three months. The cleaned database becomes the frame; irrelevant replacement cases are removed; records are randomized; systematic probability sampling is selected for cost and implementation reasons. The online survey is direct, branded, short, and optimized for likely laptop or phone use. A disguised Service Code links answers to repair records, and an open comment box captures unusual experiences.
5. **Validate measurement.** VI tests the existing numerical scale against the custom five-point Hybrid Expectation Scale. The wording is adjusted from "met no expectations" to "met few expectations" after BrainSavvy experts flag strictness at the low end. Delayed equivalent forms produce a .90 convergence correlation; one-week stability is .93 for the expectation scale versus .75 for the numerical scale; no order effect appears. The expectation scale is adopted.
6. **Analyze for decisions.** Question 6, overall impression of effectiveness, is the initial outcome. Component cross-tabs, likely reuse and repurchase measures, coded comments, and AID identify the drivers. Poor problem resolution is the first major split; poor condition on arrival worsens the low-satisfaction path, while speed and technical competence distinguish other branches. The team keeps the nearly fifty analytical displays behind the scenes and selects only decision-relevant support for the report.
7. **Report and align.** A shortened deadline requires separate leadership for the oral and written products, graphics by Ethan, prepared Q&A, two projectors, a laptop backup, a topline management report, and a technical report. The oral briefing is both in-person and Web-delivered. A poll asks the audience to choose its first action priority; the result helps reconcile divergent pre-report views. The group reaches a strong first priority: negotiate a stronger courier contract to address in-transit damage. A perceptual map makes the major issues visible.
8. **Track and learn.** The later monthly topline reports 175 respondents and a 35 percent response rate, states that nonresponse differences are unknown, and organizes results around an executive summary, methodology, Service Improvement Grid, findings, and open-ended comments. December identifies responsiveness, technician competence, and courier arrangements as concentrate-efforts arenas; repair speed, problem resolution, and pickup are maintained; delivery speed and arrival condition receive limited additional investment. VI then debriefs the project and supports a 12-month survey and log-assessment continuation.

The artifact is valuable because each decision is traceable: the final priorities are linked to the question hierarchy, the sample to the target population, the scale to the service promise, the analysis to the outcome, and the report to the audience's action.

## Key Takeaways

- An integrated project keeps the management decision visible from RFP screening through reporting.
- Exploration earns the right to specify the research question; it is not a decorative preliminary step.
- Use operational records, interviews, customer language, secondary benchmarks, process models, and qualitative work together to explain a complaint pattern.
- Define the population and frame in terms of the decision, then clean and randomize the frame before probability sampling.
- Customize measurement when the construct, sponsor language, or decision requires it, and validate the change before full fielding.
- Pre-plan the analysis around outcomes and drivers so the survey collects what the decision requires.
- Distinguish exploratory output from the final management artifact.
- State response rates and nonresponse limits, even when the report is intended to persuade.
- Treat the oral report, topline, technical report, interactive poll, backups, and follow-up as one reporting system.
- End with a debrief and a monitoring design so the benchmark can support learning over time.
