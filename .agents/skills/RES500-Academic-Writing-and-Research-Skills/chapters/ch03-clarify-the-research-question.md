# Chapter 3: Stage 1: Clarify the Research Question

## Core Idea
Stage 1 uses purposeful but usually unstructured exploration to turn a management dilemma into a researchable question hierarchy. It also determines whether new information can change the decision enough to justify the cost, then builds the budget and ethical conditions for the next stage.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Stage 1 tasks**: Use the four-part sequence to establish a defensible project: (1) define the management-research question hierarchy, (2) define and execute the exploration strategy, (3) value the research, and (4) justify and budget for research.
- **Management-Research Question Hierarchy**: Use the hierarchy to keep every measurement connected to a decision. Start with the **management dilemma** (problem symptom or opportunity), restate it as the **management question** (what should be done), identify plausible alternatives as **research question(s)**, break each into **investigative questions** (what the manager needs to know), and finally specify **measurement questions** (what participants will be asked or what will be observed). Insights from the measurements support the management decision.
- **Types of Management Questions**: Classify the decision before designing research. **Evaluation of solutions** asks which concrete action should be taken; **choice of purpose** asks what objective or goal should be pursued; **troubleshooting** diagnoses why a program is failing to meet its goals; and **control** monitors or diagnoses performance against goals.
- **Fine-tuning the research question**: Use this checkpoint after preliminary literature review or exploration. Decide whether the original question is already answered or has evolved; define variables operationally, break questions into lower-level questions, state hypotheses appropriately, identify evidence needed, and set explicit boundaries for what is outside scope.
- **Exploration Strategy**: Use exploration to understand the dilemma, discover how others addressed similar problems, prioritize problems, identify options and hypotheses, refine the question, develop operational definitions, and support the later design and budget. Specify both information sources and methods for extracting information. The three subtasks are examination of published sources, examination of digital sources, and interviews with knowledgeable people. A **literature search** proceeds by consulting reference works for search terms, applying those terms in indexes, bibliographies, and the web, locating and reviewing relevant secondary sources, and evaluating each source's value.
- **Primary, secondary, and tertiary sources**: Use the source levels deliberately. **Primary sources** are original research or raw data; **secondary sources** interpret primary data; **tertiary sources** are interpretations or finding aids such as indexes, bibliographies, and search engines. A strong exploration combines internal and external sources and uses people as information gatekeepers: company historians, industry/problem experts, employees involved in the dilemma, and employees affected by possible solutions.
- **External and internal source evaluation**: Evaluate external secondary sources by **purpose, scope, authority, audience, and format**. Evaluate internal sources by compatibility with the current dilemma, timelessness, recentness, quality of the original research, and the experience of the authors, while considering the political and managerial context in which the source was created.
- **Option Analysis and research value**: Use option analysis before approving a study. Specify two or more action options, one or more quantifiable decision variables, an unbiased decision rule, and the estimated research budget. Research has positive value when new information could change the decision or improve its result; it has negative value when the best decision could already be made without it, including the research's cost and opportunity cost. If value cannot be estimated in advance, use an ex post facto evaluation to improve future decisions.
- **Research budgeting**: Use a **task budget** when funds come from discretionary reserves or an in-house research operation, and a **functional area budget** when the manager allocates part of the business unit's operating funds. Both compete with other priorities and may require a persuasive written proposal.

## Key Concepts
- **Management dilemma**: The symptom, problem, or opportunity that triggers a decision.
- **Management question**: The dilemma restated as a question about action or objectives.
- **Research question**: The principal objective of the study, often framed as a choice among plausible actions.
- **Investigative question**: Information the decision maker needs in order to choose among alternatives.
- **Measurement question**: An actual survey question or observation item used to collect evidence.
- **Exploration**: Loosely structured research with a defined purpose of clarifying the dilemma and question.
- **Exploration strategy**: The planned sources and methods used during exploration.
- **Data warehouse**: An electronic repository organizing large volumes of continually updated data for retrieval and organizational intelligence.
- **Data mining**: Digital extraction used to discover patterns and predict trends or behaviors in internal data.
- **Decision variable**: A projected, quantifiable effect of an option.
- **Decision rule**: The criterion used to choose objectively among options.
- **Source evaluation**: Assessing the credibility and usefulness of information before relying on it.

## Mental Models
- Use a **question funnel**: move from "What is wrong or promising?" to "What action is possible?" to "What must we know?" to "What will we ask or observe?" If a measurement cannot be traced upward, it is probably scope creep.
- Treat exploration as **low-cost uncertainty reduction**, not aimless browsing. Spend less on discovering what is already known before commissioning costly primary research.
- Triangulate a dilemma through a **source ladder**: original records and knowledgeable people provide evidence; secondary sources provide interpretation; tertiary sources point to leads that still require evaluation.
- Apply the **value-of-information test**: if the manager will make the same decision whether or not the study is run, the study has negative value.

## Anti-patterns
- **Jumping from symptom to survey**: A declining metric is not yet a research question; exploration may reveal a different underlying dilemma or a better action.
- **Stopping at an overbroad management question**: "How can we increase profits?" must be decomposed into actionable questions such as improving deposits or reducing complaint-producing operations.
- **Treating search results or company dashboards as verified truth**: Tertiary leads and internal data require source evaluation, context, and compatibility checks.
- **Letting data mining replace question clarification**: Pattern discovery and prediction can reveal options, but historical fields may not fit today's operational definitions or decision.
- **Restricting exploration to preferred evidence or a hidden agenda**: Suppressing unfavorable discoveries can misstate the research question and destroy quality.
- **Budgeting research that cannot change action**: If existing experience and information already support the same decision, account for the study's cost and opportunity cost rather than approving it automatically.

## Worked Example
SalePro sees declining sales in the South and Northeast while the Southwest grows. Exploration shows that environmental conditions do not explain the regional difference. The management question becomes how to improve sales in the two regions. Plausible research questions compare a 2 percent commission on sales over quota, a 5 percent regional profit bonus if sales rise 10 percent, a product-formula change, or more trade advertising. Investigative questions ask whether compensation would drive away strong salespeople, reduce customer satisfaction, or lose existing customers. The resulting measurement questions ask affected salespeople to rate 1-to-7 concern about unpredictable monthly pay, internal competition, and reduced post-sale service. The hierarchy keeps the eventual survey tied to an action decision instead of merely documenting that sales declined.

## Key Takeaways
1. Clarify the dilemma before naming a research method.
2. Convert symptoms into a hierarchy that ends in measurable evidence and begins with a management decision.
3. Explore internal records, external literature, digital sources, and knowledgeable people rather than relying on one source class.
4. Evaluate sources for purpose, scope, authority, audience, and format; evaluate internal data for fit, timing, quality, and context.
5. Approve research only when its information can improve or change the decision enough to justify its cost.
6. Make the budget argument in terms of decision variables, decision rules, alternatives, expected value, and funding source.
7. Ethical Stage 1 practice requires full disclosure of relevant facts, access to necessary people and records, a clear scope, unrestricted relevant secondary sources, shared discoveries, and no hidden agenda.
