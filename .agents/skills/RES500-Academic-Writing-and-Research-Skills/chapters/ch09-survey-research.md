# Chapter 9: Survey Research

## Core Idea
A survey is a structured interview, with or without a human interviewer, in which questions are carefully selected, sequenced, and asked consistently so responses are comparable across cases. Use it when the research requires attitudes, motivations, expectations, intentions, knowledge, or past events that observation cannot provide; select the mode by balancing the investigative question, participant access and articulation, speed, cost, privacy, and error risks.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Communication Approach and Survey Research Process**: Translate investigative questions into measurement questions, select a survey method, prepare the instrument, train interviewers when used, verify protocols, pretest the instrument and procedures, select cases using a probability or nonprobability design, and collect and prepare comparable data. Probability sampling makes findings projectable to a larger population; a survey alone does not.
- **Selecting a Survey Method**:
  - **Self-administered survey**: Use mail, email, web, mobile, or intercept when low cost, geographic reach, participant privacy, hard-to-reach respondents, visual stimuli, or time to think matter and probing is not essential. Computer-delivered self-interviews are also called **CASI**; targeted and self-selecting recruitment have different coverage risks.
  - **Telephone survey**: Use when rapid completion, moderate cost, broad geography, repeated callbacks, or access to hard-to-reach participants matters and the questionnaire can be relatively short and verbal. **CATI** supports interviewer prompts and direct data entry; computer-administered telephone surveys may use touch-tone, voice recognition, or automatic speech recognition.
  - **Survey via personal interview**: Use a structured interview when depth, probing, visual material, controlled conditions, observation of nonverbal behavior, or access to illiterate/functionally illiterate participants is important. A trained interviewer may conduct an individual depth interview (IDI), and **CAPI** can enter responses directly.
  - **Mixed-mode survey**: Combine modes when no single method meets the project's access, speed, cost, and depth requirements. Treat mode as a possible source of measurement difference, not merely a logistical convenience.
- **Total Design Method (TDM)**: Maximize participation by making the instrument easy to read, giving clear directions, personalizing communication, providing advance notification, and encouraging response. Diagnose the entire participation chain: delivery, opening, routing to the right person, motivation, completion, and return.
- **Participant Motivation**: Participation improves when the experience seems pleasant and satisfying, the task seems important and worthwhile, and reservations about participation are removed. Explain the purpose and use of the information appropriately, establish confidence and rapport, provide a credible invitation, and respect the participant's time.
- **Error in Survey Research**: Plan for three source families: measurement questions and instruments, interviewers, and participants. Reduce error through sound question wording/order/instructions, screening, standardized training and supervision, accurate data entry, suitable environments, pretesting, and an explicit error-reduction plan.
- **Outsourcing Survey Tasks**: Contract the whole project or only exploration, design, sampling, fieldwork, processing, or analysis when specialized expertise, a large field team, centralized interviewing, panel access, software, rapid turnaround, or confidentiality requirements exceed internal resources. Use panels for longitudinal tracking, and distinguish omnibus from syndicated research.
- **Informed Consent for Surveys and the IRB Process**: A consent introduction should identify the researcher and sponsor, topic and purpose, target population, good-faith time estimate, privacy/confidentiality protections, voluntary status, acceptability of item nonresponse, and permission to begin. Debrief when deception was used; follow applicable Institutional Review Board (IRB) review, revision, and continuing-approval requirements.

## Key Concepts
- **Structured interview**: A standardized question-and-order format designed to make differences in answers, rather than question delivery, interpretable.
- **Self-administered survey**: A questionnaire completed without interviewer assistance, including mail, web, mobile, email, and intercept forms.
- **Noncontact rate**: The percentage of potential contacts not reached, excluding refusals.
- **Refusal rate**: The percentage of contacted potential participants who decline.
- **Nonresponse error**: Systematic difference between participants and nonparticipants or between respondents and missing items.
- **Response error**: Failure to provide a correct or complete answer, including misunderstanding and misrepresentation.
- **Interviewer error**: Bias or data-quality loss caused by sampling/cooperation failures, inaccurate recording, inconsistent procedures, poor environment, falsification, influencing behavior, or physical presence.
- **Physical presence bias**: Participant response changes caused by the interviewer's appearance, behavior, identity, or perceived status.
- **Acquiescence bias**: A tendency to agree, affected by knowledge, language, anonymity, question speed, and question placement.
- **Social desirability bias**: Altering an answer to appear acceptable, rational, or favorable to an interviewer or others.
- **Probe**: A prepared follow-up used to clarify or complete an answer without leading the participant.
- **Panel**: The same participants surveyed over time to track attitudes, adoption, consumption, or trends.

## Mental Models
- **Fit the mode to the information**: Use self-administration for privacy and scale, telephone for speed and reach, personal interviews for depth and probing, and mixed mode only when the added cost and mode effects are justified.
- **Participation is a funnel**: A valid sample can still fail at delivery, contact, invitation, motivation, completion, or return. Improve each break point rather than focusing only on the questionnaire.
- **Know, understand, and want**: A participant must possess the requested information, understand the role as an accurate provider, and be motivated to cooperate. Failure at any one produces weak data.
- **Silence is not neutral**: Noncontact, refusal, skipped items, and "don't know" responses may be systematic. Diagnose why information is absent before treating it as random missingness.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating surveys as the default method when the question is directly observable, or asking participants to report events they did not experience as if they were primary facts.
- Choosing mail for a deadline, telephone for complex visual tasks, or personal interviews for a dispersed population without acknowledging the resulting cost and coverage tradeoffs.
- Launching a long or complex instrument without a pretest and a defensible completion-time estimate; fatigue and abandonment create error.
- Treating a self-selected online panel as representative without a coverage and selection argument, or assuming mixed modes are interchangeable.
- Leading through tone, emphasis, rephrasing, body language, or unplanned probes; altering question order; summarizing answers inaccurately; or falsifying interviews.
- Conflating noncontact, refusal, nonresponse, and response error, or forcing a "don't know" participant to invent an opinion.
- Hiding sales or fundraising behind research (**sugging** or **frugging**), lying to gain access or intelligence, or understating the time required.
- Ignoring privacy, confidentiality, informed consent, participant or interviewer safety, data quality, or required IRB review.

## Worked Example
**Detecting survey-mode effects.** A mixed-mode study compared reports of neutral grocery-shopping behavior and a more socially sensitive behavior, alcohol consumption, using interviewer-administered landline and cell-phone modes, an online-recruited interviewer mode, and online self-completion. Reports of grocery shopping were nearly alike across modes, suggesting little mode effect for a neutral behavioral item. Alcohol reports were lower in interviewer-involved modes and higher in self-completion, consistent with reduced social-desirability error when participants felt more anonymous.

The design lesson is not that online surveys are always best. Self-completion may reduce social-desirability error but can introduce coverage, recruitment, technology, and nonresponse problems. A researcher should therefore select the mode after identifying the sensitivity of the measures, the accessible sample frame, the needed speed and geographic coverage, and whether probing or visual material is essential; then pretest and compare mode-specific quality before combining results.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use surveys for information participants can articulate, especially internal states and past or intended behavior; use observation for directly observable acts.
2. Choose self-administered, telephone, personal, or mixed mode from the question and constraints, not habit.
3. Keep questions, order, transitions, instructions, interviewer behavior, and recording procedures standardized.
4. Treat participation as a design responsibility: clear invitations, meaningful purpose, manageable burden, reminders, return mechanisms, and appropriate incentives matter.
5. Distinguish nonresponse, response, interviewer, measurement, coverage, and physical-environment errors before interpreting results.
6. Probability sampling supports projection; high response alone cannot repair a biased frame or self-selection.
7. Pretest time and procedures, obtain informed consent, protect privacy and confidentiality, debrief when warranted, and protect interviewer safety.
