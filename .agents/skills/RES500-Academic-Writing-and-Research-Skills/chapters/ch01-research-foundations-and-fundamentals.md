# Chapter 1: Research Foundations and Fundamentals

## Core Idea
Business research is systematic inquiry that provides information to guide a specified managerial decision. It complements data analytics: analytics mines existing data for patterns, while research supplies dilemma-specific evidence and insight when existing information does not explain what is happening or what should be done.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Business research versus data analytics**: Use the distinction when deciding whether a dilemma needs new research. Data analytics draws mainly on existing internal or external data, including a decision support system and data blended from separate files. Research plans, acquires, analyzes, and reports relevant data for a particular decision; it may supply the new, smaller-scale evidence that analytics cannot provide. Data used for a purpose other than the one for which it was collected is still analytics, not automatically research.
- **The Research Process**: Use the five-stage sequence to keep a project orderly, while recognizing that stages may overlap, recycle, or be skipped when information is already available: (1) clarify the research question, (2) design the research, (3) collect and prepare data, (4) analyze and interpret data, and (5) report insights and recommendations.
- **Scientific Method**: Use it to make conclusions observable, testable, and self-correcting. Define concepts, constructs, variables, methods, and procedures clearly; formulate empirically testable hypotheses; observe phenomena directly; base conclusions on statistical evidence rather than inferred justification; and replicate or reassess conclusions.
- **Concept-construct-operational definition-variable chain**: Use this chain before measuring an abstract idea. Classify observations into a **concept**; combine concrete concepts into a theory- or study-specific **construct**; state an **operational definition** as criteria that any competent observer could apply consistently; and represent the concept or construct with measurable **variables**. A conceptual scheme maps relationships among the concepts and constructs.
- **Variable types**: Use the roles to specify a research relationship. The **independent variable (IV)** is the presumed cause or manipulated condition and the **dependent variable (DV)** is the measured outcome. A **moderating variable (MV)** changes the strength or direction of the IV-DV relationship. An extraneous variable may be a **control variable (CV)** whose possible influence is measured but is not central, a **confounding variable (CFV)** whose alternative influence must be measured, or an **intervening variable (IVV)** whose theoretical effect is inferred rather than directly observed.
- **Double movement of reflective thought**: Use induction to move from an observed fact to a plausible hypothesis, then deduction to derive observable consequences and test them. Develop rival hypotheses, run a crucial empirical test that can exclude alternatives, infer a conclusion, and feed the evidence back into the original problem.
- **Hypothesis, theory, and model**: A **hypothesis** is an unsubstantiated, empirically testable assumption about concepts or constructs; a **theory** is a set of data-tested, supported hypotheses; and a **model** is a visual representation of a theory or system used to clarify, understand, predict, or control it. A strong hypothesis is adequate for its purpose, testable, and better than its rivals.
- **Checklist for Developing a Strong Hypothesis**: Use the three-part test before committing to a design. It should be adequate for its purpose (identify the problem, relevant facts, suitable design, and conclusion framework), testable (plausible, simple, and capable of yielding observable consequences), and better than its rivals (explain more or a wider range of facts and be the most credible explanation to informed judges).

## Key Concepts
- **Business research**: A systematic process of planning, acquiring, analyzing, synthesizing, and communicating relevant information so decision makers can act toward organizational goals.
- **Data analytics**: Analysis of existing data to extract insights, identify patterns, or support decisions.
- **Data blending**: Combining separate data files into a composite data file for querying and decision support; it is useful but is not itself research.
- **Concept**: A generally accepted collection of meanings or characteristics associated with an event, object, condition, situation, or behavior.
- **Construct**: An abstract idea specifically invented for a research or theory-building purpose.
- **Operational definition**: A definition expressed as specific criteria for measurement or testing.
- **Variable**: A measurable symbol or substitute for an event, characteristic, concept, or construct.
- **Hypothesis**: A conjectured relationship that can be judged true or false through empirical measurement.
- **Theory**: An empirically supported description of relationships among concepts, constructs, and hypotheses.
- **Model**: A representation of a theory or system.

## Mental Models
- Think of information as **decision fuel**: historical data may be regular fuel, while new data tailored to the dilemma can enrich the mixture.
- Use an **abstraction ladder**: concept -> construct -> operational definition -> variable. Every step downward should make observation more reproducible without pretending the abstract idea is directly visible.
- Treat an IV-DV claim as a map, not a fact. Ask whether a moderator changes the relationship, a confounder offers a rival explanation, or an intervening variable explains the mechanism.
- Use the induction-deduction loop to resist premature certainty: facts generate hypotheses, and hypotheses generate the next facts to seek.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating analytics or data blending as research**: Existing data may be stale, collected for another purpose, or unable to answer the current why.
- **Leaving concepts operationally vague**: Terms such as income, socialization, or productivity produce inconsistent data unless the time period, scope, units, and observation rules are specified.
- **Reading correlation as causation**: A relationship can be real without establishing which variable influences which, or whether a third variable explains it.
- **Testing only a favored hypothesis**: Multiple explanations and rival hypotheses reduce researcher attachment and confirmation bias.
- **Using a model as proof**: A model clarifies a theory; it does not replace empirical testing of the theory's assumptions.

## Worked Example
To evaluate a four-day workweek, define the workweek arrangement as the **IV** and a consistent productivity measure as the **DV**. The hypothesis is that the shorter week increases productivity by increasing **job satisfaction** (the unobserved **IVV**), especially for younger workers (the **MV**). Measure weather as a **CV** if it could affect productivity but is not central, and measure type of work as a **CFV** because the effect may differ for office, manufacturing, and distribution jobs. If younger workers already outperform older workers by 12 percentage points and the four-day schedule adds 6 points for all workers, an observed 18-point difference is only the sum of main effects, not moderation. A 25-point difference would show a surplus consistent with an age interaction. The study must operationalize each variable before comparing groups.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start with the managerial dilemma and decide whether existing information can change the decision.
2. Do not confuse a large database with an answer to a current question.
3. Make abstract ideas measurable through constructs and operational definitions.
4. Assign variable roles according to the relationship being studied, not according to the variable's name.
5. Use induction to generate hypotheses and deduction to derive tests.
6. Treat theories as revisable and models as representations, not substitutes for evidence.
