# Chapter 2: The Research Process: An Overview

## Core Idea
Good research has value only when it helps a manager make a better decision than the manager could make with existing information. The process is a useful five-stage sequence, but quality depends on decision relevance, defensible procedures, ethical conduct, appropriate analysis, frank limitations, and clear reporting.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Six standards of good research**: Use this as a quality-control checklist. The purpose must be clearly defined in writing, including scope and meanings; the research design must be thoroughly planned and executed without personal bias; high ethical standards must protect people, organizations, and data; analysis must fit the decision maker's needs and disclose error, validity, and reliability; limitations must be frankly revealed with their likely effect; and findings, insights, and conclusions must be reported unambiguously and justified by the evidence.
- **The Research Process**: Use the stages as a decision-linked workflow. **Stage 1: Clarify the Research Question** identifies and prioritizes the dilemma, explores what is known and unknown, tests whether research would improve the decision, and identifies funding. **Stage 2: Design the Research Project** specifies data collection and sampling. **Stage 3: Collect and Prepare the Data** executes collection, editing, coding, and error handling. **Stage 4: Analyze and Interpret the Data** reduces data, identifies patterns, produces findings and insights, and supports recommendations. **Stage 5: Report Insights and Recommendations** adapts the communication to the audience and the management decision.
- **Data collection and sampling design**: Use the two design decisions together. Data collection design specifies what data, method, timing, instrument, wording, scaling, and procedures will be used. Sampling design defines the target population, the case, the number of cases, and how cases will be recruited or selected; choose a census when every case is examined and a sample when only part of the population is studied.
- **Four measurement scales**: Use the scale that matches the intended empirical operation. **Nominal** data classify and support equality judgments; **ordinal** data classify and order but lack equal distances; **interval** data add equal distances but have no natural origin; **ratio** data add a natural origin and support ratio comparisons. The desired analysis should influence what is collected.
- **Data quality dimensions**: Evaluate data not only by scale but also by **abstractness, verifiability, elusiveness, and truthfulness**. Abstract data require substitutes for direct observation; verifiable data produce consistent sensory results; elusive data should be captured as near to the event as possible; and truthful data accurately reflect the phenomenon measured.
- **Findings-insights-recommendations chain**: First reduce raw data to findings and patterns; interpret those findings against the research questions to create insights; then make recommendations only when the evidence supports an action within the study's scope.
- **Critical Path Method (CPM) and Gantt charts**: Use CPM to identify the longest-duration path whose delay delays the project, and use a Gantt chart to track all tasks, overlaps, milestones, and schedule recovery. Record actual timing to improve future estimates.
- **Research process ethical responsibilities**: Use the rights-and-responsibilities view at every stage. Participants deserve informed consent, safety, choice, privacy, confidentiality, and absence of harm; sponsors deserve quality research, confidentiality, and protected findings; researchers deserve truthful participation, completion, absence of sponsor deception or coercion, and adequate compensation. Managers must maintain accurate records, disclose purpose truthfully, discourage falsification, and fulfill contracts.

## Key Concepts
- **Management dilemma**: A problem symptom or opportunity that requires a decision.
- **Research design**: The blueprint for collecting data that fulfills objectives and answers questions.
- **Primary data**: Raw data recorded directly by the researcher or data collector.
- **Secondary data**: Data containing at least one level of interpretation, often used during exploration.
- **Target population**: The people, events, or records possessing information needed to answer the research question.
- **Case**: One unit in the target population from which variables can be measured.
- **Sample**: A portion of the target population examined in a study.
- **Key performance indicator (KPI)**: A metric used to track performance against organizational, unit, or individual goals.
- **Dashboard**: A visualization showing current and prior status on key metrics.
- **Validity and reliability**: Whether data measure what they should and whether collection produces consistent results.
- **Findings, insights, recommendations**: Successive outputs from analysis, interpretation, and decision-oriented reporting.

## Mental Models
- Think of the process as a **funnel from dilemma to action**: raw observations become data, data become findings, findings become insights, and insights support a bounded recommendation.
- Use the sequence without becoming captive to it. Recycling or doing stages simultaneously is acceptable; skipping a decision-critical stage is not.
- Treat the measurement scale as a **permission structure**: the scale determines which comparisons and statistical operations are defensible.
- Plan time backward from the decision. Protect the CPM's critical path, but use parallel work and recovery time where possible.

## Anti-patterns
- **Collecting interesting information without a decision purpose**: Information has limited business value if it cannot improve a critical choice.
- **Treating big data as a substitute for dilemma-specific research**: Database fields may reflect another purpose, period, or operational definition and may sit in incompatible silos.
- **Using a favored technique for every question**: Require justification for the chosen design and rejection of plausible alternatives.
- **Accepting an ill-defined or unresearchable question**: A question must be answerable through data and analysis, current techniques, and accessible, willing cases.
- **Overgeneralizing from exploration or a limited sample**: Personal experience and a small or unrepresentative sample do not justify population-level claims.
- **Hiding limitations or coding problems**: Out-of-range answers, missing data, weak procedures, and sample differences must be addressed and disclosed.

## Worked Example
For a survey of 2,000 prospects considering a new Pocket-Phone, four yes/no questions produce 8,000 raw responses. Six demographic questions multiply the data further. Analysis reduces the four questions to eight percentages (yes and no for each), then cross-tabulates the answers by gender, producing 16 statistics plus significance tests. An open-ended question about undesirable phone situations requires coding keywords and sentiment before patterns can be interpreted. The manager should receive the findings that answer the decision question, not the entire data file: explain the relevant pattern, the analysis used, the uncertainty and limitations, and any recommendation that the evidence supports.

## Key Takeaways
1. Judge research by decision usefulness, not by novelty or volume of data.
2. Define the purpose and scope before choosing a method or sample.
3. Keep collection, preparation, analysis, interpretation, and reporting traceable to the research question.
4. Match measurement scales and analytical procedures to the data actually collected.
5. Report limitations and uncertainty as part of the result, not as an afterthought.
6. Protect participants, sponsors, researchers, and data from deception, coercion, harm, privacy loss, and bias.
