# Chapter 16: Research Reports: Supported Insights and Recommendations

## Core Idea

A research report is not a warehouse for everything the researcher found. It is an audience-facing argument that uses selected evidence to help the right decision makers understand, trust, and act on actionable insights. The reporting task therefore begins with the desired audience effect, not with a list of available tables.

The chapter describes three linked shifts in reporting:

- Data and information are no longer sufficient; the researcher must interpret them as insights and, where justified, recommendations.
- Oral reporting, often paired with a written report, has become central because technology makes interaction, clarification, and real-time questions easier.
- Planning is audience-centric rather than data-centric. Statistics support a persuasive research story; they are not the story's sole focus.

The report must show how an insight was reached, provide enough credible support for the audience to judge its value, and route the insight to the people who can act on it. Reporting is successful when the audience embraces the relevant insight or recommendation without being misled about what the study can establish.

## Frameworks Introduced (exact names, when/how)

### Data, Information, Insights, and Actionable Insights

- **Data** are raw, unprocessed facts, including numbers, text, pictures, and video.
- **Information** results when data are aggregated, organized, measured, statistically summarized or tested, and visualized.
- **Insights** result from analyzing information in light of the management dilemma, the research questions, and the sponsor's business context. They commonly depend on interactions among variables rather than one isolated result.
- **Actionable Insights** are insights that lead to a specific decision. They should align with business goals, be novel or unexpected, be clearly explained in terms of importance and decision value, and reach the appropriate decision makers.

Use this chain after analysis and interpretation to decide what belongs in the report. Do not confuse a statistically interesting result with an actionable insight.

### Audience-Centric Planning

**Audience-Centric Planning** starts with the effect the report should have on the audience: what members should think, feel, and do. The planning sequence is:

1. Conduct **Audience Analysis**.
2. Define the **Desired Audience Effect**.
3. Choose the **Report Structure**.
4. Organize content and style through five activities: organize, visualize, compile, practice, and deliver.

This replaces **Data-Centric Planning**, which emphasizes sharing as much data or information as possible. Audience-centric reports are more selective, persuasive, and story-like; data become support for the decision rather than the endpoint.

### Audience Analysis and Audience Profile Statement

**Audience Analysis** may be informal, using conversations with the sponsor liaison or opinion leaders, or formal, using interviews, surveys, and background research on audience members. Summarize the result in an **Audience Profile Statement** for planning; it is not normally included in the sponsor's report.

The analysis must cover six named areas:

- **Audience Composition**: who receives the report, including role, authority, experience, education, organizational culture, and technical or analytical skill.
- **Research Question Knowledge**: what the audience already knows, what was shared during exploration, and what must be explained.
- **Research Predispositions**: attitudes and beliefs formed by prior knowledge, experience, and values.
- **Recommendation Effects**: who gains, loses, bears risk, or controls implementation, and who may support or resist the recommendation.
- **Audience Effect**: the specific insight or recommendation the audience must accept or act upon.
- **Supplemental Purposes**: whether unexpected but tangential findings should be included, reported separately, or excluded at the sponsor's direction.

Use the profile to predict resistance and receptivity, especially for a remote audience, which requires deliberate work to establish and maintain connection.

### Report Structure

**Report Structure** is the combination of oral and written components:

- **Oral Only**: fast dissemination and immediate questions, usually accompanied by a slide deck, topline handout, or infographic even when there is no formal written report.
- **Written Only**: lets readers work at their own pace and handle large volumes of information, but offers no immediate clarification and can be shelved without advocacy for implementation.
- **Oral Plus Written**: combines persuasion and clarification with durable detail. The written component may be a brief management report, a detailed report, or a technical report, depending on the audience and use.

Choose the structure from audience composition, location, available time, need for interaction, and the level of detail required during decision execution. When the written report precedes the oral report, the oral should clarify and persuade rather than simply repeat it.

### Report Types and Report Components

The chapter distinguishes three audience-based types:

- **Management Report**: brief/topline or long; written for managers and limited in methodology detail.
- **Technical Report**: short or long; written for people knowledgeable about research methods and analysis.
- **Infographic**: a visual written report for a nontechnical audience that may include employees, suppliers, customers, or other stakeholders.

The common report components are **Introduction**, **Background**, **Methodology**, **Findings, Insights, and Recommendations**, and, in written reports, **Appendices**.

- The **Introduction** establishes scope, credibility, and navigation. A descriptive title states the variables, relationship, and population. A written report may add a researcher profile, executive summary, and table of contents.
- The **Background** gives the management question, research objectives, and exploration information needed to interpret the results and judge validity. Its detail depends on audience involvement and knowledge.
- **Methodology** summarizes sample design and profile, margin of error and confidence where appropriate, research design, data collection and the rationale for multiple methods, analysis, and limitations. Limitations increase credibility when they explain how problems affect validity and how they were handled.
- **Findings, Insights, and Recommendations** is selective. Include information needed to understand a hypothesis, insight, or recommendation, not every table or graph generated during analysis.
- **Appendices** hold complex tables and tests, instruments, interview or observation materials, collector instructions, contracts or RFPs, and bibliographies for later reference. They are especially useful for technical readers.

### Traditional Report Frameworks

A **Report Framework** determines both the order of content and the types of support materials that will work. Select it for the particular management problem rather than applying a familiar template automatically.

- **Research Brief**: project origin, purpose, and methodology, followed by insights and supporting findings, usually ending with recommendations. Use as the general-purpose structure.
- **Topical**: organizes several findings and insights by subject or measurement-instrument topic. Use when one topic naturally leads to the next.
- **Narrative**: uses stories, participant quotations, audio, or video as the primary vehicle for communicating insights and recommendations. Order stories by audience impact.
- **Classification**: groups findings, insights, and recommendations into categories, such as product features or customer segments.
- **Motivated Sequence**: establishes the need for change, presents supporting data, pictures the benefits of change, and details recommendations. Use when overt action is the goal.
- **Problem/Solution**: describes the problem, presents the research findings and insights, and ends with a recommendation.
- **Climax Order**: moves from least important foundation information to the most important insight and recommended action.
- **Chronological**: follows the sequence in which research activities occurred. Use when the timing of activities clarifies the result.
- **Past/Present/Future**: explains historical patterns, current conditions, and predicted consequences or opportunities.
- **Cause/Effect/Solution**: identifies the cause, shows its effects, and presents a solution.
- **Pros/Cons/Recommendations**: compares benefits and disadvantages, then explains why the benefits justify the recommended action.
- **Spatial**: organizes material by physical location or geographic area.

When ordering findings, account for **Primacy**, the importance assigned to what comes first; the **Early** advantage for the first few items; **Recency**, the influence of what comes last; and **Perceptible Difference**, the tendency to treat clearly differentiated items as more important. Also order by relevance to the management question and to understanding the insight or recommendation. A storyboard or decision tree can map each recommendation to the evidence it requires.

### Supporting Materials and the Three Proofs

**Supporting Materials** are the evidence and explanatory devices attached to the report framework. The chapter's **A Checklist for Better Support Materials** asks whether each item is:

- Relevant to the point and topical theme.
- Audience Appropriate for the audience and report structure.
- Simple enough to understand quickly.
- Detailed enough to explain the insight and its management significance.
- Believable, accurate, ethically sourced, and fairly presented.
- Balanced in quantity and variety.
- Varied rather than dependent on one form of support.
- Styled effectively through analogy or metaphor when useful.
- Spatially workable within time and venue limits.
- Speaker Specific, fitting the researcher's delivery style as well as the message.

The persuasive architecture is **Aristotle's Proofs**:

- **Ethos** establishes the researcher's credibility through qualifications, experience, prior performance, credible methods, and expert testimony.
- **Logos** supplies the logical argument through facts, valid statistics, and demonstrations.
- **Pathos** creates an emotional connection through stories, quotes, audio, video, analogy, and metaphor, while respecting audience identity and self-interest.

Facts and statistics are the core of many research reports, but logos alone may fail when an audience distrusts or misunderstands statistics. A credible report combines all three proofs without substituting emotion for evidence.

### Visualization and Finding Displays

**Visualization** is the development of textual, tabular, graphical, audio, and video support that lets the audience share the researcher's understanding. Choose displays for data clarity and audience familiarity, not for novelty or visual decoration.

- **Words/Text** work for a small number of statistics, findings, insights, and recommendations. Highlight important numbers so they do not disappear inside prose. In qualitative reports, a short theme statement followed by carefully identified verbatim quotations can be the main finding.
- **Tables** present related data in rows and columns for comparison. Use a concise summary table for a specific insight; move large general tables to a written appendix unless they are central. A table needs a meaningful title, optional subtitle, row stub, column heads, body, explanatory footnotes, and a source note for secondary data.
- **Graphs** reveal fewer details than tables but improve comparison, perceived importance, and retention. Select a graph for the relationship being shown and the audience's ability to interpret it.

Important graph names and uses include:

- **Line Graph**: time-series trends; use time on the horizontal axis, the dependent variable vertically, a zero baseline, distinguishable line types, and no more than about four lines.
- **Area Graph**: total frequency, group frequency, or changing composition over time.
- **Pie Graph**: parts of a whole or a single frequency distribution. Label slices directly, do not use one pie to show change over time, and complement a time series with an area display when needed.
- **Bar Graph** and **Column Bar Graph**: comparisons across categories or over time. Horizontal bars help with long category labels; simple bars are usually best orally, while more complex variations may suit written reports.
- **Stacked Bar Graph**: shows components while allowing the audience to consider collapsed categories.
- **Deviation Bar Graph** and **Mirror Image Bar Graph**: make positive/negative or opposing differences visible.
- **Pictograph**: uses repeated, same-scale pictures to show quantities. Changing picture size instead of stacking equal images can exaggerate differences.
- **Geograph**: maps regional patterns such as sales, response rates, demographics, or productivity.
- **3-D Graph**: represents three or more variables, such as a multivariate surface or scatter pattern. It may be useful in a technical written report, but it is rarely appropriate for a nontechnical oral audience. Decorative pseudo-3-D effects reduce clarity.
- **Bubble**, **Multiple Item Scatterplot**, **Filled Line**, **Boxplots**, **Stacked Pie**, and **Spider (and Radar)** displays are specialized options for introducing a third variable, comparing multiple series, emphasizing a series, showing distributions, comparing data series, or showing several performance dimensions. Use them only when their added structure helps the audience.

Scale choice is an ethical and interpretive decision. A zero baseline and a prominent break are preferable when a scale must be condensed. Arithmetic scales show absolute differences; semi-logarithmic scales preserve percentage relationships when growth rates are the issue. A line graph used for unrelated categories, a pie chart used alone for a time series, or a truncated axis without warning can create a false impression.

For oral reports, pictures must be closely related to the slide topic. Audio and video can improve understanding and persuasion; motion can reveal the part of a graph being discussed; whitespace, large type, limited text, and one main graph per slide improve data clarity. A flow marker can show the audience and speaker where they are in the report.

### Infographics, Compilation, Practice, and Delivery

An **Infographic** blends data and design into a concise visual message. Design choices serve three communication goals: appeal, comprehension, and retention. For business research, comprehension dominates. Position and size should express relationships and importance; the infographic may prime readers for a fuller report but is not linked to underlying data in the way a dashboard is.

Compilation prepares the written report, oral presentation, and support aids. Limit the number of insights on a page or slide. Use the same presentation style for corresponding written and oral material, while making oral slides less dense because the presenter supplies explanation. Control jargon, language level, and tone: define necessary research terms, use short familiar sentences, target a readable high-school-senior level, and express insights and recommendations professionally and positively.

An **Extemporaneous Presentation** is audience-centered and uses minimal keyword notes. Practice determines timing, pauses, engagement exercises, anticipated questions, staging, and contingency plans. It reduces performance anxiety and improves the ability to respond to audience reactions. Reading a manuscript, memorizing exact wording, or speaking impromptu generally weakens rapport and flexibility.

Delivery may be physical, Web-based, or combined. Face-to-face delivery offers stronger emotional connection at greater cost; Web delivery reaches dispersed audiences more cheaply but requires extra planning to compensate for weaker rapport. Web platforms can provide typed questions, polls, archives, registration data for audience analysis, follow-up surveys, and electronic report distribution. Written reports may precede, accompany, or follow the oral report, and the oral purpose changes accordingly.

### Ethical Responsibilities in Reporting

The researcher-sponsor relationship carries three explicit responsibilities:

- **Sponsor's Right to Quality Research**: use visualization and reporting techniques appropriate to the data, present results objectively to minimize false conclusions, and maximize the value of the resources spent. Quality does not mean producing unnecessary reports, handouts, or decorative materials.
- **Absence of Coercion**: resist requests to falsify presentations, omit findings that conflict with a preferred objective, or make recommendations beyond the study's scope. Explain the researcher's role in finding and interpreting facts, the sponsor's role in deciding, and the future cost of distorting evidence.
- **Sponsor's Right to Findings Nondisclosure**: control the safety and distribution of data, reports, displays, recordings, and presentation materials, including copies stored on cloud servers.

Educate the audience about the research purpose and scope before questions push beyond the evidence. Obtain consent before using participant video, and keep clips and quotations anonymous through nonidentifying demographic or psychographic descriptors.

## Key Concepts

- **Audience Effect** is specific, not vague. "Accept the recommendation to improve courier arrangements" is more useful than "understand the research."
- Audience predispositions affect how information is selected, interpreted, and remembered. Reports must anticipate **confirmation bias**, **anchoring bias**, **conformity bias**, **survivorship bias**, and **loss-aversion bias**. The researcher may use or counter these tendencies, but should not manipulate them deceptively.
- The researcher needs business knowledge and intimate sponsor knowledge to interpret findings. This includes organizational culture, employee capability, products and brand, communication processes, technology, finances, and management capacity.
- Findings are not synonymous with the complete analysis file. Selective inclusion makes each presented item appear and function as important support.
- Recommendations can concern managerial action or further research. They should be tied to the evidence and to the sponsor's decision authority.
- Visual learners benefit from images and models; auditory learners from language, examples, stories, and clips; kinesthetic learners from demonstrations and real experiences. A report must accommodate all three.
- Readability is part of validity in practice. An audience cannot use a technically correct report if the language, jargon, or density prevents comprehension.
- A dashboard is a linked visual display of key information; an infographic is a single digital image file. Both force the researcher to identify what is most important.
- Delivery logistics are part of report quality: room layout, lighting, sound, screen placement, browser compatibility, backup equipment, and document distribution affect the audience effect.
- Written-only reports have a real implementation risk: without a researcher present to interpret and advocate, recommendations may be ignored.

## Mental Models

- **The report as a decision bridge**: data are on one side, sponsor action on the other; insight and well-chosen support span the gap.
- **The funnel**: raw data become information, then insight, then an actionable recommendation. Each step narrows volume and increases decision relevance.
- **The audience at the center**: audience analysis determines the desired effect, which determines the structure, content, visual language, and delivery.
- **The report as a tree**: the organizational framework is the trunk and branches; findings, stories, visuals, and demonstrations are leaves that support and make visible the insights.
- **The research story as a decision path**: begin with the action or insight the audience should embrace, map the minimum evidence needed, and remove detours.
- **Three-part persuasion**: ethos makes the messenger credible, logos makes the claim defensible, and pathos makes the decision personally meaningful.
- **One visual, one cognitive job**: every chart, slide, or page should make the relevant comparison, pattern, or implication easier to see.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating the report as a data dump or including every table and graph produced during analysis.
- Planning from the data file without deciding what the audience should think, feel, and do.
- Reusing a report template when its framework does not fit the project's findings or audience.
- Presenting an insight without showing the reasoning and evidence that support it.
- Using logos alone and assuming statistics automatically persuade a skeptical audience.
- Using jargon, long sentences, unexplained constructs, or a negative tone that puts an affected group on the defensive.
- Choosing a graph because it looks sophisticated, using decorative pseudo-3-D, crowding a slide, or combining too many variables for the audience.
- Truncating a scale without warning, using a line graph for unrelated categories, or using a single pie chart to imply change over time.
- Reading a manuscript, memorizing exact wording, or skipping practice and contingency planning.
- Making the oral report a word-for-word duplicate of a written report instead of using each component for its proper role.
- Falsifying a display, omitting conflicting findings, making recommendations beyond the study's scope, or exposing participants through identifiable quotes or video.
- Spending sponsor resources on unnecessary reports, decorative support, or delivery features that do not increase decision value.
- Allowing report files, cloud links, or presentation recordings to circulate beyond authorized recipients.

## Worked Example

### A Branch-Banking Report Built Around the Audience Effect

This compact application combines the chapter's branch-manager audience profile with its City Bank savings-preference findings page and slide.

1. **Analyze the audience.** The audience is branch managers with five to fifteen years of experience, mostly from finance or customer service, comfortable with numbers but not trained in statistical analysis. They have not been kept informed about the study and fear that branch closures may be imminent. Their profile predicts skepticism and a need for reassurance, not a dense methods lecture.
2. **Set the desired audience effect.** The report should bring managers into the information loop, reduce the fear that closures are already decided, and motivate them to use the findings to strengthen branch effectiveness with customers.
3. **Select structure and framework.** Use an oral-plus-written management report. Organize it around the recommendation and its support, with a simple research-brief or problem/solution flow. Keep technical detail in the written report or appendix. Use primacy for the central message and recency for the action managers should take.
4. **Show only decision-relevant findings.** The savings-preference display shows that commercial banks are not the preferred depository in the city. City Bank customers are somewhat more favorable toward bank savings and view them more like government bonds. The resulting insight is that attracting money-market and government-bond savers requires competitive savings rates and credible security of capital.
5. **Choose the support.** A simple comparison bar graph is more suitable than a complex table for the oral report. The written findings page can show the finding, insight, measurement question, and graph; the oral slide contains less text and uses a flow marker to show the current section.
6. **Compile and deliver.** Use professional, positive language and define any unavoidable research term. Explain the chart aloud, leave whitespace around the key comparison, rehearse likely objections, and provide the written detail for later execution.

The example illustrates the difference between reporting what respondents selected and explaining what managers should do with that result. It also shows why the same evidence may need a compact oral form and a more detailed written form.

## Key Takeaways

- Start reporting with the desired audience effect and the management decision, not with the quantity of available data.
- Convert information into actionable insights by combining analysis with knowledge of the sponsor's business context.
- Choose oral-only, written-only, or oral-plus-written structure deliberately; do not treat the written report as the automatic primary product.
- Select the report framework that makes the decision path easiest to follow.
- Map each recommendation to the minimum findings and insights needed to support it.
- Design words, tables, graphs, stories, demonstrations, and clips for data clarity, audience fit, and ethical accuracy.
- Practice the oral report, stage the venue, prepare for questions, and plan backups.
- Preserve the distinction between the researcher's fact-finding role and the sponsor's decision-making role.
- Protect participants, present conflicting evidence honestly, stay within scope, and control confidential materials.
