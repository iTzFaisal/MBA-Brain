# Chapter 8: Experiments

## Core Idea
An experiment intervenes beyond ordinary measurement: the researcher manipulates an independent variable (IV), observes a dependent variable (DV), and evaluates whether the change is plausibly attributable to the treatment. Experiments cannot prove causation with certainty, but careful control, comparison, and replication produce progressively stronger causal inferences.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Causation Evidence**: A causal hypothesis requires (1) **covariation** between A and B, (2) the hypothesized **time order** with A before B, and (3) no other plausible cause of B. Control holds factors other than the IV constant, and random assignment gives each case an equal chance of exposure to each treatment level. These conditions support a probability judgment, not deductive certainty.
- **Mill's Method of Agreement and Method of Difference**: The method of agreement looks for the factor present whenever an outcome occurs and rules out factors that vary inconsistently. The method of difference compares cases in which the outcome is present and absent, asking whether the suspected factor is present only when the outcome occurs. Both help eliminate inadequate explanations, but an unstudied variable can always remain.
- **Symmetrical, Reciprocal, and Asymmetrical Relationships**: Symmetrical variables move together without either causing the other; reciprocal variables mutually reinforce one another; asymmetrical relationships hypothesize that an IV produces change in a DV. When direction is unclear, identify the less alterable variable and the variable that occurs first. Asymmetrical forms include **stimulus-response**, **property-disposition**, **disposition-behavior**, and **property-behavior**.
- **Seven Steps to Conduct an Experiment**: (1) select relevant variables, (2) specify treatment levels, (3) control the experimental environment, (4) choose the experimental design, (5) select and assign participants, (6) pilot test, revise, and test again, and (7) analyze the data. Each step protects the causal comparison rather than merely producing observations.
- **Treatment Levels, Controls, and Assignment**: Treatment levels should be simple, meaningful, and far enough apart to produce an observable difference. A control group receives no experimental treatment or a comparison level. Random assignment makes groups comparable but does not make a nonprobability sample representative. If random assignment is impossible, use matching on characteristics related to the IV or DV, often with a quota matrix; combine matching, randomization, and a larger sample when necessary.
- **Internal and External Validity**: **Internal validity** asks whether X, rather than an extraneous factor, caused the observed change. **External validity** asks whether the relationship generalizes across persons, settings, and times. Seek internal validity first, then make conditions as similar as possible to the intended application without sacrificing the causal control.
- **Experimental Research Designs**:
  - **Preexperiments**: the after-only design `X O` lacks a baseline and control; the one-group pretest-posttest design `O X O` measures change but leaves history and other threats; the static group comparison `X O1 / O2` adds a comparison group but cannot establish equivalence.
  - **True experiments**: the pretest-posttest control group design `R O1 X O2 / R O3 O4` uses random assignment and estimates the treatment as `(O2 - O1) - (O4 - O3)`. The posttest-only control group design `R X O1 / R O2` omits the pretest when randomization is sufficient and compares the posttests.
  - **Field or quasi-experiments**: the nonequivalent control group design `O1 X O2 / O3 O4` uses intact groups or a weaker self-selected experimental group; the separate sample pretest-posttest design measures different randomized samples when exposure cannot be controlled; and the group time series design repeats measurements before and after treatment, using participants or records as their own controls. These designs trade control for natural conditions and usually require attention to history.

## Key Concepts
- **Causal hypothesis**: A proposed claim that changing an IV will change a DV.
- **Experimental treatment**: The manipulation of the IV presented to a treatment group.
- **Control**: Holding non-IV factors constant or preventing them from being confounded with the treatment.
- **Random assignment**: Placing cases into treatment groups by chance to improve equivalence.
- **Matching**: Constructing comparable groups on characteristics related to treatment or outcome when randomization is unavailable.
- **Internal validity threat**: A factor that makes the treatment an uncertain explanation of the observed change.
- **Seven core internal threats**: history, maturation, testing, instrumentation, selection, regression toward the mean, and experimental mortality.
- **Additional internal threats**: diffusion/imitation, compensatory equalization, compensatory rivalry, resentful demoralization, and local history.
- **External-validity threats**: testing reactivity, interaction of selection and X, and other reactive factors from the experimental setting or participants' awareness.
- **Blind/double blind**: The participant, or both participant and experimenter, respectively, does not know treatment status.
- **Replication**: Repeating an experiment across participant groups and conditions to estimate an average effect.

## Mental Models
- **Causal claim checklist**: Ask "Do A and B covary? Does A precede B? What else could explain B?" Then check control and assignment.
- **Validity before generality**: First make the comparison internally credible; then test whether the result survives changes in people, place, and time.
- **Design-strength continuum**: Use the strongest design feasible. A preexperiment is a last resort; a quasi-experiment is a practical compromise; random assignment creates the clearest comparison.
- **Control without irrelevance**: A perfectly controlled but artificial result may not travel. Preserve enough similarity to the real setting to support external validity.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating correlation or covariation alone as proof of causation while ignoring time order or alternative causes.
- Comparing treatment and control groups without random assignment, matching, or evidence that they are equivalent.
- Using an after-only or one-group pretest-posttest design and making a strong causal claim without naming its threats.
- Choosing treatment levels that are too close to produce a meaningful difference, or changing presenter, room, timing, instructions, or instruments across groups.
- Selecting extreme scorers and interpreting movement toward the mean as treatment impact.
- Allowing groups to exchange information, giving the control group compensating benefits, or assigning whole groups to sessions with uncontrolled local histories.
- Generalizing from volunteers, students, or a laboratory setting to a different population without replication and a selection/reactivity analysis.
- Exposing participants to avoidable harm, invading privacy, or withholding debriefing when field deception or distress is involved.

## Worked Example
**Placement of a benefits module in a sales presentation.** The research question is whether placing product benefits at the beginning of a 12-minute sales message improves retention of product knowledge compared with placing the module at the conclusion. The IV is module placement; the DV is a post-presentation knowledge measure. The two treatment levels should be meaningfully separated, with the module positioned so its midpoint is equally distant from the end of the introduction and the end of the conclusion, rather than choosing arbitrary nearby minutes.

A practical design uses three randomly assigned groups: benefits early, benefits late, and a control condition. Use decision-capable buyers from the target market rather than convenient undergraduates when the intended population is industrial buyers. Keep the room, time, presenter or recorded script, introduction, instructions, and measurement procedure constant; a blind or double-blind procedure can reduce participant expectations and experimenter influence. Pilot the presentation and knowledge instrument, then compare retention across groups. If random assignment is infeasible, match participants on relevant characteristics such as gender, business experience, and communications training, while recognizing the resulting weaker causal claim.

## Key Takeaways
1. Manipulation of the IV, measurement of the DV, control, and assignment distinguish experimentation from merely observing what exists.
2. A causal conclusion needs covariation, temporal precedence, and elimination of plausible alternatives.
3. Random assignment protects group equivalence; random sampling protects population representation. They solve different problems.
4. Internal validity threats must be managed explicitly; external validity requires attention to testing, selection, setting, and replication.
5. Choose among preexperimental, true, and quasi-experimental designs based on the control actually available, not the design's label.
6. Pilot tests expose confounds, instrument problems, timing issues, and environmental events before the final test.
7. Protect welfare, privacy, confidentiality, and research quality; obtain consent when possible and debrief when concealment is necessary.
