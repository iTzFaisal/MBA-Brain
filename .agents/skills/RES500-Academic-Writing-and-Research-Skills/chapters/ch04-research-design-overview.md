# Chapter 4: Stage 2 - Research Design, An Overview

## Core Idea

Research design is a time-based, procedural blueprint for answering the research question. It links three integrated tasks: deciding who or what will be measured and how they will be accessed (sampling design), deciding how, when, where, and how often variables and relationships will be observed (data collection design), and translating those decisions into tested measurement instruments.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Research design as a three-task architecture**: Use it after Stage 1 has produced investigative questions. Define the sampling design, data collection design, and measurement instrument as connected choices; either sampling or data collection may be planned first. Keep every activity focused on the research question.
  - Sampling asks: Who or what should be measured, and how will that source be recruited or accessed?
  - Data collection asks: How will variables and relationships be measured, and under what timing, setting, and interaction conditions?
  - Instrument development asks: What questions, observation elements, instructions, order, training, and pretests are needed to collect usable data?

- **Dimensions of Data Collection Design**: Classify a proposed study across the named dimensions before choosing a method. The dimensions are objective of the study; researcher ability to manipulate variables; topical scope; measurement emphasis; complexity; method of data collection; research environment; time dimension; and participants' research awareness.
  - **Objective of the study**: A reporting study recasts existing data; a descriptive study asks who, what, where, when, or how much; a causal-explanatory study asks why or how variables are related; a causal-predictive study manipulates an independent variable to predict an effect on a dependent variable while holding other variables constant.
  - **Ability to manipulate variables**: Use an **ex post facto design** when exposure has already occurred and the researcher compares exposed and unexposed cases without influencing the variables. Use an **experiment** when the researcher can manipulate or control variables; this provides the strongest support for a causal hypothesis.
  - **Topical scope**: Use a **statistical study** for breadth, quantitative hypothesis testing, and inferences from a sample. Use a **case study** for substantial contextual depth around one or a few events or conditions, using multiple sources to verify evidence and generate or challenge hypotheses.
  - **Measurement emphasis**: **Qualitative research** interprets meaning, feelings, motives, and language; **quantitative research** measures quantity, frequency, or magnitude and depends heavily on instrument quality. They may be combined.
  - **Complexity**: A **single-methodology design** uses one method. A **multiple-methodology design** combines methods, often as a two-stage design in which qualitative work frames the problem or questions and a quantitative study measures them. Complexity should rise with the cost and risk of the proposed managerial action.
  - **Method of data collection**: A **monitoring study** observes and records behavior, activity, or records without eliciting responses. A **communication study** questions participants through interviews, telephone or web conversations, self-administered instruments, or pre/post treatment instruments.
  - **Research environment**: **Field conditions** preserve natural behavior but may expose participants or researchers to risk; **laboratory conditions** provide staged or confidential control but may alter behavior. A **simulation** is a replica of a system or process, including mathematical, virtual-reality, role-playing, or mystery-shopper simulations.
  - **Time dimension**: A **longitudinal study** repeats measurements over a specified period, using the same people (panel) or different participants in cohort groups. A **cross-sectional study** is a one-time snapshot; it is faster and cheaper but requires care when interpreting remembered or expected behavior.
  - **Participants' research awareness**: Classify whether participants perceive no deviation from routine, perceive a deviation unrelated to the researcher, or perceive a researcher-induced deviation. Awareness can change behavior, as in a mystery-shopper study, so findings should be qualified accordingly.

- **Correlation versus causation**: Use cross-tabulation or correlation to determine whether variables are independent and to estimate relationship strength. Do not infer that one variable causes another merely because they move together; causal claims require manipulation and control or unusually strong alternative evidence.

- **Measurement instrument development**: After design and sampling choices identify the variables, choose a measurement approach, write questions or observation elements, order them, add definitions and instructions, train data collectors, and test the instrument in the same mode in which it will be used. Instruments include questionnaires, interview or group-discussion guides, and observation checklists.

## Key Concepts

- **Investigative question**: A question derived from the research question that specifies what information the study must obtain.
- **Operational definition**: A rule that turns an abstract variable into a measurable condition, such as defining "near" as a home address within two miles of a branch.
- **Reporting study**: A study based largely on existing data that summarizes or recasts what happened in the past.
- **Descriptive study**: A study that measures characteristics, proportions, or associations in a target population.
- **Causal-explanatory study**: A study seeking to explain how one variable produces changes in another.
- **Causal-predictive study**: A study that manipulates an independent variable to predict a dependent-variable effect under controlled conditions.
- **Ex post facto design**: Comparison of cases already exposed, or not exposed, to an independent variable without researcher manipulation.
- **Experiment**: A design in which the researcher manipulates or controls study variables.
- **Statistical study**: A breadth-oriented study that generalizes from sample characteristics when the sample and design support that inference.
- **Case study**: A contextual analysis of one or a few cases, usually drawing on multiple evidence sources.
- **Sampling design**: The decision about the source of measurement and access or recruitment.
- **Data collection design**: Decisions about how, when, how often, and where variables and relationships will be measured.
- **Measurement instrument**: A tool for collecting data, such as a questionnaire, guide, or checklist.
- **Sampling error versus nonsampling error**: Sampling error is the difference between a sample estimate and the population value; nonsampling error comes from other design decisions, including measurement and behavioral effects.

## Mental Models

- **Design is a set of trade-off dials, not a method label.** Start with the decision risk, the information needed, and the research question; then set scope, control, timing, method, and awareness conditions.
- **Correlation is evidence about association, not a causal verdict.** A relationship can support further hypotheses, but it cannot by itself identify the cause.
- **Match certainty to the cost of action.** An inexpensive exploratory decision may tolerate a single qualitative or observational method; a costly investment merits multiple methods and stronger statistical evidence.
- **The instrument is part of the design.** A sound sample and data collection mode cannot rescue ambiguous wording, poor order, missing instructions, or untrained data collectors.

## Anti-patterns

- **Choosing a method before clarifying the research question**: This makes the question fit the available tool instead of making the design fit the decision.
- **Treating correlation as causation**: Cross-tabs can show association or magnitude, but not which variable produces the other.
- **Manipulating an ex post facto variable**: Influencing a variable that is supposed to be observed as already occurring introduces bias.
- **Using an untested instrument**: Wording, order, definitions, instructions, and mode effects can create nonsampling error even when the research design is otherwise appropriate.
- **Ignoring participant awareness or environmental risk**: Research-induced behavior can invalidate conclusions, while unsafe field or laboratory conditions can harm participants or researchers.

## Worked Example

For a bank considering a new branch, the research question is whether distance from an account owner's residence is related to saving activity; an initial hypothesis defines "near" as living within two miles of the branch where the account was opened. Phase 1 is a monitoring and reporting study: existing records are examined over the life of each account and cross-tabulated by home-to-branch distance, account size, gender, and activity such as deposits, withdrawals, CDs, and IRAs. The pattern shows that nearby accounts tend to be larger and more active, but it does not establish that distance causes saving behavior.

Phase 2 is a cross-sectional communication study of savers. A survey adds family life-cycle stage, income, attitudes and motivations toward saving, employment, work location, and access mode. The researcher tests why some distant savers still use the branch: they may have once lived nearby, have an inaccurate address, work nearby, or have responded to an electronic-banking promotion. Evidence supports the first and third explanations for a substantial portion of distant savers. The resulting design is multiple-methodology, quantitative, largely ex post facto, field-based, longitudinal for the records and cross-sectional for the survey. Its record checklist and survey must both be developed and tested before collection.

## Key Takeaways

1. Build the design around the research question and investigative questions, not around a preferred data-collection tool.
2. Sampling, data collection, and instrument development are separate tasks but one integrated plan.
3. Use experiments for the strongest causal support; use ex post facto designs for relationships that cannot ethically or practically be manipulated.
4. Reporting and descriptive studies describe patterns; causal-explanatory and causal-predictive studies address why or what will happen under intervention.
5. Qualitative and quantitative methods answer different questions and can be sequenced or combined.
6. Classify environment, time, collection method, complexity, and participant awareness explicitly because each can change the evidence.
