# Chapter 12: Reporting and Analyzing Cash Flows

## Core Idea
The statement of cash flows explains the change in cash and cash equivalents by classifying actual receipts and payments as operating, investing, or financing. Accrual net income must be reconciled to operating cash, while noncash investing and financing transactions are disclosed separately.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Cash-flow classification test**: Use the transaction's economic purpose, not merely the account name. Operating activities determine net income: customer collections, supplier and employee payments, taxes, interest, dividends received, and trading securities. Investing activities acquire or dispose of long-term assets, nontrading investments, and loan principal. Financing activities change debt principal or equity: issue/repay debt, issue/reissue or repurchase stock, and pay dividends. Under the chapter's U.S. GAAP treatment, interest received/paid and dividends received are operating; debt principal and dividends paid are financing.
- **Cash-equivalent boundary**: Include investments readily convertible to a known cash amount and sufficiently near maturity that interest-rate changes do not materially affect value. Debt securities are generally within three months of maturity. Do not report exchanges between cash and cash equivalents as cash flows; apply the policy consistently.
- **Five-step statement procedure**: (1) Compute ending cash minus beginning cash; (2) compute operating cash using direct or indirect method; (3) compute investing cash; (4) compute financing cash; (5) add the three net flows to beginning cash and prove the ending balance. Report noncash investing/financing in a note or separate schedule.
- **Indirect operating method**: Start with net income. Add back noncash expenses and losses; subtract noncash revenues and gains. Subtract increases in operating current assets and add decreases; add increases in operating current liabilities and subtract decreases. This converts accrual income to cash from operations.
- **Direct operating method**: Convert each operating income-statement item to cash. `Cash from customers = sales + decrease in A/R - increase in A/R`. For inventory, first compute `purchases = COGS + increase in inventory - decrease in inventory`, then `cash paid for inventory = purchases + decrease in A/P - increase in A/P`. Adjust wages, prepaids, accrued liabilities, interest payable, and taxes payable similarly. Direct and indirect methods produce the same net operating cash.
- **Three-stage investing/financing analysis**: For each noncash balance-sheet account, (1) identify the beginning-to-ending change, (2) reconstruct the entries explaining it, and (3) report only the cash effect in the proper section. A plant asset sold at a gain/loss reports the cash proceeds in investing, not the gain/loss; debt retirement reports cash paid in financing, not the accounting gain/loss.
- **Cash-flow decision analysis**: Compare sources and uses across the three sections. Free cash flow is commonly `cash from operations - capital expenditures - debt repayments`; `cash flow on total assets = cash from operations / average total assets`. Low cash coverage of growth, debt, or sales can signal funding pressure even when net income is positive.

## Key Concepts
- **Cash equivalents**: Short-maturity, highly liquid investments included with cash.
- **Operating activities**: Cash flows from transactions that determine net income.
- **Investing activities**: Cash flows from long-term assets, nontrading investments, and loan principal.
- **Financing activities**: Cash flows from debt principal and owners' equity.
- **Net cash inflow/source**: Receipts exceed payments in a section.
- **Net cash outflow/use**: Payments exceed receipts in a section.
- **Indirect method**: Reconciles accrual net income to operating cash.
- **Direct method**: Lists major operating cash receipts and payments.
- **Noncash investing/financing**: Significant investing or financing without an immediate cash receipt/payment.
- **Cash flow on total assets**: Operating cash generated per dollar of average total assets.

## Mental Models
- **Use the purpose test**: Ask whether the transaction runs the business, changes productive assets/investments, or changes debt/equity; classify from that answer.
- **Think of indirect reporting as reversing accruals**: Add back expenses without cash, remove gains without operating cash, and let working-capital changes explain timing.
- **Treat the cash change as a target and check figure**: The three sections must reconcile beginning cash to ending cash; an unexplained difference is an error or an omitted noncash item.
- **Prefer operating cash generated internally over asset sales or new borrowing** when assessing sustainable funding capacity, then investigate why the mix differs from competitors.

## Anti-patterns
- **Classifying by balance-sheet location alone**: A current note receivable from a noncustomer is investing, while a customer note and its collection are operating.
- **Putting a noncash asset purchase in an operating/investing cash line**: Disclose the purchase and financing separately; do not invent an immediate cash flow.
- **Adding gains or subtracting losses in the indirect method**: Nonoperating gains are removed and losses are added because their related cash belongs in investing or financing.
- **Reporting net income as cash from operations**: Accrual timing and noncash charges require the reconciliation.
- **Failing to prove ending cash**: A statement that does not tie to the cash account or cash-equivalent balance is incomplete.

## Worked Example
Genesis reports 2016 net income of $38,000. The indirect operating adjustments are depreciation `+24,000`, loss on plant sale `+6,000`, gain on note retirement `-16,000`, increases in accounts receivable/inventory/prepaids `-20,000 -14,000 -2,000`, decreases in accounts payable/interest payable `-5,000 -1,000`, and an increase in income taxes payable `+10,000`:

```text
Operating: 38,000 + 24,000 + 6,000 - 16,000
           - 20,000 - 14,000 - 2,000 - 5,000 - 1,000 + 10,000 = 20,000
Investing: cash from plant-asset sale                         =  2,000
Financing: stock issued 15,000 - notes retired 18,000
           - dividends 14,000                                  = (17,000)
Net increase in cash: 20,000 + 2,000 - 17,000                 =  5,000
Beginning cash 12,000 + 5,000 = ending cash 17,000
```

The $60,000 plant purchase financed by issuing notes is disclosed as a noncash investing and financing activity, not included in the three cash sections.

## Key Takeaways
1. Define cash and cash equivalents first, then classify every other cash receipt or payment by purpose.
2. Build operating cash from net income with the correct noncash and working-capital signs under the indirect method.
3. Analyze plant assets, debt, equity, and retained earnings with reconstructed entries before reporting their cash effects.
4. Keep noncash investing and financing transactions out of cash totals but disclose them prominently.
5. Reconcile the three section totals to beginning and ending cash before interpreting performance.
6. Compare operating cash, capital spending, debt repayment, and financing sources rather than relying on net income or net cash increase alone.
