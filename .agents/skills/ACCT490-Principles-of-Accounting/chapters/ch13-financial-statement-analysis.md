# Chapter 13: Analyzing and Interpreting Financial Statements

## Core Idea
Financial statement analysis transforms accounting data into evidence for decisions about performance, position, risk, and future prospects. Reliable analysis combines four building blocks, explicit comparison standards, horizontal and vertical tools, ratios, and informed judgment rather than treating any single number as decisive.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Four building blocks**: Use liquidity and efficiency to assess short-term obligations and revenue generation; solvency to assess long-term obligations and financing risk; profitability to assess returns that attract financing; and market prospects to assess expectations reflected in stock prices. The relative emphasis depends on the decision user and can change as evidence develops.
- **Four comparison standards**: Compare with the company's own prior periods (intracompany), direct competitors, industry statistics, and carefully applied rules of thumb. Competitor and industry comparisons are often stronger than an isolated guideline; context, accounting methods, business type, and asset composition still matter.
- **Horizontal analysis**: For two or three periods, compute `dollar change = analysis amount - base amount` and `percent change = dollar change / base amount x 100`. For longer series, use trend analysis: choose a base period equal to 100% and compute `trend percent = analysis amount / base amount x 100`. A negative-to-positive change or a zero base has no meaningful percentage; a positive base falling to zero is a 100% decrease.
- **Vertical/common-size analysis**: Express each balance-sheet item as a percentage of total assets and each income-statement item as a percentage of sales. Use common-size statements or graphics to compare composition across periods, companies, and currencies; percentages reveal structure but not dollar magnitude.
- **Ratio-analysis dashboard**: Organize ratios by the four building blocks. Liquidity/efficiency includes `working capital = current assets - current liabilities`, `current ratio = current assets/current liabilities`, `acid-test = (cash + short-term investments + current receivables)/current liabilities`, A/R turnover `= net sales/average A/R`, inventory turnover `= COGS/average inventory`, days' sales uncollected `= ending A/R/net sales x 365`, days' sales in inventory `= ending inventory/COGS x 365`, and total asset turnover `= net sales/average total assets`. Solvency includes debt ratio `= liabilities/assets`, equity ratio `= equity/assets`, debt-to-equity `= liabilities/equity`, and times interest earned `= income before interest and taxes/interest expense`. Profitability includes profit margin `= net income/net sales`, gross margin, ROA `= net income/average assets`, and ROE `= (net income - preferred dividends)/average common equity`. Market prospects include `PE = market price/EPS` and dividend yield `= annual cash dividend per share/market price`.
- **Sustainable-income framework**: Use a complete income statement to separate continuing operations, unusual and/or infrequent items within continuing operations, discontinued segments, comprehensive income, and EPS. Continuing operations are the primary basis for forecasting; a discontinued segment reports both its current-period operating result and disposal gain/loss, with tax effects shown separately. A principle change is applied retrospectively; a change in depreciation, amortization, or depletion estimate is prospective.
- **Analysis-report procedure**: Report an executive summary, analysis overview, evidential matter, assumptions, key favorable/unfavorable factors, and inferences. Let the user's decision determine relevance, and separate observations from interpretations and forecasts.

## Key Concepts
- **Financial statement analysis**: Systematic use of statements and related data to reduce decision uncertainty.
- **Liquidity**: Ability to meet short-term cash requirements.
- **Efficiency**: Productivity of assets in generating revenue.
- **Solvency**: Long-run ability to generate revenue and meet obligations.
- **Profitability**: Ability to earn an adequate return on invested capital.
- **Market prospects**: Positive or negative expectations reflected in market measures.
- **Horizontal analysis**: Across-time comparison of amounts and changes.
- **Vertical analysis**: Common-size comparison to a base amount.
- **Ratio analysis**: Economically meaningful relation between two quantities.
- **Sustainable income**: Income level most likely to continue into the future.

## Mental Models
- **Use a decision-first dashboard**: Start with the lender, investor, manager, or supplier's question, then choose the building block and ratios that can answer it.
- **Think in joint movements**: A small sales increase plus a small expense decrease can produce a large profit-margin change; never clear both accounts separately without examining their combined effect.
- **Use ratios as signals, not verdicts**: A ratio identifies a condition needing investigation; benchmark it, inspect its numerator and denominator, and test the underlying business explanation.
- **Separate recurring capacity from noise**: Forecast continuing operations and recurring margins, then isolate unusual events, discontinued segments, accounting changes, and market expectations.

## Anti-patterns
- **Calling a single ratio good or bad without a benchmark**: Business model, industry, composition, timing, and accounting methods determine meaning.
- **Relying only on percentage changes**: A large percentage on a tiny base can be immaterial; retain dollar changes and materiality.
- **Treating common-size percentages as dollar performance**: They reveal mix, not scale or absolute growth.
- **Comparing raw ratios across currencies or accounting systems without caution**: Horizontal/vertical percentages help, but recognition and measurement differences can remain.
- **Reporting only favorable metrics**: Selective ratio disclosure can hide deteriorating liquidity, turnover, or profitability and weaken the analysis report.

## Worked Example
Apple's comparative statements show 2014 net sales of $182,795 million versus $170,910 million in 2013: dollar change `$11,885` and percent change `7.0%`. Total assets rose from $207,000 million to $231,839 million, or 12.0%, while liabilities rose 44.1% and equity fell 9.7%. For 2014, profit margin is `$39,510/$182,795 = 21.6%`, total asset turnover is `$182,795/[($207,000 + $231,839)/2] = 0.83`, and ROA is `21.6% x 0.83 = 18.0%`. The evidence points to strong profitability but also asks why financing shifted toward liabilities and assets grew faster than sales.

## Key Takeaways
1. Define the user's decision and select the relevant liquidity, efficiency, solvency, profitability, or market-prospect block.
2. Compare dollar and percentage changes across time before explaining favorable or unfavorable movements.
3. Use total assets and sales as common-size bases for balance sheets and income statements, respectively.
4. Compute ratios with consistent averages, then compare with prior periods, competitors, industry data, and cautious guidelines.
5. Decompose ROA into profit margin and total asset turnover to identify the operating driver.
6. Forecast sustainable income from continuing operations and explicitly isolate unusual, discontinued, and accounting-change effects.
7. Make the report's assumptions, evidence, key factors, and inferences visible to the decision maker.
