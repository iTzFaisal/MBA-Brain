# Chapter 16: Macroeconomic Policy in an Open Economy

## Core Idea

Open-economy stabilization has two linked targets: **internal balance** (full employment with stable or reasonable inflation) and **external balance** (current-account equilibrium, simplified as exports equaling imports). **Overall balance** requires both. Trade flows, capital mobility, and the exchange-rate regime change the transmission of fiscal and monetary policy: every intervention has an initial domestic-spending effect and a secondary effect through interest rates, capital flows, exchange rates, and net exports.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Aggregate Demand-Aggregate Supply Model:** Use AD-AS to locate the equilibrium price level and real GDP. `AD = C + I + G + NX`; shifts in consumption, investment, government purchases, or net exports move AD. With unused capacity, expansion mainly raises output; near full employment, it increasingly raises prices.
- **Expenditure Changing Policies:** Fiscal policy changes government spending or taxes; monetary policy changes the money supply and interest rates. Expansionary policy raises total spending; contractionary policy reduces it. Use this framework when the main problem is recession, unemployment, or excess demand.
- **Expenditure Switching Policies:** Redirect demand between domestic output and imports. Devaluation under fixed rates or depreciation under floating/managed rates tends to raise exports and reduce imports, but success requires appropriate magnitude and domestic capacity to supply more output.
- **Direct Controls:** Selective restrictions, including tariffs, quotas, capital-flow controls, and wage/price controls, target particular current-account, capital-account, or inflation problems. They supplement expenditure changing and switching when those tools cannot meet all targets.
- **Effect of Fiscal and Monetary Policy under Fixed Exchange Rates:** With high capital mobility, expansionary fiscal policy is strengthened: higher interest rates attract capital, create appreciation pressure, and force foreign-exchange intervention that expands the money supply and AD. Expansionary monetary policy is weakened: lower interest rates cause capital outflow and depreciation pressure; defending the peg contracts the money supply and offsets the initial stimulus.
- **Effect of Fiscal and Monetary Policy Under Floating Exchange Rates:** Expansionary monetary policy is strengthened because lower interest rates cause capital outflow and depreciation, which improves net exports and adds to AD. Expansionary fiscal policy is weakened because higher interest rates attract capital, appreciation reduces exports and raises imports, and the current-account deterioration offsets part of the stimulus. Managed floats fall between the fixed- and floating-rate conclusions according to intervention intensity.
- **Macroeconomic Stability and the Current Account: Policy Agreement Versus Policy Conflict:** A policy is in agreement when its internal-balance effect also advances external balance; it is in conflict when it solves one target while worsening the other. Match separate instruments to separate objectives when one policy cannot restore both.
- **International Economic Policy Coordination:** Because national policies spill over through goods, services, capital, and labor markets, countries can move along a spectrum from conflict and independence through cooperation and coordination to integration. Coordination is a formal attempt to modify national monetary, fiscal, or exchange-rate policies with interdependence in mind.

## Key Concepts

- **Internal balance:** Full employment without problematic inflation.
- **External balance:** No current-account deficit or surplus.
- **Overall balance:** Simultaneous internal and external balance.
- **Capital mobility:** The sensitivity of international investment flows to interest-rate differences; high mobility magnifies exchange-rate feedback.
- **Policy agreement/conflict:** Whether a stabilization action improves or worsens the other macroeconomic target.
- **Demand-pull inflation:** Inflation caused by aggregate demand exceeding output capacity.
- **Inflation with unemployment:** Two independent internal targets requiring opposing AD movements; aggregate-demand policy alone is insufficient.
- **Crowding out:** Deficit spending can raise interest rates and reduce private spending, but foreign capital inflows can limit this effect in an open economy.

## Mental Models

- **Two-effect rule:** Trace every policy twice: first through domestic spending, then through the exchange-rate/current-account channel. Reinforcing effects strengthen the policy; opposing effects weaken it.
- **Regime-policy matrix:** Fixed rates favor fiscal policy and constrain monetary independence; floating rates favor monetary policy and let fiscal stimulus leak through appreciation.
- **Target-instrument matching:** Two targets may require two instruments. With current-account equilibrium, employment, and price stability, monetary/fiscal policy may need direct controls as a third instrument.
- **Coordination as a collective-action problem:** Each country may protect its own target and unintentionally harm others. Agreement can improve joint outcomes, but incentives to act alone, incomplete information, and institutional differences impede it.

## Anti-patterns

- Analyze fiscal or monetary policy as if imports, exports, capital flows, and the exchange-rate regime did not exist.
- Assume expansionary policy always produces its full closed-economy output increase; the secondary effect may reverse part of it.
- Treat fixed, floating, and managed exchange rates as interchangeable.
- Use one aggregate-demand instrument to solve inflation with unemployment or to achieve three independent targets.
- Assume international coordination automatically works; objectives, institutions, election cycles, business cycles, information, central-bank independence, and large financial markets can defeat implementation.

## Worked Example

Suppose Canada has a recession and a current-account deficit, with high capital mobility and a floating exchange rate. Expansionary monetary policy increases the money supply, lowers the domestic interest rate, and raises consumption and investment. Investors shift funds abroad, the Canadian dollar depreciates, exports rise, and imports fall. Net exports therefore provide a second increase in AD, so the policy raises output and also reduces the current-account deficit: policy agreement.

Change the case to inflation plus a current-account deficit. Contractionary monetary policy raises the interest rate and appreciates the dollar. Appreciation lowers exports and raises imports, making the deficit worse even as inflation is restrained: policy conflict. A policy combination is needed, such as monetary contraction for prices plus a suitable expenditure-switching or direct-control measure for the external account. Under a fixed exchange rate, the same high-capital-mobility logic reverses the instrument ranking: fiscal expansion receives an intervention-driven monetary boost, while monetary expansion is partly neutralized by reserve flows.

## Key Takeaways

1. Open-economy policy must pursue internal and external balance together.
2. The exchange-rate regime and capital mobility determine which policy is powerful.
3. Fiscal policy is strengthened by fixed rates; monetary policy is strengthened by floating rates.
4. Policy agreement is possible, but conflicting targets require multiple instruments.
5. International coordination can internalize spillovers, yet practical gains are uncertain.
