# Chapter 17: International Banking: Reserves, Debt, and Risk

## Core Idea
International reserves let a country bridge temporary gaps between external receipts and payments while adjustment occurs. International banking extends that bridge through lending, but adds credit, political, and exchange-rate risks; sustainable solutions must preserve both debtor adjustment and creditor confidence.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Demand for International Reserves**
  - When to use: Estimate how much reserve liquidity a country or monetary system needs.
  - How: Start with the value of international transactions and the size and duration of possible balance-of-payments disequilibrium. Increase the estimate for high prices/income, slow adjustment, rigid exchange rates, and weak policy coordination; reduce it for flexible rates, effective automatic adjustment, credible corrective policy, and coordinated policy.
- **Supply of International Reserves**
  - When to use: Identify how a deficit country can finance external payments.
  - How: Separate **owned reserves** (foreign currencies, gold, SDRs) from **borrowed reserves** (IMF facilities, official credit, or central-bank/commercial-bank arrangements). Borrowing buys time; it does not remove the need for adjustment or owned reserves.
- **International Lending Risk**
  - When to use: Price or approve an overseas loan.
  - How: Test three layers: **credit risk** (default on principal or interest), **country risk** (political action that obstructs repayment), and **currency risk** (depreciation, appreciation, or exchange controls). A forward market may hedge currency exposure, but many developing-country currencies lack usable hedges.
- **Debt Service/Export Ratio**
  - When to use: Judge whether external debt can be serviced in the short run.
  - How: `debt service/export ratio = scheduled interest and principal payments / export earnings`. Holding other factors constant, higher interest raises the ratio and faster export growth lowers it. A practical rule is: debt burden rises when `interest rate on debt > growth rate of exports`.
- **Debt Reduction versus Debt Forgiveness**
  - When to use: Evaluate restructuring proposals for an overleveraged sovereign.
  - How: Debt reduction changes terms, timing, or financing to ease service; forgiveness reduces the contractual value of the obligation through markdowns or write-offs. Ask whether released foreign exchange will fund productive investment and future repayment capacity.

## Key Concepts
- **International reserves**: Assets used to settle external payment deficits and support exchange-market intervention.
- **Reserve currency**: A national currency widely accepted and held for international payments; the dollar became dominant after World War II.
- **Gold exchange standard**: A fixed-rate system economizing on gold by making the dollar the principal convertible reserve currency and pegging other currencies to it.
- **Demonetization of gold**: The removal of gold's official monetary role, completed through the end of official pricing and IMF use.
- **Special drawing rights (SDRs)**: IMF-created reserve assets and a claim on members' freely usable currencies, not an ordinary international currency.
- **IMF drawings**: Temporary foreign-currency access obtained when a member purchases currency from the IMF with its own currency and later reverses the transaction.
- **Conditionality**: IMF requirements that a borrower adopt corrective programs, often including austerity, import restraint, and export expansion.
- **Debt rescheduling**: Stretching out repayment, usually at the cost of continued interest on the outstanding balance.
- **Debt/equity swap**: Exchanging discounted sovereign debt for local currency used to acquire an ownership stake in a debtor-country enterprise.
- **Eurodollar market**: An international intermediary market for dollar (and other foreign-currency) deposits held outside the United States, including at foreign branches of U.S. banks.

## Mental Models
- **Reserves as an insurance bridge**: Reserves smooth timing mismatches; they should finance temporary disequilibrium, not disguise a persistent deficit.
- **Flexibility-reserve tradeoff**: The more an exchange rate can adjust and the faster other mechanisms work, the less official reserve stock is needed. Rigid pegs exchange flexibility for intervention needs.
- **Risk stack**: An international loan is not merely a borrower credit assessment. Default exposure is compounded by sovereign politics and the currency/payment channel.
- **Debt sustainability loop**: High interest or weak export growth raises service pressure; restructuring can restore breathing room, but only adjustment and productive growth create durable repayment capacity.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating reserves as a permanent cure**: Limited stocks postpone, rather than eliminate, the need to correct a payments deficit.
- **Defending a rigid peg without adequate reserves**: A demand shock can force costly intervention or abandonment of the peg.
- **Assessing only credit risk**: A solvent borrower can still be blocked by expropriation, capital controls, or currency depreciation.
- **Assuming debt relief automatically creates growth**: Forgiven foreign exchange may be consumed or misallocated instead of invested in export-generating activity.
- **Assuming debt/equity swaps eliminate risk**: Local equity can be as risky as the original loan, and the bank may lack the expertise to manage it.

## Worked Example
Suppose Brazil owes Manufacturers Hanover Trust $1 billion. The bank selects $115 million of Brazilian-government-guaranteed loans and sells them through a broker at a central-bank debt auction, where they are valued at an average 87 cents per dollar. The central bank exchanges the loans for $100 million in Brazilian cruzados and retires the debt. The bank uses the cruzados to buy 12% of Companhia Suzano del Papel e Celulose; the company uses the funds to expand capacity and exports. The transaction converts a discounted, hard-to-service claim into local equity and may improve export capacity, but the bank still bears political, operating, and asset-value risk.

## Key Takeaways
1. Reserve demand is driven by transaction volume and the size/duration of external disequilibrium.
2. Floating or more flexible rates reduce the reserve stock required for stabilization; policy coordination and rapid adjustment reduce it further.
3. Reserve supply combines owned assets with temporary borrowing through IMF, official, and central-bank facilities.
4. International loans must be screened for credit, country, and currency risk together.
5. Export growth relative to the interest burden is central to debt sustainability.
6. Rescheduling, exposure reduction, debt reduction, and forgiveness distribute adjustment costs differently; none substitutes for credible growth and stabilization.
7. Eurodollar intermediation lowers regulatory costs and connects regional capital markets, but does not erase international financial risk.

## Connects To
- **Balance-of-payments adjustment**: Reserves finance the interval before price, income, interest-rate, monetary, or policy adjustments restore equilibrium.
- **Exchange-rate regimes**: Fixed, banded, and floating regimes imply different intervention and reserve requirements.
- **International monetary institutions**: The IMF, SDRs, and swap arrangements address liquidity, confidence, and crisis-financing problems.
- **Foreign-exchange risk management**: Currency denomination, forward hedging, and exchange controls determine the repayment value of cross-border loans.
