# Chapter 3: Sources of Comparative Advantage

## Core Idea

Trade begins with differences in pre-trade relative product prices. Those price differences arise from technology, relative factor endowments, and demand, then are reshaped by economies of scale, clustering, government policy, innovation, and transportation costs. Comparative advantage is therefore not only an inherited condition; it can evolve.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Factor-Endowments Theory (Heckscher-Ohlin)**: Use when countries differ in relative supplies of capital, labor, land, or skills. A country exports the good intensive in its relatively abundant, cheap factor and imports the good intensive in its relatively scarce, expensive factor. Compare ratios, not absolute quantities: capital/labor = machines/workers.
- **Factor-Price Equalization**: Use to trace how trade changes resource returns. Product-price convergence raises demand and prices for each country's abundant factor and reduces demand for its scarce factor, tending to equalize wages and capital returns. Treat this as a tendency, not a full prediction, when technology, human capital, barriers, or transport costs differ.
- **Stolper-Samuelson Theorem and Magnification Effect**: Use to identify domestic winners and losers. A higher product price raises the income of factors used intensively in that product; the abundant factor gains from exporting, while the scarce factor loses. Factor-price changes can exceed the associated product-price changes.
- **Specific-Factors Theory**: Use for short-run distributional analysis, when capital, land, or occupational skills cannot move easily between industries. Factors specific to expanding export industries gain; factors specific to import-competing industries lose, while mobile labor can relocate.
- **Increasing-Returns Trade Theory and the Home Market Effect**: Use when internal or external economies of scale matter. A larger home demand supports longer production runs and lower average cost; firms and clusters tend to locate near large markets, though this can marginalize small or remote markets.
- **Theory of Overlapping Demands (Linder)**: Use mainly for manufactured goods. Firms first serve a substantial domestic market, then export to countries whose income levels and consumer demand overlap with the home market.
- **Product Life Cycle Theory**: Use for dynamic manufactured-product trade. The sequence is introduction, export strength, foreign production, loss of competitive advantage, and import competition. Innovation starts the cycle; standardization and technology diffusion move production toward lower-cost locations.
- **Dynamic Comparative Advantage and Industrial Policy**: Use when a country seeks to create advantage in a high-growth or "sunrise" industry by mobilizing skills, capital, and technology. Industrial policy may use R&D subsidies, tax incentives, loans, protection, or coordination, but risks political favoritism, targeting losers, and beggar-thy-neighbor protectionism.

## Key Concepts

- **Relative factor abundance**: The relevant comparison is a resource-endowment ratio across countries, not the absolute stock of a resource.
- **Factor intensity**: A product is capital-, labor-, land-, or skill-intensive according to the factor mix used in production.
- **Human capital**: Education, training, and experience; recognizing it as capital helps explain why skill-intensive exports can resolve the **Leontief paradox**.
- **Inter-industry trade**: Different industries are exchanged, reflecting different factor requirements and conventional comparative advantage.
- **Intra-industry trade**: Similar or differentiated products flow in both directions, supported by variety, competition, specialization in product versions, scale economies, transport, or seasonality.
- **Internal economies of scale**: A firm's average cost falls as its own output expands, often because fixed setup costs and specialization are spread over more units.
- **External economies of scale**: A firm's average cost falls as the industry's output or geographic concentration expands, through skilled labor pools, knowledge spillovers, suppliers, or shared infrastructure.
- **Regulatory competitiveness**: Safety and environmental rules can raise domestic costs and reduce export competitiveness while still producing social benefits.
- **Transportation costs**: Freight, handling, packing, and insurance costs limit trade and specialization.

## Mental Models

- **Abundance-to-export chain**: Relative abundance -> lower factor price -> lower cost of factor-intensive goods -> comparative advantage. A change in wages, skills, capital, or technology can move the entire chain.
- **Trade as embodied factor mobility**: Exporting a labor-intensive good can transmit the economic effect of labor to another country without workers crossing borders. Trade and migration may substitute in the long run, but can complement each other during adjustment.
- **Scale-and-location loop**: Large demand or an industry cluster supports longer runs, specialized suppliers, knowledge sharing, and lower average cost; lower cost attracts still more demand and production.
- **Moving-target advantage**: Innovation creates an advantage, diffusion erodes it, and continued innovation renews it. Do not treat today's export pattern as permanent.

## Anti-patterns

- **Using absolute endowments**: A country can possess more of both factors yet be relatively abundant in only one; use ratios.
- **Treating national gains as universal gains**: Free trade can raise total welfare while reducing the real income of scarce or industry-specific factors.
- **Forcing every trade flow into Heckscher-Ohlin**: Similar wealthy countries trade heavily in differentiated goods; scale, demand overlap, imperfect competition, and product variety matter.
- **Assuming equalization is complete**: Technology gaps, different human capital, trade barriers, and transport costs stop product and factor prices from fully converging.
- **Assuming protection creates durable advantage**: Subsidies or regulations may alter costs, but government may choose politically powerful industries rather than productive ones.

## Worked Example

Suppose the United States has 100 machines and 200 workers, while China has 20 machines and 1,000 workers. The capital/labor ratios are 0.50 and 0.02 machines per worker. The United States is therefore relatively capital abundant; China is relatively labor abundant. If aircraft are capital intensive and textiles are labor intensive, capital is cheaper in the United States and labor is cheaper in China. The United States has the lower pre-trade relative price of aircraft and exports aircraft; China exports textiles. Trade moves each country toward specialization until their relative product prices meet at the terms of trade. Consumption can then lie beyond each country's autarky consumption point, producing gains from trade. The distributional result follows the same logic: U.S. capital gains as aircraft demand expands, while relatively scarce U.S. labor loses; China's abundant labor gains and its scarce capital loses.

## Key Takeaways

1. Relative pre-trade product prices are the immediate basis of trade; technology, resources, and demand are the core underlying sources.
2. Heckscher-Ohlin predicts exports from relative abundance and product factor intensity, not from absolute resource totals.
3. Trade changes factor returns, so countrywide gains coexist with persistent political conflict over distribution.
4. Scale economies, overlapping demand, differentiation, and clustering explain much two-way trade among similar industrial countries.
5. Product-cycle dynamics make comparative advantage temporary unless innovation keeps ahead of diffusion.
6. Regulations and transportation costs can reduce competitiveness and trade even when their social or logistical benefits are real.
