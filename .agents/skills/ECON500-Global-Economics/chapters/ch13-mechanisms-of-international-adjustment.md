# Chapter 13: Mechanisms of International Adjustment

## Core Idea

International adjustment is the return toward current-account equilibrium after trade or financial flows disrupt it. Under fixed exchange rates, this can occur automatically through prices, income, interest rates, or money-market flows, but automatic adjustment may impose recession or inflation and therefore conflict with domestic policy goals.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Adjustment mechanism / automatic adjustment**: Use this distinction when diagnosing a current-account surplus or deficit. Automatic forces restore equilibrium without a deliberate corrective program; discretionary adjustment uses government policy when automatic forces are too slow, weak, or politically unacceptable.
- **Gold Standard**: Apply the classical model when money is gold or gold-backed, the government maintains an official gold price, and gold can move freely across borders. A current-account surplus brings gold in and expands the money supply; a deficit sends gold out and contracts it.
- **Quantity Theory of Money**: Use `MV = PQ` to connect money and prices. With velocity (`V`) and full-employment output (`Q`) treated as stable, a change in money supply (`M`) produces a proportional change in the price level (`P`).
- **Price adjustment mechanism**: Hume’s chain is `current-account deficit -> gold outflow -> M down -> P down -> exports up/imports down`; for a surplus, every direction reverses. Continue until external equilibrium is restored. The trading partner adjusts in the opposite direction at the same time.
- **Financial flows and interest rate differentials**: Use the capital and financial account schedule to predict asset movements. A higher domestic interest rate relative to the foreign rate causes net financial inflows; a lower rate causes outflows. Investment profitability, tax policy, and political stability shift the schedule rather than causing movement along it.
- **Income adjustment mechanism**: Under fixed exchange rates, a persistent surplus raises national income and imports, while a deficit lowers income and imports. Import changes work toward reversing the original imbalance.
- **Foreign repercussion effect**: Include this feedback whenever one country’s income or spending changes affect its trading partner. The partner’s changed income alters its imports and exports, feeding back into the first country.
- **Monetary approach to the balance-of-payments**: Focus on the gap between desired money holdings and money supplied by the central bank. Excess money demand is met by monetary inflows; excess supply is eliminated through outflows.

## Key Concepts

- **Current-account deficit**: Selected current-account outpayments exceed inpayments, commonly because imports exceed exports.
- **Current-account surplus**: The opposite imbalance, with inpayments exceeding outpayments.
- **International reserves**: Assets used to finance a deficit; their stock and lenders’ willingness limit how long a deficit can persist.
- **Gold standard**: A fixed-rate monetary system tying national money to a stated gold price and permitting gold transfers.
- **Velocity of money (`V`)**: The number of times the average currency unit is spent on final goods in a period.
- **Capital and financial account**: Records international purchases and sales of assets such as stocks, bonds, deposits, real estate, and government securities.
- **Interest-rate differential**: Domestic interest rate minus the foreign rate; its sign predicts net financial inflow or outflow.
- **Foreign repercussion effect**: A cross-border income-and-trade feedback chain that dampens the original disturbance.
- **Monetary approach**: Explains payments imbalances through excess demand for or supply of money.

## Mental Models

- **Balance through linked markets**: Trace a disturbance across the chain `payments -> money -> prices -> trade` rather than treating the current account as isolated.
- **Movement versus shift**: A rate differential moves financial flows along the capital-and-financial-account schedule; profitability, taxes, or political stability shift the schedule.
- **Feedback dampens shocks**: An import increase lowers the importer’s income and raises the exporter’s income, causing subsequent trade changes that partially offset the initial shock.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming a deficit can be financed indefinitely**: Reserves are finite and foreign investors or lenders may stop supplying funds.
- **Treating `MV = PQ` as an unconditional price law**: It is an identity; the classical price result requires stable `V` and full-employment, fixed `Q`.
- **Ignoring sticky prices and unemployment**: When prices and wages resist downward movement, a falling money supply may reduce output and employment instead of restoring competitiveness through lower prices.
- **Reading every financial-flow change as an interest-rate effect**: Profitability, tax treatment, and political stability can shift flows at every differential.
- **Optimizing external balance without considering domestic costs**: Income adjustment may require accepting recession in a deficit country or inflation in a surplus country, a politically difficult trade-off.

## Worked Example

Start with current-account equilibrium between the United States and Canada. A change in preferences causes an autonomous increase in U.S. imports from Canada. The United States initially loses income because imports replace domestic production, while Canada gains income from higher exports. The U.S. income decline reduces later U.S. imports, cushioning Canada’s income gain; Canada’s higher income increases Canadian imports, including goods from the United States, cushioning the U.S. income loss. Repeated rounds of lower U.S. imports and higher Canadian imports dampen the original imbalance. The effect is stronger when both countries are major trading partners and weaker when one country is small relative to the other.

## Key Takeaways

1. Current-account adjustment can be automatic or discretionary; fixed exchange rates do not eliminate the need for adjustment.
2. Hume’s classical mechanism links reserve flows to money, prices, competitiveness, and trade.
3. The price mechanism is fragile when central banks offset reserve changes, output is below full employment, prices are downwardly inflexible, or velocity changes.
4. Interest-rate differentials primarily move financial assets, while non-interest conditions shift the underlying flow schedule.
5. Keynesian income adjustment changes imports through national income and must include foreign repercussions.
6. Automatic adjustment can conflict with full employment and price stability, making it politically unacceptable.
7. The monetary approach asks whether the public’s desired money holdings match the central bank’s supply.
