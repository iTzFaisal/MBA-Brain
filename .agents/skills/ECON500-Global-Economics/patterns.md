# Analytical Patterns

## Opportunity-Cost Specialization
**When to use**: Determine trade direction or test whether exchange can benefit both countries.
**How**: Compute each country's opportunity cost from the production frontier or schedule; export the good with the lower relative cost; check that the terms of trade lie between the two domestic cost ratios.
**Trade-offs**: Specialization raises joint output, but increasing costs, nontraded goods, and adjustment frictions usually prevent complete specialization.

## Factor-Endowment Distribution Test
**When to use**: Explain trade patterns and domestic winners and losers.
**How**: Compare factor ratios, identify each good's intensive factor, then apply Heckscher-Ohlin and Stolper-Samuelson. Use Specific-Factors Theory when mobility is limited in the short run.
**Trade-offs**: National gains can coexist with losses for scarce or industry-specific factors; technology and scale can overturn a simple endowment prediction.

## Tariff Welfare Decomposition
**When to use**: Evaluate an import tariff rather than merely count protected output.
**How**: Identify price, production, consumption, imports, government revenue, producer transfer, consumer loss, and deadweight-loss areas. Use the small-nation model unless the importer can move the world price.
**Trade-offs**: A large country may gain terms of trade, but retaliation and the two efficiency losses can erase it.

## Effective Protection Calculation
**When to use**: A finished good and its imported inputs face different duties.
**How**: Compute `e = (n - a*b)/(1-a)`, where `n` is the output tariff, `a` is the input share, and `b` is the input tariff. Judge the incentive using domestic value added, not the nominal final-good rate.
**Trade-offs**: Escalation can protect domestic processing while taxing downstream firms and blocking developing-country upgrading.

## Quota-Rent Trace
**When to use**: Compare a quota, tariff-rate quota, or export restraint.
**How**: Establish whether the restriction binds; calculate rent as `(domestic price - world price) x quota`; then identify whether licenses, foreign exporters, or government capture it.
**Trade-offs**: A tariff adjusts through import volume and normally raises revenue; a quota fixes volume, suppresses competition, and becomes more restrictive as demand grows.

## Remedy-Matching Test
**When to use**: Select a trade remedy or judge a protection claim.
**How**: Match fairly traded import surges to safeguards, foreign subsidies to countervailing duties, defined dumping plus injury to antidumping duties, and foreign barriers to Section 301. Follow the supply chain before deciding.
**Trade-offs**: Remedies can address injury or bargaining leverage, but they also raise input costs, protect inefficient firms, and invite retaliation.

## Development Strategy Test
**When to use**: Compare import substitution, export-led growth, commodity stabilization, or FDI.
**How**: Ask whether policy builds skills, scale, technology, infrastructure, institutions, and diversification; require a credible exit from protection; test cartel or buffer-stock enforcement incentives.
**Trade-offs**: Openness amplifies capability but cannot substitute for institutions; stabilization and preferences can reduce volatility yet create fiscal, lobbying, and cheating risks.

## Trade-Creation/Trade-Diversion Test
**When to use**: Evaluate a customs union or preferential bloc.
**How**: Compare lower-cost partner imports replacing domestic production with higher-cost partners replacing cheaper outsiders. Use `net static gain = trade creation - trade diversion`; then add scale, competition, investment, and sovereignty effects.
**Trade-offs**: Deeper integration buys lower border friction but transfers more policy authority and may obstruct multilateral liberalization.

## Export, Licensing, or FDI Choice
**When to use**: Serve established foreign demand.
**How**: Compare centralized production plus transport with local production; license when local capability and market size do not justify ownership; choose FDI when scale, control, tariffs, or variable-cost advantages cover fixed costs.
**Trade-offs**: FDI adds control and technology transfer but creates sunk-cost, political, tax, and currency exposure; low wages matter only after productivity and risk.

## Balance-of-Payments Diagnostic
**When to use**: Interpret an external surplus or deficit.
**How**: Start with double-entry credits and debits; separate goods/services, current, and capital/financial accounts; use `CA deficit = (G-T) + (I-S)`; distinguish the period flow from the accumulated international investment position.
**Trade-offs**: A deficit can fund productive investment, but consumption-financed borrowing, debt service, or confidence loss raises future risk. Import barriers do not fix the saving-investment cause.

## Exchange-Rate Horizon Filter
**When to use**: Explain or forecast a currency movement.
**How**: Short run: compare real returns, expected currency gains, safe-haven demand, and asset positions. Medium run: follow cycles and capital flows. Long run: use inflation, productivity, preferences, trade barriers, and PPP as an anchor.
**Trade-offs**: Expectations can move today's rate and cause overshooting; PPP is useful for long-run orientation, not a reliable short-run signal.

## Exposure and Hedging Choice
**When to use**: Manage a known foreign-currency payable, receivable, or investment.
**How**: State the quote convention; map currency-denominated costs and revenues; buy foreign currency forward for a payable and sell receipts forward for a receivable. Use an option when downside protection is needed without surrendering favorable moves.
**Trade-offs**: Forwards provide certainty but no upside; options preserve choice but require a premium; leverage can make small rate changes catastrophic.

## Depreciation Adjustment Test
**When to use**: Decide whether depreciation or devaluation will improve the trade balance.
**How**: Check pass-through and `|eta_M| + |eta_X| > 1`; allow for the J-curve; then test `B = Y - A`, asking whether output can rise relative to absorption. Add the monetary effect for long-run price consequences.
**Trade-offs**: Low elasticities, fixed contracts, weak capacity, partial pass-through, or excess absorption can turn depreciation into a higher-price rather than real-adjustment policy.

## Regime and Policy Matrix
**When to use**: Choose an exchange-rate regime or stabilization instrument.
**How**: Apply the Impossible Trinity first. Under high capital mobility, fixed rates strengthen fiscal policy and constrain monetary policy; floating rates reverse that ranking. Match separate instruments to internal and external targets.
**Trade-offs**: Pegs buy nominal stability at the cost of reserves and flexibility; floats absorb shocks through currency movements but increase volatility and may complicate inflation control.

## Reserve and Debt Risk Stack
**When to use**: Set reserve needs or approve cross-border lending.
**How**: Estimate reserve demand from transaction volume and imbalance duration; separate owned from borrowed reserves; screen credit, country, and currency risk; monitor `debt service/export ratio` and whether interest exceeds export growth.
**Trade-offs**: Borrowed reserves buy time, not solvency. Rescheduling, reduction, or forgiveness can restore liquidity but cannot replace productive adjustment and credible growth.
