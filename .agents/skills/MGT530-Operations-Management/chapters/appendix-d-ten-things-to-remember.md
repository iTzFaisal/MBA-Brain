# Appendix D: Ten Things to Remember Beyond the Final Exam

## Core Idea

Operations management is a system discipline: choices about process, people, quality, technology, capacity, and ethics reinforce or undermine one another. Use the ten rules below as a final check before committing resources, setting promises, or changing an operating system.

## Frameworks Introduced

1. **Match work organization to the work.** Choose project, job shop, batch, assembly, or continuous processing based on volume, variety, customization, and flow. Treat the choice as an enterprise design decision because it shapes forecasting, layout, equipment, maintenance, purchasing, inventory, scheduling, accounting, and marketing.
2. **Manage the variability tax.** Reduce avoidable variation in demand, supplier deliveries, and production or service rates; where it remains, provide deliberate flexibility, capacity cushion, inventory, or time buffers.
3. **Prepare before performance matters.** Do the homework before interviews, meetings, presentations, conferences, and decisions. Preparation is a low-cost capability that improves both immediate execution and career results.
4. **Enable capable people.** Select the right people, train them, provide tools and support, then give them room to act. Managerial relationships can determine whether the operating system succeeds.
5. **Pursue quality and price together.** Integrate quality into every activity while reducing process and operating costs; do not assume customers will permanently trade one for the other.
6. **Evaluate technology as a system change.** Compare quality, service, and response-time gains with acquisition, integration, update, training, transition, and temporary performance costs.
7. **Treat capacity as strategic.** Match capacity to expected demand and protect critical resources. Both excess capacity and insufficient capacity create costs; capacity decisions can open either the road to success or failure.
8. **Assume strong competitors.** Benchmark and plan as if competitors will make the best decisions. This prevents complacency and exposes weak assumptions about the market.
9. **Make trade-offs explicit.** Most decisions improve one dimension by affecting another. State the alternatives, affected stakeholders, time horizon, and total-system consequences before choosing.
10. **Make ethics operational.** Apply ethical standards to everyday choices, not only crises: sourcing, labor, quality, safety, technology, customer promises, and environmental impact all count.

## Key Concepts

- **Work organization:** the structural form through which work is performed and flows.
- **Variability:** unpredictable or changing demand, supply, processing, or service rates.
- **Flexibility:** the ability to absorb variation without unacceptable cost or delay.
- **Quality:** consistent fitness of output and process performance for customer needs.
- **Capacity:** the resources and capability available to produce or serve.
- **Capacity cushion:** deliberately unused capacity held to absorb uncertainty.
- **Trade-off:** a choice in which improving one objective changes another.
- **Ethical operations:** decisions that respect obligations to people, customers, partners, society, and the environment.

## Mental Models

- **System-design lens:** changing one operating choice changes the requirements of connected functions; optimize the flow, not a department in isolation.
- **Variability tax:** every unmanaged source of variation consumes some combination of inventory, waiting time, overtime, flexibility, or customer goodwill.
- **Capacity road:** undercapacity loses sales and service; overcapacity raises cost; the right capacity is a strategic position, not merely a utilization target.
- **Best-response discipline:** evaluate your plan against a capable competitor and against the full lifecycle of the technology or process you are adopting.

## Anti-patterns

- Selecting a process because it is familiar, then forcing mismatched volume or variety through it.
- Treating variability as unavoidable and buying buffers without first removing its causes.
- Entering important interactions unprepared and relying on improvisation.
- Hiring people, withholding tools or authority, and then blaming them for weak performance.
- Treating inspection as a substitute for designing quality into the work or pursuing the lowest visible price.
- Automating for novelty while ignoring integration, training, maintenance, upgrades, and transition losses.
- Waiting for a capacity crisis or maximizing utilization without a cushion for demand and disruptions.
- Assuming competitors are slow, careless, or less informed.
- Choosing on a single metric while hiding cost, service, quality, risk, or stakeholder effects.
- Treating ethics as a compliance appendix rather than an operating constraint.

## Worked Example

A meal-kit company moves from customized weekly orders to a repeatable assembly process. First, it separates high-volume standard kits from low-volume exceptions instead of forcing both through one line. It smooths supplier deliveries and demand signals, then keeps a modest labor and ingredient cushion for residual variation. Before installing automated packing, it pilots integration, trains operators, measures quality during the transition, and includes update and maintenance costs in the case. Managers give trained employees authority to stop a line for defects. Capacity is set against peak demand and bottleneck time, not average utilization. The business compares its service and cost assumptions with a strong rival, makes the quality-speed-cost trade-off visible, and rejects a cheaper supplier whose labor and food-safety practices violate its ethical standard.

## Key Takeaways

1. Design the operating system around the work and its volume-variety profile.
2. Remove variability first; buffer only what cannot be removed.
3. Prepare, equip, train, and trust people.
4. Build quality in while managing total cost.
5. Treat technology, capacity, competition, trade-offs, and ethics as strategic decisions, not afterthoughts.

## Connects To

- **Process selection and facility layout:** the work-organization choice determines flow, equipment, material handling, and scheduling requirements.
- **Forecasting, aggregate planning, and inventory management:** variability and capacity cushions connect demand uncertainty to buffers and service levels.
- **Job design and workforce management:** selection, training, motivation, support, and empowerment turn resources into reliable performance.
- **Quality management and statistical process control:** quality must be designed and controlled throughout the process, with cost consequences made visible.
- **Product/service design and technology management:** automation and new technology require feasibility, integration, training, and lifecycle analysis.
- **Strategic capacity planning, scheduling, and supply chain management:** capacity, supplier variation, bottlenecks, and customer promises must be coordinated end to end.
- **Competitiveness, decision theory, and ethics:** quality-price positioning, explicit trade-offs, strong-competitor assumptions, and ethical judgment guide sustainable choices.
