# Chapter 4: Product and Service Design

## Core Idea
Product and service design is an operations strategy decision, not an isolated engineering task. Design choices determine customer value, cost, quality, time-to-market, supply-chain requirements, safety, sustainability, and the organization's ability to deliver consistently. Start with customer needs, then test demand, capability, economics, ethics, and life-cycle consequences through a cross-functional process.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Life-cycle assessment (cradle-to-grave assessment)**: Evaluate environmental effects from raw-material extraction through production, transport, use, and end-of-life. Use it before the design is locked to compare energy, emissions, waste, and material choices while retaining economic feasibility.
- **The Three Rs**: Apply the hierarchy **reduce, reuse, recycle**. Use value analysis to remove unnecessary functions, combine parts, simplify specifications, or substitute standard materials; use remanufacturing to replace worn components and resell useful products; design for disassembly and design for recycling so materials can be recovered.
- **Quality function deployment (QFD)**: Convert the "voice of the customer" into technical requirements. Build the house of quality by relating customer requirements (what) to design or process requirements (how), weighting importance, checking competitive performance, exposing technical conflicts, and setting targets. Carry the requirements through components, processes, and the quality plan.
- **Kano model**: Classify attributes as **basic**, **performance**, or **excitement** quality. Satisfy basic requirements first; invest in performance features when customer benefit exceeds cost; seek economically feasible excitement features that customers may not request. Reassess over time because excitement features migrate into performance and then basic expectations.
- **Concurrent engineering**: Bring design, manufacturing, purchasing, materials, marketing, suppliers, and sometimes customers into early simultaneous development. This replaces the "over-the-wall" handoff, reveals feasibility and tooling issues early, and reduces development time, but requires deliberate communication and conflict resolution.
- **Reliability and robust design**: Define reliability under normal operating conditions and distinguish total failure, substandard performance, and unintended response. Improve reliability through component and process design, testing, backups, preventive maintenance, user education, and simpler systems. Use robust design to make performance less sensitive to variation in use or production; set the target where incremental reliability benefit equals incremental cost.
- **Service blueprinting**: Map customer actions, front-stage contact, backstage work, support systems, visibility boundaries, timing, variability, and failure points. Use the map to prevent errors and design recovery responses before launch.

## Key Concepts
- **Design screen**: Ask whether demand exists, whether the organization has the knowledge, capacity, supply chain, and skills to deliver, what quality level fits the market, and whether the risks and economics make sense.
- **Idea generation**: Use customers, employees, suppliers, competitors, research and development, complaints, and legally permissible reverse engineering. Basic research expands knowledge; applied research targets commercial use; development converts results into applications.
- **Manufacturability and serviceability**: A design must be producible or deliverable at an acceptable cost and profit, not merely attractive in concept.
- **Product life cycle management (PLM)**: Manage beginning-of-life design, middle-of-life suppliers, information and warranties, and end-of-life discontinuance, disposal, or recycling.
- **Standardization versus variety**: Standardization lowers cost, training, inventory, and quality variation, but can reduce appeal and freeze an imperfect design too early.
- **Mass customization**: Preserve standardized cores while customizing at the end through delayed differentiation or interchangeable modular design.
- **Product design phases**: Feasibility analysis, product specifications, process specifications, prototype, design review, market test, introduction, and follow-up evaluation.
- **Service package**: Design the physical resources, accompanying goods, explicit core service, and implicit ancillary experience together.

## Mental Models
- **Design is a system boundary decision**: Trace consequences across marketing, finance, operations, suppliers, users, regulators, and disposal before approving a change.
- **Standardize the platform, customize the edge**: Use common parts, modules, or unfinished units to gain volume efficiency while postponing customer-specific choices.
- **Reliability is conditional**: A reliability claim has meaning only with stated load, temperature, humidity, operating procedure, and maintenance conditions.
- **Service design is a contact-variability choice**: Low contact and low variation support standardization; high contact and high variation require customization, flexible capacity, and stronger employee/customer-process design.

## Anti-patterns
- **Over-the-wall design**: Handing a finished design to production creates late feasibility problems, conflict, rework, and slow launch.
- **Creeping featurism**: Adding features without protecting ease of use increases complexity and can reduce customer satisfaction.
- **Efficiency-only service design**: Cutting choices, personal attention, or staffing quality may lower cost while damaging perceived service quality.
- **Premature standardization**: Freezing a design before defects and customer needs are understood makes later improvements expensive and discourages learning.
- **Ignoring safety, ethics, or end-of-life**: Short-term revenue or schedule gains can become injuries, recalls, liability, environmental harm, and reputational loss.

## Worked Example
A paint company redesigns a bulky metal can. Customer research identifies four priorities: easy carrying, controlled pouring, tool-free opening, and a tight seal. QFD translates these into a stable handle, a removable pour spout, a threaded lid, and sealing targets; the team weights the requirements and checks competitor performance. Value analysis asks whether packaging material, parts, or operations can be simplified without losing protection. Concurrent engineering then tests the square package with filling, labeling, stacking, shipping, and store-display capabilities rather than waiting until production. A life-cycle review compares material use, transport efficiency, recyclability, and disposal. Prototype testing checks leakage, handling, label application, and performance under normal use. The launch decision should proceed only if the improved customer value and logistics savings outweigh tooling, process changes, and reliability risks.

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat design as a cross-functional, supply-chain-wide decision tied to strategy.
2. Convert customer wants into measurable requirements, then test feasibility before committing resources.
3. Use QFD and Kano together to prioritize what must work, what should perform better, and what can delight.
4. Design for reliability, robustness, manufacturability, serviceability, safety, and sustainability from the beginning.
5. In services, balance efficiency with customer contact, variability, capacity, employee capability, and perceived quality.

## Connects To
- **Strategy and competitive priorities**: Design determines cost, quality, delivery speed, flexibility, and differentiation.
- **Quality and environmental management**: QFD, reliability, robust design, life-cycle assessment, and the Three Rs feed later quality and sustainability systems.
- **Capacity and waiting lines**: Service contact, demand variability, and non-inventoried output make delivery-system design inseparable from capacity decisions.
- **Lean and enterprise systems**: PLM, component commonality, standardization, and concurrent engineering reduce waste and connect design data to operations.
