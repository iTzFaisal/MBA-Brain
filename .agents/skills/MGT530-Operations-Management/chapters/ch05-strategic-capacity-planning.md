# Chapter 5: Strategic Capacity Planning for Products and Services

## Core Idea
Capacity is the upper limit on the load an operating unit can handle. Strategic capacity planning matches an organization's long-term supply capability with predicted demand by deciding **what kind**, **how much**, and **when** capacity is needed. Too much capacity raises operating costs; too little strains resources, slows delivery, and loses customers. Because capacity choices often require large, difficult-to-reverse investments and long lead times, forecasts, uncertainty, flexibility, supply-chain readiness, and sustainability all matter.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Capacity measurement ladder**: Design capacity is the maximum output under ideal conditions; effective capacity is design capacity less allowances for maintenance, breaks, scheduling, product mix, and similar realities; actual output is what the system really produces.
  - **Efficiency** = actual output / effective capacity.
  - **Utilization** = actual output / design capacity.
  - Use both: high efficiency can conceal weak utilization when effective capacity is far below design capacity.
- **Capacity-planning process**: Forecast future requirements; evaluate current capacity and gaps; identify alternatives; conduct financial analysis; assess qualitative issues; select the best long-term option; implement; monitor results.
- **Capacity strategy choices**: A leading strategy adds capacity before demand; a following strategy waits until demand exceeds capacity; a tracking strategy adds capacity in small increments as demand rises. A capacity cushion is extra capacity above expected demand: `capacity cushion = capacity - expected demand`. More uncertainty generally justifies more cushion, while standardized offerings usually need less.
- **Alternative-development lens**: Build flexibility into facilities and processes, account for product life-cycle stage, take a systems view, plan for capacity arriving in chunks, smooth uneven demand, identify the optimal operating level, and choose expansion timing and size deliberately.
- **Constraint-management cycle**: Identify the most pressing constraint, exploit the constrained operation for maximum benefit, align other activities to support it, evaluate ways to remove or loosen it, and repeat until constraints are acceptable.
- **Alternative-evaluation toolkit**: Use cost-volume analysis, financial analysis, decision theory, waiting-line analysis, and simulation, then add qualitative and public-impact assessment.

## Key Concepts
- **Capacity measure**: Tailor the measure to the operation. For mixed outputs, input-based measures such as machine hours, beds, seats, or floor space may be more stable than units or dollars.
- **Effective-capacity determinants**: Facilities and location; product/service design and mix; process quantity and quality; job design, training, motivation, absenteeism, turnover, and compensation; policy choices such as overtime and shifts; scheduling, materials, quality, and maintenance; suppliers and logistics; and standards, safety, unions, and pollution controls.
- **Capacity requirement calculation**: Sum forecast demand multiplied by standard processing time for each product, then divide by available processing time per capacity unit. Include workdays, shift length, and shifts. Product mix matters, not just revenue forecasts.
- **Service capacity**: Services often must be near customers, cannot be stored, and experience volatile timing and service times. Waiting time becomes a capacity cost. Flexible staffing, temporary workers, outsourcing, pricing, promotions, and discounts can shift or absorb demand.
- **Make-or-buy**: Consider available capacity, expertise, quality control, demand stability and volume, relevant cost savings, unrecoverable fixed costs, flexibility, supplier control, proprietary knowledge, liability, and reputation. Partial outsourcing can preserve flexibility and hedge against supplier failure.
- **Bottleneck**: The lowest-capacity operation in a sequence limits total system output. Increasing capacity elsewhere does not increase system output until the constraint is loosened; once it is, another operation may become the bottleneck.
- **Economies and diseconomies of scale**: Average cost falls as output approaches an optimal rate because fixed costs spread and work standardizes. Beyond the optimum, congestion, complexity, fatigue, breakdowns, bureaucracy, and inflexibility raise unit cost.
- **Cost-volume rules**: With fixed cost `FC`, variable cost per unit `v`, revenue per unit `R`, and volume `Q`: `TC = FC + vQ`; `P = Q(R - v) - FC`; break-even quantity is `Q = FC/(R - v)`; volume for target profit `P` is `Q = (P + FC)/(R - v)`. Compare alternatives at their projected volumes using their profit lines or total costs. With step fixed costs, calculate break-even within each capacity range; a mathematically computed point outside its range is not valid.
- **Evaluation assumptions and limits**: Basic cost-volume analysis assumes one product, all output can be sold, constant unit revenue and variable cost, and fixed costs that are constant or stepwise. Recheck these assumptions when demand is uncertain, products are mixed, or costs change with volume.

## Mental Models
- **The three-question gate**: Before discussing equipment, answer what capability is required, how much is required, and when it must be available.
- **The effective-capacity lid**: Actual output cannot exceed effective capacity. Improve utilization by removing quality, maintenance, training, scheduling, waste, and bottleneck losses, not by celebrating efficiency alone.
- **The system-chain rule**: Capacity is determined by the tightest link and by the readiness of connected suppliers, transporters, distributors, space, labor, and service processes.
- **The uncertainty trade-off**: Lead early to capture growth, scale, or competitors; wait to improve demand matching and utilization. Flexibility and a cushion buy responsiveness but cost money.
- **The contribution-margin rule**: A capacity choice is attractive only when its contribution margin and expected volume cover fixed cost; compare choices at the volume range that will actually occur.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating design capacity as attainable everyday output.
- Reporting efficiency without utilization or without investigating why effective capacity is low.
- Maximizing utilization when demand is absent, creating inventory, variable cost, or waiting-time problems.
- Expanding a nonbottleneck while leaving the system constraint untouched.
- Forecasting aggregate sales without translating product mix and processing times into hours.
- Choosing make or buy on quoted unit price alone while ignoring stranded fixed costs, quality, control, risk, and reputation.
- Assuming smooth capacity increments when equipment or facilities arrive in chunks.
- Using break-even results outside their applicable range or ignoring qualitative, time-value, uncertainty, and community effects.

## Worked Example
For a vehicle-repair department with design capacity of 50 trucks per day, effective capacity of 40, and actual output of 36, efficiency is `36/40 = 90%`, while utilization is `36/50 = 72%`. The result says the team performs well against its realistic limit but the system leaves substantial designed capacity unavailable; maintenance, quality, training, scheduling, and bottleneck work should be investigated. For a new product with fixed cost of $6,000 per month, variable cost of $2, and price of $7, the contribution is $5: break-even is `6,000/5 = 1,200` units; 1,000 units produces a $1,000 loss, and 2,000 produces a $4,000 profit. These conclusions require demand and cost assumptions to hold.

## Key Takeaways
1. Capacity is a strategic supply decision, not merely an equipment count.
2. Forecast demand by product or service, convert it to processing requirements, and compare it with effective capacity and timing.
3. Use design capacity, effective capacity, efficiency, utilization, and cushion together.
4. Resolve the binding constraint before adding capacity elsewhere.
5. Balance early investment, delayed investment, flexibility, cushion, scale, and the cost of being wrong.
6. Evaluate make-or-buy and capacity alternatives with relevant economics, risk, time value, uncertainty, and qualitative consequences.

## Connects To
- **Forecasting and product/service design**: Demand patterns, customization, and processing requirements establish the capacity need.
- **Aggregate planning**: Overtime, subcontracting, inventory, staffing, and demand smoothing handle short-term variation.
- **Supply chain management**: Capacity changes must be feasible for suppliers, warehouses, transporters, and distributors.
- **Lean and quality management**: Waste reduction, reliable equipment, quality, and training raise effective capacity.
- **Waiting-line analysis**: Balances customer waiting costs against the cost of additional service capacity.
- **Financial decision methods**: Payback, present value, and internal rate of return rank investments; decision theory addresses risk and uncertainty; simulation tests what-if scenarios.
