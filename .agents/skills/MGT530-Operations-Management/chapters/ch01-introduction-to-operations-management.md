# Chapter 1: Introduction to Operations Management

## Core Idea
Operations is the core function of every organization: it produces goods and/or provides services. **Operations management** manages the systems or processes that create them. A business seeks an economic match between supply and demand: excess capacity is wasteful and costly, while insufficient capacity creates lost opportunity and customer dissatisfaction. Operations creates supply; marketing and sales shape demand; finance supplies and allocates resources.

Operations and supply chains are inseparable. A **supply chain** is the sequence of organizations, facilities, functions, and activities involved in producing and delivering an offering, from basic suppliers to final customers. Operations transforms land, labor, capital, materials, and information into outputs. Measurement and feedback are compared with standards for control. The purpose is value-added: the difference between input cost and output value or price.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Transformation system**: Inputs -> transformation/conversion process -> goods and/or services, with measurement, feedback, and control. Use it to locate value, variation, and corrective action.
- **Three-function organization model**: Finance secures funds; marketing assesses wants, promotes, and sells; operations produces. They overlap on forecasts, design, schedules, quality, quantity, investment, and lead time. Operations and sales are line functions; other areas support them.
- **Process-management lens**: A process transforms inputs into outputs. Upper-management processes govern; operational processes form the value stream; supporting processes include accounting, HR, and IT. Manage processes to meet demand and control variability.
- **System design versus system operation**: Design sets capacity, location, layout, product/service plans, and equipment as mainly long-term strategic commitments. Operations manages people, inventory, schedules, projects, and quality. Design constrains later cost, space, capacity, and quality.
- **Operations decision set**: Ask what resources are needed, when and where work occurs, how the system should work, and who performs it. Judge cost/budget, quality, and time/schedule together.
- **Decision toolkit**: Physical, schematic, and mathematical **models** simplify reality and support what-if analysis. Check purpose, assumptions, interpretation, and limits; combine quantitative and qualitative judgment, metrics, trade-offs, systems thinking, and Pareto prioritization.
- **Supply-chain management**: Translate customer demand into coordinated customer, design, capacity, processing, inventory, purchasing, supplier, location, and logistics activity. Strategic decisions set network policy; operational decisions manage daily flows.
- **Ethical framework**: Recognize the issue, establish facts and stakeholders, evaluate using utilitarian, rights, fairness, common-good, and virtue principles, select and test the best option, then review consequences.

## Key Concepts
- Goods are tangible; services combine time, location, form, and psychological value. Most offerings are product packages containing both. Goods usually have lower contact and labor content, more uniform inputs, easier productivity measurement, more correction opportunity, more inventory, narrower wage ranges, and greater patentability. Services often reverse these characteristics, cannot be stored, and must be delivered on demand. Both require forecasting, capacity, process, variation, cost/productivity, supply-chain, quality, location, inventory, and scheduling management.
- The four sources of variation are offering variety, structural demand variation such as trends and seasonality, random variation, and assignable variation from defective inputs, poor methods, or faulty equipment. Mean measures the average; standard deviation measures variation around it. Variation creates cost, delays, shortages, quality problems, and dissatisfaction.
- Customization increases labor, skill, time, flexibility, and price while usually lowering volume; standardization supports volume and low unit cost. Align process choice with customization.
- The operations manager is the planner and decision maker ultimately responsible for creating goods or providing services. The role requires product/service, process, financial, project, communication, collaboration, negotiation, and people skills.
- Historical progression: **craft production** used skilled workers and flexible tools for small customized quantities; **mass production** used specialized equipment, interchangeable parts, a moving assembly line, division of labor, and standardized high volume; **lean systems** use skilled teams, low inventory and space, quality at the source, continuous improvement, and empowered problem solving to combine volume with variety. Scientific management added measurement and work-method analysis; human relations added motivation and participation; quantitative models expanded forecasting, inventory, quality, and project decisions. Japanese manufacturers advanced quality, continual improvement, teamwork, empowerment, customer satisfaction, and lean production.
- Current operations address e-business/e-commerce; product, process, and information technology; globalization, tariffs, outsourcing, innovation, quality, risk, cybersecurity, agility, Six Sigma, total quality management, revenue management, and lean production. **Sustainability** uses resources without harming ecological systems supporting present and future human existence and includes social criteria. Ethical issues include safety, accurate statements, warranties, defects, environmental effects, hiring, closures, and workers' rights.
- Problem-solving: identify the question; summarize data and symbols; classify the problem and select a formula/table; solve and label the answer. Inventory buildup = production rate - usage rate (50 - 10 = 40 parts/day). Economic order quantity: `Q = sqrt(2DS/H)`, where `D` = annual demand, `S` = ordering cost, and `H` = annual holding cost per unit. With D = 400, S = $36, H = $2, Q = 120 units.

## Mental Models
- **Chain/tree**: Each link is a customer of the prior link and supplier to the next; failure interrupts downstream flow. The tree reveals hidden tiers and dependencies.
- **Capacity dial**: Match output to demand, then use forecasts, capacity, schedules, inventory, pricing, and flexibility to absorb variation.
- **Whole-system test**: Optimize total organizational output, not a department's local metric.
- **Trade-off lens**: More inventory improves service but raises holding cost; faster transport costs more but reduces lead time; customization raises price and flexibility needs but lowers volume.
- **Pareto focus**: A few issues often account for a disproportionate share of results; address them first.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating operations, finance, and marketing as silos or promising an infeasible lead time.
- Designing capacity around an average while ignoring seasonality, random variation, or assignable causes.
- Treating services as storable goods or judging service productivity without adjusting for case complexity.
- Accepting model output without checking assumptions, qualitative factors, or interpretation.
- Outsourcing for wage savings while ignoring transportation, quality, liability, disruption, inventory, and carbon costs.
- Pursuing efficiency by compromising safety, honesty, fairness, sustainability, or stakeholder responsibilities.

## Worked Example
Wegmans illustrates an integrated goods-service system. Produce may be replenished up to 12 times daily, while local growers can bypass the main warehouse through a farm-to-market arrangement. This shortens flow time and holding exposure but depends on supplier quality and delivery. Departments combine scanner history with promotions, prices, weather, festivals, and local conditions to forecast. A companywide system reports days of supply, balancing stockout risk against excess inventory. Centralized meat processing and vacuum packaging extend shelf life and reduce store labor, while butcher expertise preserves customization and service. Training, quality testing, complaint feedback, efficient lighting, and refrigerated-case sensors connect people, quality, technology, and sustainability. The lesson is to improve the whole supply chain, not one store activity in isolation.

## Key Takeaways
1. Operations converts inputs into value-adding goods and services through managed processes.
2. Supply-demand matching, variation management, and cross-functional coordination are central operating problems.
3. Goods and services differ, but share the same core management disciplines.
4. Good decisions combine models, metrics, trade-off analysis, systems thinking, priorities, and ethics.
5. Supply-chain performance depends on coordinated location, production, distribution, inventory, purchasing, suppliers, and logistics.
6. Technology, globalization, sustainability, risk, and ethical conduct continually reshape operations.

## Connects To
- **Forecasting and capacity planning**: Convert demand into feasible supply and resource requirements.
- **Process analysis and quality**: Diagnose flow, variation, defects, productivity, and improvement opportunities.
- **Inventory and supply-chain decisions**: Balance service, cost, lead time, suppliers, transportation, and resilience.
- **Operations strategy and technology**: Align process choice, customization, agility, lean systems, and competitive priorities.
- **Ethics and sustainability**: Apply stakeholder reasoning to safety, sourcing, environmental impact, and long-run system performance.
