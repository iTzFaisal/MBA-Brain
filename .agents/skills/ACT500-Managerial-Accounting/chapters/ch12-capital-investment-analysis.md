# Chapter 12: Capital Investment Analysis

## Core Idea
Capital investment analysis evaluates long-term fixed-asset commitments using cash flows, accounting returns, present value, risk, and strategic judgment. Average rate of return and payback are useful screens; net present value (NPV) and internal rate of return (IRR) incorporate the time value of money and are stronger value-creation tests.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Average rate of return (ARR)**: `Average annual accounting income / average investment`; use as a simple profitability screen.
- **Cash payback**: Measure time until cumulative cash inflows recover the initial investment; use for liquidity and recovery-risk screening.
- **Net present value**: Discount expected incremental cash inflows at the hurdle rate and subtract the investment; accept when NPV is at least zero.
- **Present value index**: `Total present value / amount invested`; use to rank projects when capital is limited or project sizes differ.
- **Internal rate of return**: Find the rate that makes NPV zero; accept when it meets the minimum required return.
- **Capital rationing**: Screen, evaluate, rank, and fund the best acceptable combination under limited capital.
- **Sensitivity/expected-value analysis**: Change assumptions or probability-weight them to expose uncertainty.

## Key Concepts
- **Hurdle rate**: Minimum desired return.
- **Initial investment**: Upfront cash outflow.
- **Annual net cash inflow**: Incremental cash revenue less incremental cash costs.
- **Residual value**: Cash recovered at the end of useful life.
- **Depreciation**: Noncash expense that affects accounting income but not basic cash flow.
- **Capital rationing**: Allocation of limited funds among competing proposals.

## Mental Models
- ARR measures accounting profitability; payback measures recovery speed; NPV measures value today; IRR measures implied return rate.
- Discounting rewards earlier cash flows, not merely larger total cash flows.
- Equalize project horizons before ranking NPVs.
- Screen, discount, rank, fund: capital budgeting is a staged process.
- A positive expected value is an estimate, not a guarantee.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating ARR as a cash-flow measure.
- Using payback as a complete profitability test.
- Ignoring cash flows after payback.
- Comparing NPVs for unequal lives without adjustment.
- Treating probability-weighted cash flow as certain.
- Ignoring taxes, inflation, currency, leasing, qualitative effects, or ethical assumptions.

## Worked Example
An investment costs `$200,000`, lasts five years, has annual cash inflows of `$70,000`, `$60,000`, `$50,000`, `$40,000`, and `$40,000`, no residual value, and a 10% hurdle rate.

- ARR: average cash inflow is `$52,000`; less `$40,000` annual depreciation gives `$12,000` average income; average investment is `$100,000`, so ARR is `12%`.
- Payback: cumulative inflows reach `$180,000` after year 3; `$20,000 / $40,000` of year 4 is needed, so payback is `3.5 years`.
- NPV: discounted inflows are approximately `$202,900`; NPV is **`$2,900`** and PVI is `1.0145`, so the project passes the 10% test.
- Because NPV at 10% is positive, IRR is above 10%; unequal cash flows require a calculator or spreadsheet for the exact rate.

## Key Takeaways
1. Build cash flows from incremental revenues, savings, costs, taxes, timing, and residual value.
2. Exclude depreciation from basic cash flow but include it in ARR and tax analysis as appropriate.
3. Use NPV as the primary value-creation measure and payback/ARR as screens.
4. Use sensitivity analysis to identify assumptions that determine the recommendation.
5. Combine numerical results with strategic, qualitative, legal, and ethical judgment.
