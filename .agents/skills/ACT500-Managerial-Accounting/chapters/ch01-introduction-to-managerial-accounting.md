# Chapter 1: Introduction to Managerial Accounting

## Core Idea
Managerial accounting supplies information for planning, directing, controlling, improving, and making decisions. It is designed around management's needs, so it may use estimates, segment reports, non-GAAP measures, and as-needed reporting. In manufacturing, it also explains how costs move from materials to work in process, finished goods, and finally cost of goods sold.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Management process**: Use the cycle of planning, directing, controlling, improving, and decision making; use feedback from actual results to revise plans.
- **Management by exception**: Compare actual results with expectations, investigate significant departures, correct root causes, and revise future plans.
- **Cost object and traceability**: Identify what receives the cost first. Trace direct costs when economically useful; allocate indirect costs with a reasonable driver.
- **Manufacturing cost architecture**: Classify product costs as direct materials, direct labor, and factory overhead; use prime costs (DM + DL) and conversion costs (DL + FOH) for analysis.
- **Cost-of-goods-manufactured flow**: Compute materials used, manufacturing costs incurred, COGM, COGS, gross profit, and net income in sequence.
- **Utilization/occupancy rate**: Evaluate service capacity as actual usage divided by available capacity, such as guest-nights divided by room-nights.

## Key Concepts
- **Managerial accounting**: Internal information tailored to operating, planning, and strategic decisions.
- **Financial accounting**: General-purpose reporting for external users, normally prepared under GAAP.
- **Cost object**: Anything for which a cost is measured, such as a product, job, department, customer, or territory.
- **Direct cost**: Economically traceable to a particular cost object.
- **Indirect cost**: Not economically traceable to one cost object and therefore allocated.
- **Product cost**: Manufacturing cost carried in inventory until the related product is sold.
- **Period cost**: Selling or administrative cost expensed in the period incurred.
- **Manufacturing inventories**: Materials, work in process, and finished goods.

## Mental Models
- Directness is relational: a supervisor may be indirect to one guitar but direct to the production department.
- Costs move through states: materials -> WIP -> finished goods -> COGS when sold.
- Management reports are a feedback loop, not a one-time scorecard.
- Service capacity cannot normally be stored, so utilization matters more than raw customer counts.

## Anti-patterns
- Classifying a cost before defining the cost object.
- Treating all traceable items as direct when tracing costs more than the information is worth.
- Calling selling or administrative costs manufacturing costs.
- Expensing unsold product costs immediately.
- Combining materials, WIP, and finished goods into one undifferentiated inventory balance.
- Confusing COGM with COGS or ignoring beginning and ending inventory.

## Worked Example
Legend Guitars reports beginning materials of `$65,000`, purchases of `$100,000`, ending materials of `$35,000`, direct labor of `$110,000`, factory overhead of `$44,000`, beginning WIP of `$30,000`, ending WIP of `$24,000`, beginning finished goods of `$60,000`, ending finished goods of `$62,500`, and sales of `$366,000`.

1. Direct materials used = `$65,000 + $100,000 - $35,000 = $130,000`.
2. Manufacturing costs incurred = `$130,000 + $110,000 + $44,000 = $284,000`.
3. COGM = `$30,000 + $284,000 - $24,000 = $290,000`.
4. COGS = `$60,000 + $290,000 - $62,500 = $287,500`.
5. Gross profit = `$366,000 - $287,500 = $78,500`.
6. After `$35,000` selling and administrative costs, net income is `$43,500`.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start with the decision and cost object, then choose the information and level of detail.
2. Separate direct materials, direct labor, factory overhead, and period costs.
3. Prime and conversion costs overlap in direct labor but answer different questions.
4. Reconcile materials, WIP, and finished goods before interpreting COGS or profit.
5. Investigate meaningful actual-versus-expected differences and correct root causes.
