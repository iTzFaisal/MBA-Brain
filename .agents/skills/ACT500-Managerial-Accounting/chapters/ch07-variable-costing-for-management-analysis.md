# Chapter 7: Variable Costing for Management Analysis

## Core Idea
Absorption costing assigns variable and fixed manufacturing costs to products and inventory; variable costing assigns only variable manufacturing costs to products and expenses fixed manufacturing overhead in the period incurred. The difference in operating income is a timing effect caused by inventory, making variable costing especially useful for short-run decisions, control, and segment analysis while absorption costing is required for GAAP external reporting.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Absorption-costing income statement**: Sales - COGS = gross profit; subtract selling and administrative expenses.
- **Variable-costing income statement**: Sales - variable COGS = manufacturing margin; subtract variable selling costs for contribution margin; subtract all fixed costs for operating income.
- **Income reconciliation**: `Absorption income - Variable income = fixed manufacturing overhead in ending inventory - fixed manufacturing overhead in beginning inventory`.
- **Horizon filter**: Use variable costing for short-run pricing, special orders, control, and segment analysis; include all costs for long-run capacity and pricing decisions.
- **Controllability filter**: Evaluate managers with costs they can influence, separating variable and fixed costs.
- **Segment contribution report**: Compare sales, variable costs, contribution margin, ratio, volume, and mix; analyze avoidable fixed costs separately.

## Key Concepts
- **Absorption costing**: Product cost includes direct materials, direct labor, variable factory overhead, and fixed factory overhead.
- **Variable costing**: Product cost includes only variable manufacturing costs.
- **Manufacturing margin**: Sales less variable cost of goods sold.
- **Contribution margin**: Sales less all variable costs.
- **Controllable cost**: Cost a manager can influence at the relevant level.
- **EBITDA**: Operating income plus depreciation and amortization; a supplemental segment measure, not contribution margin.

## Mental Models
- Inventory stores fixed manufacturing overhead under absorption costing.
- Production is not sales: producing extra units can increase reported absorption income without increasing demand.
- Use a time-horizon filter: short-run incremental economics differs from long-run capacity economics.
- Mix can dominate volume; equal sales dollars may produce different contribution margins.

## Anti-patterns
- Producing excess inventory solely to increase absorption income.
- Treating lower absorption cost per unit as genuine efficiency.
- Ranking products, territories, or salespeople only by revenue.
- Treating allocated fixed costs as avoidable savings after discontinuation.
- Using variable cost as a permanent long-run price.
- Treating EBITDA as identical to contribution margin.

## Worked Example
Frand expects to sell `20,000` units at `$75`, with variable manufacturing cost of `$35`, variable selling cost of `$5`, fixed manufacturing cost of `$400,000`, and fixed selling/administrative cost of `$100,000`.

- If it manufactures and sells `20,000`, absorption income is `$1,500,000 - (20,000 x $55) - $200,000 = $200,000`.
- If it manufactures `25,000` but sells `20,000`, fixed manufacturing cost per unit falls to `$16`; absorption COGS is `$1,020,000`, so absorption income is `$280,000`.
- Variable costing reports the same result in both cases: contribution margin is `$1,500,000 - $700,000 - $100,000 = $700,000`; less `$500,000` fixed costs gives `$200,000`.
- The `$80,000` absorption-income difference equals `5,000 ending units x $16` fixed overhead deferred in inventory, not an operating improvement.

## Key Takeaways
1. Absorption costing is for GAAP external reporting; variable costing is primarily an internal decision tool.
2. Income differences arise from inventory timing, not different total economics over the life of the business.
3. Contribution margin includes variable selling costs as well as variable manufacturing costs.
4. Separate avoidable fixed costs before discontinuing a segment.
5. Service businesses use contribution-margin reporting without inventory or manufacturing margin.
