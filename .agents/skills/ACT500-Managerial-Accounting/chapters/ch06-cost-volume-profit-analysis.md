# Chapter 6: Cost-Volume-Profit Analysis

## Core Idea
Cost-volume-profit (CVP) analysis studies how selling price, volume, variable costs, fixed costs, and sales mix affect operating income. Contribution margin first covers fixed costs; only the remainder becomes operating income. The analysis is useful for break-even, target-profit, pricing, volume, and capacity decisions within a relevant range.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Cost behavior**: Classify costs as variable, fixed, or mixed relative to an activity base and relevant range.
- **High-low method**: Estimate variable cost per unit as change in total cost divided by change in activity; estimate fixed cost from the resulting equation.
- **Contribution-margin format**: Present sales less variable costs as contribution margin, then subtract fixed costs.
- **Break-even/target-profit analysis**: Solve for the volume where contribution margin equals fixed costs, or add target profit to fixed costs.
- **CVP and profit-volume charts**: Use graphs to show loss, break-even, profit, and sensitivity to changed assumptions.
- **Product E/sales mix**: Convert multiple products into a weighted composite product only when the sales mix is stable.
- **Operating leverage and margin of safety**: Use operating leverage to estimate income sensitivity and margin of safety to measure downside buffer.

## Key Concepts
- **Variable cost**: Changes in total with activity but remains constant per unit.
- **Fixed cost**: Remains constant in total within the relevant range but changes per unit.
- **Mixed cost**: Contains both fixed and variable components.
- **Contribution margin**: Sales less variable costs.
- **Contribution margin ratio**: Contribution margin divided by sales.
- **Break-even point**: Volume at which operating income is zero.
- **Margin of safety**: Sales above break-even that can be lost before an operating loss.
- **Operating leverage**: Contribution margin divided by operating income.

## Mental Models
- Contribution margin is a coverage ladder: variable costs -> fixed costs -> profit.
- Variable costs are stable per unit; fixed costs are stable in total.
- Break-even is a threshold, not a forecast.
- High operating leverage amplifies both sales gains and sales declines.
- Unit contribution margin answers unit-volume questions; contribution-margin ratio answers dollar-sales questions.

## Anti-patterns
- Extending cost behavior beyond the relevant range.
- Treating a mixed cost as entirely fixed or variable.
- Using a dollar ratio for a unit change or a unit margin for a dollar change.
- Applying single-product CVP after the sales mix changes.
- Treating high operating leverage as purely favorable.
- Ignoring fixed costs in a zero-sales scenario or using the wrong activity unit for a service business.

## Worked Example
Lambert sells `50,000` units at `$20`, has variable cost of `$12` per unit, and fixed costs of `$300,000`.

| Measure | Calculation | Result |
|---|---:|---:|
| Contribution margin | `50,000 x ($20 - $12)` | `$400,000` |
| Unit contribution margin | `$20 - $12` | `$8` |
| Contribution-margin ratio | `$400,000 / $1,000,000` | `40%` |
| Break-even units | `$300,000 / $8` | `37,500` |
| Break-even sales | `$300,000 / 40%` | `$750,000` |
| Target `$100,000` profit | `($300,000 + $100,000) / $8` | `50,000` units |
| Margin of safety | `$1,000,000 - $750,000` | `$250,000` or `25%` |
| Operating leverage | `$400,000 / $100,000` | `4.0` |

At operating leverage of 4.0, a 10% sales change implies roughly a 40% operating-income change if other assumptions hold.

## Key Takeaways
1. Define the activity base and relevant range before classifying costs.
2. Use contribution margin, not revenue alone, to evaluate volume decisions.
3. Add target profit to fixed costs in the break-even equation.
4. Recompute composite-product margins when sales mix changes.
5. State CVP assumptions explicitly: linearity, stable efficiency, constant mix, and unchanged inventory.
