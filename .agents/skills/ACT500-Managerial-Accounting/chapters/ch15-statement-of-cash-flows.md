# Chapter 15: Statement of Cash Flows

## Core Idea
The statement of cash flows explains changes in cash and cash equivalents through operating, investing, and financing activities. It reconciles accrual-based net income to operating cash flow, reports long-term asset and financing cash flows, discloses noncash investing/financing transactions, and verifies beginning cash plus net change equals ending cash.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three-activity classification**: Operating covers day-to-day transactions; investing covers long-term assets and investments; financing covers debt and equity.
- **Direct method**: Report actual operating cash receipts and payments; use when transparent cash-source information is available.
- **Indirect method**: Start with net income, add noncash expenses, reverse gains/losses, and adjust operating working capital.
- **Cash-flow worksheet**: Analyze comparative balance sheets and classify each noncash-account change, then reconcile totals.
- **Noncash investing/financing schedule**: Disclose stock-for-debt, asset-for-note, and similar transactions outside cash totals.
- **Free cash flow**: `Operating cash flow - cash used to purchase PP&E`; use as an estimate of cash remaining after maintaining operations.

## Key Concepts
- **Cash equivalents**: Short-term, highly liquid investments such as money-market accounts or Treasury bills.
- **Operating cash flow**: Cash generated by ordinary operations.
- **Investing cash flow**: Cash from purchases/sales of long-term assets and investments.
- **Financing cash flow**: Cash from debt and equity changes.
- **Working-capital adjustment**: Indirect-method adjustment for timing between accrual recognition and cash.
- **Quality of earnings**: Degree to which reported income converts into operating cash.

## Mental Models
- Classify by economic purpose, not merely account name.
- Under the indirect method, net income is a starting point, not cash flow.
- An increase in a noncash operating asset usually uses cash; an increase in an operating liability usually provides cash.
- A cash increase can come from borrowing or stock issuance, so inspect operating cash separately.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating net income as operating cash flow.
- Treating depreciation as a cash payment.
- Reporting an asset-sale gain as operating cash rather than removing it and reporting sale proceeds as investing cash.
- Reversing working-capital signs.
- Treating dividends declared as dividends paid without adjusting dividends payable.
- Including stock-for-debt exchanges in cash financing totals.
- Confusing free cash flow with total change in cash.

## Worked Example
Rundell reports net income of `$108,000`, depreciation `$7,000`, a land-sale gain `$12,000`, increases in receivables `$9,000` and accrued expenses `$2,200`, decreases in inventory `$8,000`, accounts payable `$3,200`, and taxes payable `$500`.

Indirect operating cash flow is:

`$108,000 + $7,000 - $12,000 - $9,000 + $8,000 - $3,200 + $2,200 - $500 = $100,500`.

Investing cash flow is `$72,000` land-sale proceeds less `$15,000` land and `$60,000` building purchases = `$3,000` outflow. Financing cash flow is `$48,000` stock issuance less `$50,000` debt retirement and `$24,000` dividends = `$26,000` outflow. Net cash increases `$71,500`; with beginning cash of `$26,000`, ending cash is `$97,500`.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use operating, investing, and financing classification consistently.
2. Direct and indirect methods produce the same operating cash-flow total.
3. Add noncash expenses and reverse gains/losses under the indirect method.
4. Reconcile the statement to the balance sheet's ending cash.
5. Interpret cash flow with purpose, working capital, financing dependence, and earnings quality.
