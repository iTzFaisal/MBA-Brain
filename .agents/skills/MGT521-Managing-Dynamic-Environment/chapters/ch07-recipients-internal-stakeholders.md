# Chapter 7: Managing Recipients of Change and Influencing Internal Stakeholders

## Core Idea

People who receive change are not a uniform "resistant" group. Their responses range from enthusiasm to ambivalence to active or passive opposition, depending on perceived costs and benefits, values, identity, relationships, capability, prior experience, and confidence in the leaders and the change process. Effective change leadership treats reactions as information, makes changes to the psychological contract explicit, gives people time and voice, and aligns leaders' behavior with organizational systems so recipients can become capable, willing implementers and eventually agents of change.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Variable stakeholder response: support, ambivalence, and resistance**
  - **When:** Diagnose an early reaction or a change that is producing mixed signals rather than assuming that every question is opposition.
  - **How:** Ask how the recipient evaluates benefits and costs for self, team, organization, and valued relationships; the relevance to personal values; confidence in the promised results; ability to perform; job security and career prospects; and the fairness of the process and outcome. Questions and concerns usually signal sense-making. Call it resistance only after mixed feelings have been resolved into a firm judgment that the change should be opposed and behavior is slowing, blocking, or undermining it.
  - **Why it matters:** The same formal change can feel minor to one group and identity- or livelihood-threatening to another. Treating all reactions as resistance creates a self-fulfilling prophecy and hides useful design feedback.

- **The Change Path Model: stages of reaction to disruptive change**
  - **When:** A change threatens established roles, relationships, security, competence, or identity and recipients are showing emotional or performance effects.
  - **How:** Expect three broad periods: before the change, with anticipation and anxiety; during implementation, with shock, denial or defensive retreat, bargaining, and possible depression, guilt, or alienation; and after the change, with acknowledgment, adaptation, and new risk-taking. Match support to the stage: reduce uncertainty and rumors before announcement, provide safety, listening, resources, and help with endings during disruption, then reinforce learning and credible opportunities after the change.
  - **Limits:** The sequence is a useful map, not a timetable. People move at different speeds, become stuck, skip unresolved emotions, or regress. Leaders who have already diagnosed the need and accepted the future must not expect recipients to be emotionally synchronized with them.

- **ADKAR adoption sequence**
  - **When:** Individual adoption is stalled even though the organization has announced a change.
  - **How:** Check for **Awareness** of why change is needed, **Desire** to participate and support it, **Knowledge** of what to do and what the future looks like, **Ability** to perform the new behavior, and **Reinforcement** that keeps it in place. Supply the missing condition rather than repeating the same message to everyone. The model is a diagnostic aid, not a substitute for addressing grief, justice, power, or the wider organizational context.

- **The psychological contract**
  - **When:** A change alters responsibilities, autonomy, status, rewards, security, relationships, norms, or what employees believe the organization owes them.
  - **How:** Surface both explicit and implicit parts of the existing employment deal. Explain what will change, what will remain continuous, why the change is necessary, and what support or alternatives are available. Give affected people a meaningful opportunity to question and influence the process, allow time to absorb the implications, and deliver both transactional commitments and relational commitments such as respect, support, and honest treatment. Evaluate procedural justice (voice and fair process) separately from distributive justice (fair outcomes).
  - **Failure mode:** A unilateral change can be resisted even when its objective effect appears positive because it signals loss of control, a hidden agenda, or disregard for the person's understanding of "the deal." One person's altered contract also affects coworkers, managers, and leaders whose own expectations are interdependent.

- **Five audiences for the case for change**
  - **When:** A business-case presentation has not created engagement or people cannot see why the change matters to them.
  - **How:** Explain the change through five linked lenses: the business, society, customers, the working team, and the individuals doing the work. Combine this explanation with informed supervisors, recipient ideas, shared target setting, and small signs of progress. The five lenses do not replace an honest account of risks and costs; they make the rationale personally and relationally intelligible.

- **Credibility and signal alignment**
  - **When:** Trust is fragile, skepticism is rising, or leaders' stated priorities are not translating into behavior.
  - **How:** Make leader words, personal conduct, formal systems, incentives, resource access, performance measures, and informal communication point in the same direction. Use candor, visible presence, empathy, sound logic, and follow-through. Keep room for good-faith disagreement instead of building an echo chamber; candid challenge makes the message more credible and exposes implementation problems early.

- **Continuous improvement and dynamic stability**
  - **When:** The organization wants to reduce the shock of future changes rather than repeatedly force large transformations through a fatigued workforce.
  - **How:** Normalize regular questioning of the status quo, incremental improvement, experimentation, pilots, cross-training, knowledge sharing, and review of both successes and failures. Preserve a coherent purpose, shared values, and identity while allowing structures and processes to adapt. Smaller interrelated changes can accumulate into major renewal without requiring employees to "bet the farm" on one heroic initiative.

## Key Concepts

- **Recipients of change:** People on the receiving end of an initiative who have limited power to alter its direction or content.
- **Ambivalence:** Mixed positive and negative emotions or beliefs generated by uncertainty, competing values, and incompatible implications of a change. It is often a learning window, not resistance.
- **Resistance to change:** Actions intended to slow or prevent change after a person concludes that opposition is warranted; it can range from concern and delay to coalition building, protest, or sabotage.
- **Psychological contract:** The implicit and explicit employment expectations concerning norms, rights, rewards, obligations, security, and how the organization and employee will treat one another.
- **Procedural justice:** The perceived fairness of the decision process, including access to information, opportunity to ask questions, voice concerns, and suggest alternatives.
- **Distributive justice:** The perceived fairness of the resulting allocation of benefits, burdens, opportunities, or losses.
- **Survivor syndrome:** Stress, guilt, reduced loyalty and motivation, uncertainty, and entrapment experienced by people who remain after a poorly handled traumatic change such as downsizing.
- **Predisposition to change:** A general inclination toward innovation, early adoption, cautious adoption, or non-adoption; it is more informative than using age as a proxy for resistance.
- **Tolerance for turbulence and ambiguity:** The level of uncertainty and change a person can handle before stress, fatigue, defensiveness, or boredom rises.
- **Competency or complacency trap:** Reliance on once-successful strategies and competencies after environmental conditions have changed.
- **Confirmation bias:** Seeking and remembering information that supports existing beliefs while rejecting contradictory evidence.
- **Skepticism and cynicism:** Skepticism is doubt about the change, process, leaders, or results; cynicism is a deeper loss of faith that reduces commitment, effort, safety, and willingness to engage in later changes.
- **Self-efficacy and agency:** Belief in one's ability to perform tasks and exert some influence over what happens; participation and experimentation can strengthen both.
- **Organizational agility and resilience:** Agility permits responsive adaptation; resilience preserves common purpose, values, and identity that help the organization adapt deliberately rather than react chaotically.

## Mental Models

- **Question the label, investigate the system.** When someone appears obstructive, ask what is happening, why it is happening, and what it reveals about the design. Misaligned incentives, missing resources, conflicting workflows, or unclear authority often produce behavior labeled as individual resistance.
- **Change is both content and process.** A rationally necessary change can still fail if its introduction is secretive, untimely, unfair, or nonparticipative. Assess the quality of the proposed solution and the quality of the way people are being asked to adopt it.
- **Think head, heart, and hands.** Recipients need an understandable case and vision, space to process loss and emotion, and the knowledge, practice, resources, and supervisory support to perform differently. More facts cannot repair a violated contract or missing capability by themselves.
- **Read the signal stack.** Employees infer priorities from speeches, leader conduct, peers, incentives, systems, staffing, and deadlines. If a leader says "quality first" while the workflow rewards "ship now," the system wins and credibility falls.
- **Preserve continuity while changing.** Connect the future to respected parts of the past, such as purpose, customer commitment, or cooperative identity. Continuity lowers threat without pretending that roles, structures, or expectations will remain unchanged.
- **Move recipients toward agency.** Map dependencies and trusted relationships, then invite recipients to analyze problems, propose options, run bounded experiments, and own implementation. Influence and self-efficacy grow when people can make a visible difference.

## Anti-patterns

- **Assuming resistance is inevitable or age-based:** It turns normal inquiry into opposition and causes leaders to behave defensively; predisposition, impact, values, and experience are better diagnostic variables.
- **Blaming individuals before checking structures:** Existing rewards, systems, information access, staffing, and roles may instruct people to do the opposite of the stated change.
- **Treating ambivalence as an enemy:** Enthusiasts can create groupthink, silence legitimate doubts, and stereotype skeptics. Invite dissent and use it to improve the plan.
- **Announcing a contract change as a surprise:** Unilateral changes to security, status, autonomy, or obligations generate fear and resentment even when leaders believe the outcome is beneficial.
- **Using one-way, late, or indirect communication:** News learned from rumors, media, or a coffee shop creates a credibility vacuum. Employees need timely, direct communication and time to make sense of it.
- **Overpromising or substituting slogans for integrity:** Hope without candor becomes cynicism when actions, compensation, decisions, or results contradict the story. Acknowledge mistakes, accept responsibility, apologize, and make amends.
- **Relying primarily on coercion and fear:** Threats may produce short-term compliance, but they commonly produce withdrawal, turnover, aggression, errors, and underground resistance once the pressure is removed. Set explicit standards and use authority when necessary, but build capability and commitment rather than depending on the stick.
- **Creating an echo chamber:** Suppressing good-faith contrary messages protects the plan from scrutiny, not the organization from risk. Use informal channels and social media feedback to surface and answer concerns.
- **Forcing the leader's pace on recipients:** Leaders may have had months to understand and accept a change. Recipients need time, support, and stage-appropriate help to catch up.
- **Confusing visible morale with real engagement:** Mandatory celebrations, repeated reassurance, or silence about losses do not repair workload, fairness, capability, or trust problems.

## Worked Example

### Monique Leroux and Change at Desjardins

Desjardins was a century-old financial cooperative with more than 500 autonomous member branches, strong rural roots, and thousands of elected officers. When Monique Leroux became CEO in 2008, the organization faced an aging and increasingly urban membership, weak economic conditions, fragmented services, and sophisticated competitors. The required response was not simply a new strategy: it threatened branch autonomy, familiar relationships, established status, and the cooperative identity employees and members valued.

Leroux framed the need to modernize while explicitly affirming the cooperative's core values. She used transparent data and rich communication, listened to employees and members, and used consultative teams to define problems and develop solutions. Consultation was followed by decisions and active reinforcement: the hierarchy was flattened, services and processes were restructured, vice-presidential roles were reduced from 250 to 112 and senior vice-presidents from 40 to 12, and about 1,000 jobs were eliminated. She also built a more diverse leadership team and created new businesses and capabilities.

The approach produced substantial results. By 2012, Desjardins had expanded into new lines of business, grown employment from about 17,000 to 45,000, increased assets from $152 billion to $190 billion, and increased member distributions. Yet the change was not emotionally costless: some managers reported lower morale, turnover, disruption of valued relationships, and a pace that felt too fast; pockets of resistance remained. The lesson is not that engagement eliminates resistance. It is that credible, participative, data-informed change can convert skeptics into partners while still requiring leaders to monitor contract disruption, capacity, pace, and human costs. Success must be institutionalized in systems, processes, collaboration, and accountability, not declared when the business metrics improve.

## Key Takeaways

1. Diagnose the recipient's perceived costs, benefits, values, risks, relationships, capabilities, and prior experiences before labeling a reaction as resistance.
2. Use ambivalence as an early source of information: create psychological safety, listen for risks, and revise the plan while attitudes are still open.
3. Make the psychological contract visible. Explain what is ending, what continues, what support is available, and how both process and outcomes will be fair.
4. Stage communication and support. Reduce uncertainty before the change, provide emotional and practical first aid during disruption, and reinforce capability and hope afterward.
5. Build credibility through integrity: be candid about risks and unknowns, communicate in person when the news is serious, keep promises, acknowledge mistakes, and align actions with words.
6. Use timely, redundant, two-way communication. Track themes in questions, surveys, meetings, and informal channels, and give a specific follow-up time when answers are not yet available.
7. Align systems, incentives, staffing, workflows, and informal signals with the change, then normalize continuous improvement through pilots, learning, and bounded experiments.
