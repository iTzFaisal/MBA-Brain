# Chapter 2: How to Lead Organizational Change: Frameworks

## Core Idea
Organizational change has two distinct questions: **what** must change (the content of the change) and **how** leaders will move people and systems toward it (the process of change). No single model captures the whole problem. Effective change leaders select complementary lenses for system conditions, managerial actions, ethical action, emotional transitions, process design, and institutionalization.

The chapter's organizing framework is the **Deszca/Ingols Change Path Model**: Awakening, Mobilization, Acceleration, and Institutionalization. It provides a practical sequence without eliminating the uncertainty, feedback, politics, and adaptation that make change nonlinear in practice.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Lewin's Three-Stage Model: Unfreeze, Change, Refreeze
**When to use:** Use Lewin's basic system-level model to explain the overall logic of a change, diagnose why an apparently sensible initiative is not moving, or remind participants that the status quo has to become sufficiently fluid before new patterns can take hold.

**How:**

1. **Unfreeze** the existing equilibrium. Surface the assumptions, habits, structures, relationships, and performance evidence that make the current state no longer tenable. Unfreezing must occur at all relevant levels; a general manager may be ready while frontline employees still see no reason to act.
2. **Change** by designing and enacting new systems, roles, skills, procedures, and behaviors. The phase is uncertain and may require participation, sequencing, training, hiring, or technical work.
3. **Refreeze**, or more flexibly **re-gel**, the new state. Establish new routines, reporting relationships, measures, and informal habits until the desired behavior becomes normal and a new relative equilibrium develops.

Lewin is descriptive and intentionally simple. It is valuable because organizations behave like interconnected webs: altering one strand rarely changes the pattern unless related forces are also shifted. Its limitations are equally important. The sequence can imply a linear event and a permanently stable endpoint, even though dynamic organizations need continuous learning and should avoid freezing so firmly that they cannot adapt.

### Kotter's Eight-Stage Organizational Change Process
**When to use:** Use Kotter when a large organizational initiative needs a detailed, managerial roadmap with explicit conditions for progress. It is more prescriptive and granular than Lewin, and it emphasizes organizational alignment, communication, momentum, and culture.

**How:** Work through the stages in order, checking whether the preceding condition is actually present rather than treating activity as completion.

1. **Establish a sense of urgency:** challenge complacency and make threats, opportunities, and vulnerability visible.
2. **Create a guiding coalition:** assemble a substantial, credible group, often around 10 to 50 people, with authority, relevant knowledge, and the respect of colleagues.
3. **Develop a vision and strategy:** define an inspiring future and the broad route for reaching it.
4. **Communicate the change vision:** use multiple channels and repeated communication to reach employees' understanding and commitment.
5. **Empower employees for broad-based action:** remove structural, staffing, system, and resource barriers that block people who support the vision.
6. **Generate short-term wins:** produce and visibly recognize credible gains so motivation survives a change that may take three to five years.
7. **Consolidate gains and produce more change:** continue after early success; use credibility and learning to address deeper parts of the organization.
8. **Anchor new approaches in the culture:** connect the new ways of working to organizational norms and values.

The chapter also notes Kotter's later emphasis on concurrent accelerators: a single major opportunity, a maintained coalition, a flexible network and volunteer army, barrier removal, visible wins, continued learning, and cultural institutionalization. This addition is a useful response to the reality that organizations must keep delivering while changing.

### Gentile's Giving Voice to Values (GVV)
**When to use:** Use **Giving Voice to Values** when a person has identified a values conflict or wrongdoing and needs to act effectively rather than merely recognize that something is wrong. GVV operates primarily at the individual level, but a prepared individual or coalition can alter a unit, organization, or wider system.

**How:** GVV is a post-decision action methodology with three linked parts.

1. **Clarification and articulation of one's values:** make implicit principles explicit, name the consequences of acting on them, and connect the issue to values such as honesty, respect, responsibility, fairness, and compassion.
2. **Post-decision-making analysis and implementation plan:** begin from the conclusion about the right action, then analyze stakeholders as people to influence rather than merely as variables in a utilitarian trade-off. Anticipate their **reasons and rationalizations**, identify who should be approached, and determine the levers of power, evidence, relationships, or interests that could draw them toward the desired action.
3. **The practice of speaking one's values and receiving feedback:** use **pre-scripting**. Write what to say, rehearse it with another person, and receive peer-coach feedback. Practice makes an ethical intention cognitively clear and behaviorally executable under pressure.

GVV does not promise that one conversation will produce systemic change. It does, however, replace passive discomfort with preparation, stakeholder strategy, coalition building, questions, data gathering, alliances, and a feasible voice.

### Duck's Five-Stage Change Curve
**When to use:** Use Duck's model to monitor the human and emotional side of change. It is a simplification of complex, volatile responses, not a claim that every person experiences the same emotions at the same speed.

**How:** Identify the dominant organizational response and match leadership behavior to it.

1. **Stagnation:** people underestimate external threat or deny the need to move. Leaders must make the situation visible and create a credible demand for attention.
2. **Preparation:** an announcement produces anxiety, hope, cynicism, and often a productivity dip. Leaders need aligned sponsorship, planning, and operational readiness.
3. **Implementation:** structures, job descriptions, plans, work practices, and mindsets begin to change. Operational redesign without emotional and habit change is insufficient.
4. **Determination:** people realize that work will genuinely be different and search for a reason to quit. Leaders must sustain energy, attention, and commitment to the vision.
5. **Fruition:** new work is easier, faster, and more effective; confidence and optimism rise. Leaders must celebrate without allowing satisfaction to become a new stagnation.

### Beckhard and Harris's Change-Management Process
**When to use:** Use Beckhard and Harris when a change leader needs an action-oriented process for explaining why change is necessary, defining the destination, and managing the move between states.

**How:**

1. Assess the need for change by examining the forces for and against it, the organization, and its stakeholders.
2. Define the desired future state and compare it with present reality through **gap analysis**. Use the gap to sharpen both the change vision and the case for action.
3. Specify the actions and implementation route for closing the gap.
4. Manage the transition from the current mode of operation to the desired state.

The model concentrates on process and improves on a purely high-level stage description, but it gives less operational direction about how to bring each phase to life. The Change Path Model supplies that additional guidance.

### Deszca/Ingols Change Path Model
**When to use:** Use the Change Path as the chapter's primary organizing framework when a leader must connect environmental scanning, diagnosis, vision, stakeholder engagement, implementation, transition, and measurement across the life of a change.

**How:**

1. **Awakening:** conduct a Critical Organizational Analysis. Scan external forces such as legislation, competitors, demographic trends, technology, and ecological pressures; examine internal data such as turnover, capabilities, operations, and stakeholder experience. Determine why the present state is no longer sufficient and express an initial vision that relevant people can understand.
2. **Mobilization:** activate the gap analysis. Compare the present state with the desired future, test the underlying causes, and engage people across levels and functions. Analyze formal structures and processes, power and culture, stakeholders, recipients of change, and the change agents' own capabilities. Use multiple communication channels to close information gaps and build participation.
3. **Acceleration:** turn the diagnosis and vision into an action plan and implementation. Assign work, provide resources, build knowledge and skills, empower participants, manage transition while normal operations continue, adapt to new information, and use milestones and small wins to build momentum.
4. **Institutionalization:** monitor whether the change has become part of organizational processes and behavior. Measure outcomes, not only inputs such as training completion; use trustworthy data to learn, correct course, and prepare for subsequent change.

The Change Path combines descriptive insight and prescription. Its stages are a guide, not permission to oversimplify. Organizations may run several initiatives simultaneously, conditions may change during implementation, and different functions may need different communication or transition tactics.

## Key Concepts
- **How to change:** The process used to move an organization from its current state to a desired state.
- **What to change:** The content or substance of the organizational problem, system, behavior, structure, or capability requiring attention.
- **Failure of success:** The tendency for past practices that once produced success to become rules, habits, assumptions, and expectations that resist necessary adaptation.
- **Sigmoid curve:** A life-cycle pattern in which performance grows, matures, flattens, and declines; planned change should begin around point B, while the need is still emerging, rather than point A, when crisis makes it obvious and potentially too late.
- **Unfreezing:** Awakening a system to the fact that its existing equilibrium or status quo is no longer tenable.
- **Re-gel:** The development of a new relative equilibrium as changed structures, roles, processes, and behaviors become normal.
- **Homeostasis:** The interdependent balance through which organizational systems resist disturbances and restore familiar patterns.
- **Gap analysis:** A disciplined comparison of present reality and desired future state that clarifies why change is needed and what must be bridged.
- **Change vision:** A compelling, understandable description of the desired future and why pursuing it is worth the cost and uncertainty.
- **Transition management:** The practical and human management of movement between old and new ways while the organization continues operating.
- **Institutionalization:** Embedding change in processes, culture, measures, and everyday behavior so that it is not dependent on a temporary campaign.
- **Reasons and rationalizations:** The predictable explanations or defenses stakeholders may use when someone challenges an unethical or values-inconsistent action.

## Mental Models
- **Two-question discipline:** Before selecting an intervention, ask separately, "What is wrong or insufficient?" and "How will people and systems move?" A correct diagnosis can still fail through a poor process.
- **Start on the second curve:** Treat the sigmoid curve as a timing rule. Invest at point B, when the current curve still looks successful and the future benefits are uncertain, rather than waiting for point A and an emergency.
- **The organization as a web:** Assume that systems, roles, power, culture, assumptions, and habits are interdependent. A local fix will be absorbed by the old equilibrium unless connected strands are shifted too.
- **A portfolio of lenses:** Match the model to the problem: Lewin for the overall system logic, Kotter for a detailed organizational roadmap, GVV for values-based individual action, Duck for emotional transitions, Beckhard and Harris for gap and transition design, and the Change Path for an integrated lifecycle view.

## Anti-patterns
- **Jumping from symptom to solution:** A complaint or metric can signal a problem but is not a diagnosis. Investigate causes, affected systems, and whether the experience is isolated or patterned.
- **Treating the change as only technical:** Buying a system or issuing a policy does not change habits, incentives, power, skills, or culture by itself.
- **Assuming sponsor readiness equals organizational readiness:** Leaders and recipients may hold different views of urgency. Unfreeze and mobilize the levels that must actually work differently.
- **Stopping after an announcement or early win:** Change that is not consolidated and anchored can recede when attention or leadership changes.
- **Applying stages as a rigid checklist:** Models help sequence attention, but concurrent initiatives, feedback, and local context require adaptation rather than ceremonial stage completion.
- **Freezing too firmly:** A new equilibrium should support reliability without making the organization unable to notice and respond to new environmental signals.
- **Telling people to speak up without preparing them:** Values-based action needs stakeholder analysis, anticipated rationalizations, influence levers, a script, and practice, not only a statement that integrity matters.
- **Measuring activity instead of effect:** Training counts and implementation dates are inputs. Leaders also need outcome measures and safeguards against incentives to hide bad results.

## Worked Example (study depth)
The chapter applies the Change Path to a hotel after a guest reports two unmade rooms, delayed callbacks, excuses and berating among employees, inconsistent service, and an unexpected $72 room-change charge. The letter makes the **what** visible, but the general manager still has to determine the **how**.

1. **Awakening - test the signal and make the need credible.** The manager should not assume the letter is either a complete diagnosis or an isolated anomaly. He can compare it with occupancy, profitability, customer-satisfaction data, other complaints, and evidence about room readiness. The issue may be especially urgent if reputation is at risk or repeated rework is raising costs. He must also recognize that reception staff may believe performance is acceptable and therefore require their own unfreezing.
2. **Mobilization - turn symptoms into gaps and a vision.** The case suggests gaps between housekeeping's room status and the desk clerk's information, managerial promises and follow-through, the correct bill and the bill issued, desired respectful relationships and actual interactions, and intended guest handling and actual service. The manager should investigate whether the culture and systems create these patterns, then communicate a vision of reliable room readiness, accurate billing, dependable follow-through, and respectful customer care. Staff and other stakeholders should help test the diagnosis and shape the response.
3. **Acceleration - implement a selected route while running the hotel.** If the priority is the room-information system, the manager can discuss the gap and vision with staff, form a users' task force to define outcomes and usability, consult internal information-systems specialists, identify funding, issue a request for proposals, and coordinate staffing and training with human resources. Receptionists still have to allocate rooms during the change, so parallel old and new systems may be needed until defects are corrected. The system cannot substitute for training, accountability, or attention to service norms.
4. **Institutionalization - make performance visible and trustworthy.** Management can track training completion, room misallocation, customer experience, billing errors, and follow-through. It should not rely only on the easy input measure of how many people were trained. Because negative reporting could make staff look bad, the measurement system needs credible follow-up and a culture that uses data to improve rather than punish concealment. New reporting routines and cross-functional feedback can help the replacement system re-gel.

The example shows why the models complement one another. Lewin explains the need to disturb the hotel's existing equilibrium; Beckhard and Harris clarify the gap; Duck warns that employees may respond emotionally; Kotter supplies coalition, communication, empowerment, and wins; and the Change Path organizes the work from signal to embedded practice. A computer installation alone would not resolve the hotel's managerial, cultural, process, and service problems.

## Key Takeaways
1. Separate the content question, "What needs to change?" from the process question, "How will the change happen?"
2. Begin planned change before decline makes the need undeniable; the costs are visible early, but delay can make survival more expensive or impossible.
3. Unfreeze the whole relevant system, not just the executive sponsor. Existing equilibrium is sustained by interconnected structures, relationships, habits, and beliefs.
4. Use models at the level of the problem: system, organization, team or unit, and individual. Their differences in detail and lens are useful rather than contradictory.
5. Make the gap and desired future clear enough to become a concise, compelling explanation of why action is worthwhile.
6. Treat emotional responses, ethical conflicts, stakeholder influence, transition work, and ordinary operations as central change work rather than collateral issues.
7. Use short-term evidence to build momentum, but continue consolidating until new behavior is embedded in culture, processes, and measures.
8. Institutionalization is not permanent rigidity. A healthy new equilibrium remains capable of sensing and responding to the next environmental shift.
