# Patterns, Techniques, and Models

## Deszca/Ingols Change Path Model
**When to use**: Organize a significant change from first signal through durable adoption.
**How**: Awaken through external and internal diagnosis; mobilize with gap, vision, stakeholder, and readiness work; accelerate through action, resources, skills, and transition; institutionalize through outcome measures, routines, and learning.
**Trade-offs**: Gives sequence without promising linearity. Run feedback and concurrent work; institutionalization starts the next awakening.

## What/How Split and Model Portfolio
**When to use**: Before selecting a solution or process.
**How**: Answer what is insufficient and why, then choose a how-lens: Lewin for equilibrium, Kotter for a detailed roadmap, Beckhard and Harris for gap-to-transition, Duck for emotional response, and Giving Voice to Values for ethical action.
**Trade-offs**: Multiple lenses improve coverage but can become checklist theater. Use only the models that answer a live decision.

## Open Systems and PESTEL Scan
**When to use**: Establish why change is needed or test strategic fit.
**How**: Scan political, economic, social, technological, ecological, and legal forces; trace inputs, transformation, outputs, and feedback; translate trends into strategic, structural, capability, and stakeholder implications.
**Trade-offs**: Broad scanning exposes weak signals but can create noise. Prioritize forces that alter value, constraints, timing, or risk.

## Nadler and Tushman's Congruence Model
**When to use**: Performance is weak and the responsible component is unclear.
**How**: Map environment, history, resources, strategy, work, people, formal organization, informal organization, outputs, and feedback. Test internal fit and external fit at individual, group, unit, and organization levels.
**Trade-offs**: Comprehensive diagnosis takes time. Prefer workable alignment that can adapt over perfect fit delivered too late.

## Sterman Systems-Dynamics Test
**When to use**: A rational intervention produces delayed, reversed, or surprising results.
**How**: State intended and unintended effects; map actor responses, incentives, feedback, delays, buffers, and competing goals; define short- and long-horizon indicators before acting.
**Trade-offs**: More realistic causal thinking slows simple action. It prevents overcorrecting because a lagging result was misread.

## Competing-Values and Growth-Stage Diagnosis
**When to use**: Priorities conflict or growth has made a once-effective design strain.
**How**: Use Quinn's Human Resources, Open Systems, Rational Economic, and Internal Process views to expose missing values. Use Greiner to identify the growth crisis and the shift it demands; use Stacey when the future is unknowable.
**Trade-offs**: These are heuristics, not universal sequences. Use them to surface tensions, not to force a prescribed stage.

## Four-Lens Need Assessment and Readiness Scan
**When to use**: Before trying to unfreeze or build urgency.
**How**: Triangulate external data, stakeholder perspectives, internal hard and soft data, and the leader's blind spots. Test the five readiness beliefs and eight conditions; a readiness score below 10 signals that change is unlikely to be easy.
**Trade-offs**: Participation improves legitimacy but takes time. Scores structure conversation; they do not replace judgment.

## Change-Vision Design
**When to use**: The gap is credible but action lacks a shared destination.
**How**: Select leader, senior-team, or bottom-up formation; make the vision clear, memorable, challenging, tangible, flexible, and implementable; connect purpose to strategy, behavior, measures, milestones, and stories.
**Trade-offs**: Leader-built visions are faster; participative visions gain ownership. A broad slogan without choices or enactment creates cynicism.

## Lewin, Kotter, and Beckhard-Harris Sequencing
**When to use**: The initiative needs a shared lifecycle or managerial roadmap.
**How**: Unfreeze, change, and re-gel with Lewin; use Kotter's urgency, coalition, vision, communication, empowerment, wins, consolidation, and culture anchors; use Beckhard and Harris to assess forces, define the gap, specify actions, and manage transition.
**Trade-offs**: Sequence creates discipline, but rigid stage completion ignores concurrent work and feedback.

## Individual Adoption and Transition Check
**When to use**: The organization has announced a change but behavior is stalled.
**How**: Diagnose ADKAR's missing Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, or Reinforcement; separately surface psychological-contract losses, procedural justice, distributive justice, and emotional transition needs.
**Trade-offs**: Individual tools focus attention but can obscure power, culture, or system causes. Pair them with recipient and system diagnosis.

## Power, Culture, and Stakeholder Map
**When to use**: Formal authority does not explain support, resistance, or decisions.
**How**: Inventory positional, network, knowledge, reputation, resource, process, and meaning power; read Schein's three culture levels; map affected parties and connectors; use Savage's threat/cooperation choices to tailor engagement.
**Trade-offs**: Political diagnosis can feel manipulative. Use it transparently to build legitimate support, not to hide costs or divide people.

## Individual Change Equation and Force Field
**When to use**: A proposal looks good organizationally but people do not act.
**How**: For each person, compare dissatisfaction x benefits x probability of success with perceived cost. Then list driving and restraining forces, estimate strength and timing, and change a leverage point rather than merely adding pressure.
**Trade-offs**: Perceptions are hard to measure and forces interact. Treat both maps as hypotheses and revisit them after conversations.

## Galbraith Information-Processing Design
**When to use**: Uncertainty, interdependence, or decision delay is overwhelming the structure.
**How**: Reduce information demand with buffers, self-contained units, outsourcing, automation, or fewer dependencies; or increase capacity with rules, vertical systems, lateral contact, task forces, integrating roles, networks, or matrix authority.
**Trade-offs**: Slack costs money; coordination mechanisms cost attention and can blur authority. Match the smallest mechanism to the bottleneck.

## Approval, Commitment, and Acceptance Routes
**When to use**: A proposal needs legitimacy, resources, or behavior change.
**How**: Choose straightforward formal approval, creeping commitment and coalition building, or a bounded renegade experiment within discretion. After approval, align ownership, staffing, skills, measures, rewards, systems, and communication for acceptance.
**Trade-offs**: Formal routes protect risk and due process; coalition routes build ownership; renegade pilots move quickly but require defensible scope and credibility.

## Mintzberg-Westley and Nohria Path Selection
**When to use**: Choose how much to plan before acting.
**How**: Use thinking first for clear, structured work; seeing first for solutions people must experience; doing first for novel ambiguity. Pair with programmatic, discontinuous, or emergent change according to clarity, scale, and capability.
**Trade-offs**: Programmatic work is controllable but rigid; emergent work learns but can drift; doing first needs bounded experiments and feedback.

## Action-Planning Toolkit
**When to use**: Convert a diagnosis and vision into coordinated implementation.
**How**: Use the smallest adequate tool: responsibility charting, flow charts, contingency or scenario plans, critical path, design thinking, surveys, cultural and stakeholder maps, leverage analysis, training, and an after-action review. Use BART for team Tasks, Authority, Roles, and Boundaries.
**Trade-offs**: More tools increase control and coordination cost. Add structure where dependencies, risk, or ownership demand it.

## Influence and Communication Ladder
**When to use**: Support depends on understanding, trust, capability, or power.
**How**: Prefer education, participation, facilitation, and support; negotiate when interests are organized; reserve manipulation, coercion, or edict for exceptional cases. Match channel richness and personal relevance to the stakes, and repeat two-way messages.
**Trade-offs**: Pull tactics take time but build commitment. Pressure can create compliance quickly while leaving resistance, resentment, and weak adoption.

## Simon Controls, Strategy Maps, and Balanced Scorecards
**When to use**: Keep a complex change aligned while learning.
**How**: Use interactive controls for strategic sensing, boundary systems for limits, belief systems for meaning, and diagnostic/steering controls for milestones and correction. Map capabilities to processes, stakeholder value, and results; balance financial, customer, process, and learning measures.
**Trade-offs**: Too many measures dilute attention; too few invite gaming. Use leading and lagging indicators that people can influence.

## Risk Exposure Calculator and DICE
**When to use**: Decide whether the change needs stronger safeguards or capacity.
**How**: Score the nine risk-exposure drivers from pressure, culture, and information management; separately calculate DICE using Duration + 2Integrity + 2Senior Commitment + Local Commitment + Effort.
**Trade-offs**: Scores reveal levers, not certainty. Treat Risk Exposure 35-45 as danger and DICE 17 as an escalation point, then improve capability, commitment, data, or review cadence.

## Person x Vision x Situation and Change-Team Design
**When to use**: Select, develop, or support a change agent.
**How**: Match capability, emotional reserves, credibility, and style to the vision and situation; combine specialists and generalists when needed. Build a small team with sponsorship, real authority, clear BART boundaries, and shared results.
**Trade-offs**: A heroic agent may move quickly but creates dependency and burnout. Fit, coalition, reflection, and distributed ownership improve endurance.

## Loose/Tight Paradox Management
**When to use**: Global, digital, networked, or uncertain work needs both coherence and local adaptation.
**How**: Hold purpose, strategy, critical variables, and safety boundaries tight; leave local execution, experimentation, and cultural response loose. State the tension, decide what is fixed, and monitor interactions.
**Trade-offs**: Too tight creates rigidity; too loose creates fragmentation. The correct balance changes as conditions and capabilities change.
