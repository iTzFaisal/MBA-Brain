# Chapter 14: Short-Term Financial Planning

## Core Idea
Financial planning is valuable because it forces a firm to prepare for uncertainty, not because its forecast will be perfectly accurate. Forecast sales and expenses, estimate the assets needed to support them, and then identify which financing must be actively obtained. Pro forma statements and the cash budget turn the plan into a benchmark for control.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three-step financial forecasting process**
  - When to use: When estimating a firm's financing requirements over a planning period.
  - How: (1) Project sales revenues and expenses. (2) Estimate current and fixed assets needed to support sales. (3) Determine the financing required to fund those assets.
- **Percent of sales method**
  - When to use: When an expense, asset, or liability is expected to vary approximately in proportion to sales.
  - How: Convert each variable account to a current-sales ratio; multiply that ratio by forecast sales; leave accounts that do not vary with sales unchanged. Treat accounts payable and accrued expenses as spontaneous financing, and notes payable, long-term debt, and equity issues as discretionary financing.
- **Discretionary financing needs (DFN) analysis**
  - When to use: When management needs to know the amount of funding it must actively negotiate after operations and spontaneous credit are considered.
  - How: Project total assets, spontaneous liabilities, and retained earnings, then calculate:
    `DFN = change in total assets - change in spontaneous liabilities - change in retained earnings`.
    The retained-earnings addition is `projected net income - planned dividends`, or `projected sales * net profit margin * (1 - payout ratio)`. Positive DFN requires borrowing or new equity; negative DFN is a funding surplus. EFN is broader: `EFN = change in total assets - change in retained earnings`.
- **Cash budget**
  - When to use: When the amount and timing of cash shortages or surpluses matter, especially with seasonal sales.
  - How: Schedule cash receipts and cash disbursements by month, compute the net change in cash, compare with the desired minimum balance, and schedule new financing or repayment. Use actual collection and payment timing, not merely revenue and expense recognition.

## Key Concepts
- **Financial forecasting**: Estimating a firm's future financing requirements.
- **Sales forecast**: The planning input based on past trends plus anticipated events such as pricing or advertising changes.
- **Percent of sales method**: A low-cost forecast that expresses an item as a percentage of forecast sales.
- **Spontaneous financing**: Trade credit and other payables that arise automatically from day-to-day operations.
- **Discretionary financing**: Financing requiring an explicit management decision each time funds are raised.
- **External financing needs (EFN)**: Financing beyond retained earnings, including spontaneous financing.
- **Intercept**: The value predicted by a linear forecast when sales are zero.
- **Slope coefficient**: The change in the forecast item for a one-unit change in sales.
- **Cash budget**: A detailed plan of cash receipts, disbursements, net cash change, and new financing.

## Mental Models
- **Forecasts are contingency maps**: Use them to prepare responses to adverse events and opportunities; do not confuse a forecast with certainty.
- **The balance-sheet funding gap**: Assets are the uses of funds; spontaneous liabilities and retained earnings are passive sources; DFN is the residual that management must source.
- **Profitability is not cash timing**: A profitable firm can need short-term borrowing when collections lag payments. Use the cash budget to see when the gap occurs.
- **The line-through-the-origin warning**: A percent-of-sales forecast assumes zero sales imply zero balance. Test for a fixed minimum, economies of scale, or lumpy investment before applying it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Put every account on a fixed sales ratio**: Fixed-capacity assets, minimum inventories, economies of scale, and lumpy plant purchases violate the proportionality assumption.
- **Treat EFN as the amount to negotiate**: EFN includes spontaneous financing; DFN isolates the funding that requires an active financing decision.
- **Forecast annual cash only**: Annual totals hide seasonal deficits, minimum-balance requirements, and the month in which borrowing must begin.
- **Assume growth finances itself**: Higher sales can increase assets faster than retained earnings and spontaneous liabilities, especially with low margins or high dividend payouts.

## Worked Example
Drew Inc. expects sales to rise from `$10.0M` to `$12.0M`. Current assets are 20% of sales, fixed assets are projected in proportion to sales, projected net income is 5% of sales, and the payout ratio is 50%. The projected asset total is `$7.2M`; projected liabilities and spontaneous sources total `$4.9M`; existing and projected owner funds total `$1.8M`.

1. Addition to retained earnings: `$12.0M * 0.05 * (1 - 0.50) = $0.30M`.
2. Discretionary financing: `$7.2M - $4.9M - $1.8M = $0.50M`.
3. Interpretation: Drew must actively raise `$500,000` through notes, bonds, stock, or a combination. A higher profit margin or lower payout reduces DFN; a higher payout increases it. Under the same assumptions, the chapter's forecast shows DFN reaches zero at about 6.667% sales growth.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start every financial plan with a defensible sales forecast, but use the planning process to prepare contingencies rather than promise precision.
2. Apply percent-of-sales ratios only to accounts whose economic behavior is genuinely proportional to sales.
3. Calculate retained earnings after dividends; retained profit is an internal financing source.
4. Use DFN to isolate financing management must actively obtain, and interpret a negative DFN as excess internal/spontaneous funding.
5. Use a cash budget to identify the amount and timing of borrowing, repayment, and surplus investment.
