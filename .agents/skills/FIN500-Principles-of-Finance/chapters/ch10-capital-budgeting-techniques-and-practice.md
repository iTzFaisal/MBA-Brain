# Chapter 10: Capital-Budgeting Techniques and Practice

## Core Idea
Capital budgeting is the decision to accept or reject investments in fixed assets. Evaluate the project's free cash flows at the correct time value of money, with **net present value (NPV)** as the preferred criterion because it measures the dollar wealth created for shareholders.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Payback and discounted payback**
  - **When to use:** Use payback as a quick liquidity screen, especially when early cash recovery matters or capital is severely constrained; use discounted payback when the time value of money must be recognized in that screen.
  - **How:** Accumulate free cash flows until the initial outlay is recovered, then add the unrecovered amount divided by the cash flow in the recovery year. For discounted payback, first discount each FCF at `k`. Accept only when the result is no greater than the firm's maximum acceptable period. Treat the cutoff as a managerial convention, not a measure of value.
- **Net present value (NPV)**
  - **When to use:** Use for independent projects and as the tie-breaker for competing projects because the objective is shareholder-wealth maximization.
  - **How:**
    `NPV = Sum[t=1..n] FCF_t / (1 + k)^t - IO`
    Accept when `NPV >= 0`; reject when `NPV < 0`. A zero NPV earns exactly the required return.
- **Profitability index (PI), or benefit-cost ratio**
  - **When to use:** Use as a relative benefit-per-dollar measure, particularly when a budget constraint makes the scale of the investment relevant.
  - **How:**
    `PI = [Sum[t=1..n] FCF_t / (1 + k)^t] / IO`
    Accept an independent project when `PI >= 1`. PI and NPV always agree on accept/reject for an independent project, but PI can rank projects differently from NPV.
- **Internal rate of return (IRR) and modified IRR (MIRR)**
  - **When to use:** Use IRR when a rate-of-return measure is required, but prefer NPV for the final wealth decision. Use MIRR when cash-flow sign changes make ordinary IRR ambiguous.
  - **How:** IRR solves `IO = Sum[t=1..n] FCF_t / (1 + IRR)^t`; accept when `IRR >= k`. For MIRR, discount all outflows to the present at `k`, compound inflows to the terminal date at `k`, and solve `PV_outflows = TV_inflows / (1 + MIRR)^n`. Accept when `MIRR >= k`.
- **Capital rationing and mutually exclusive project selection**
  - **When to use:** Apply when a dollar budget prevents accepting every positive-NPV project, or when projects serve the same purpose and only one can be selected.
  - **How:** Under a budget constraint, choose the feasible set with the largest total NPV; do not simply select the highest PI or IRR, especially when projects are indivisible. For mutually exclusive projects without rationing, choose the largest NPV. For unequal lives, use a replacement chain or equivalent annual annuity (EAA) before ranking.

## Key Concepts
- **Capital budgeting:** Decision-making about investments in fixed assets and whether a proposal should be accepted or rejected.
- **Free cash flow (FCF):** The cash benefits and costs generated by the project, rather than accounting profit.
- **Payback period:** Years required to recover the initial outlay from undiscounted FCFs.
- **Discounted payback period:** Years required to recover the initial outlay from FCFs discounted at the required return.
- **NPV:** Present value of future FCFs less the initial cash outlay; it is stated in today's dollars.
- **Profitability index:** Present value of future FCFs divided by the initial outlay.
- **IRR:** The discount rate that makes the project's NPV equal to zero.
- **MIRR:** A modified return measure that moves outflows to the present and inflows to the terminal date at the required return.
- **Capital rationing:** A limit on the dollar size of the capital budget.
- **Equivalent annual annuity (EAA):** An annuity with the same present value as a project's NPV, used to compare unequal-life projects.

## Mental Models
- **Principle 2: Money Has a Time Value.** A dollar received earlier can be reinvested sooner; do not compare cash flows at different dates without discounting them.
- **Wealth in dollars beats a percentage ranking.** PI and IRR describe relative efficiency or return, but NPV tells how many dollars of value the project adds.
- **Sign reversals are a warning light.** A conventional cash-flow pattern has one negative-to-positive reversal and normally one positive IRR; additional reversals can produce multiple IRRs, at which point NPV is the safer decision rule.
- **Unequal life means omitted future choices.** A longer-lived project may look better only because the shorter project could be replaced or reinvested in later; equalize lives before comparing.

## Anti-patterns
- **Using accounting profit instead of FCF:** Profit timing includes noncash accounting allocations and can misstate when benefits and costs are available.
- **Treating payback as a value measure:** It ignores the time value of money, all cash flows after recovery, and the arbitrary maximum cutoff.
- **Choosing the highest IRR or PI among unequal-size projects:** A smaller project can have the higher percentage while creating fewer dollars of wealth.
- **Accepting an IRR without checking the cash-flow pattern:** Multiple sign reversals can create multiple IRRs with no meaningful single return interpretation.
- **Selecting projects independently under a hard budget:** The correct problem is a constrained portfolio choice; maximize total NPV subject to the budget and indivisibility.

## Worked Example
Ski-Doo considers machinery with an initial outlay of `$40,000`, a required return of `12%`, and FCFs of `$15,000`, `$14,000`, `$13,000`, `$12,000`, and `$11,000` in years 1-5. Discounting each inflow gives a total present value of `$47,675`. Therefore:

`NPV = $47,675 - $40,000 = $7,675`

The positive NPV means the machinery creates `$7,675` of value in today's dollars, so the project should be accepted. This illustrates why NPV incorporates both Principle 2 and the shareholder-wealth objective; the later `$11,000` inflow is not discarded merely because recovery occurred earlier.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use payback only as a rough liquidity or screening device; it is not a substitute for discounted cash-flow analysis.
2. Accept an independent project when `NPV >= 0`, equivalently when `PI >= 1` or a valid single `IRR >= k`.
3. Prefer NPV when methods conflict because it measures the dollar wealth created.
4. Under capital rationing, select the feasible combination with the largest total NPV, not necessarily the highest individual PI or IRR.
5. Resolve unequal lives with replacement chains or EAA before comparing mutually exclusive projects.
6. Use MIRR only as a more interpretable return measure; still confirm that NPV is positive.
