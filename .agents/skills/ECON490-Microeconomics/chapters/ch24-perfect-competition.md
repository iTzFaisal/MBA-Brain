# Chapter 24: Perfect Competition

## Core Idea
Perfect competition is a benchmark in which many buyers and sellers trade a homogeneous product, entry is unrestricted, and each firm takes the market price as given. The firm chooses output where `price = marginal cost`, operates in the short run when price covers average variable cost, and earns zero economic profit in long-run equilibrium after entry and exit.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Perfect-competition benchmark**: Use when analyzing a price-taking firm. The five features are many sellers, many buyers, a homogeneous product, no barriers to entry, and price-taking behavior. The firm's demand curve is horizontal at the market price, unlike the market demand curve.
- **Total and marginal output rules**: Use to find a firm's short-run output. The total approach compares total revenue and total cost at each quantity. The marginal approach uses the marginal principle: because `MR = price` for a price taker, increase output while `price > MC` and stop where `price = MC`.
- **Break-even and shutdown rules**: Use when the firm is losing money. `economic profit = (price - ATC) * Q`; the break-even price is the minimum ATC. In the short run, operate if `TR >= VC`, equivalently `price >= AVC`, because fixed cost is sunk. Shut down if price falls below minimum AVC.
- **Short-run supply framework**: Use to derive supply from firm behavior. The firm's short-run supply curve is the rising portion of MC above the shutdown price; the market supply curve is the horizontal sum of firms' supplies.
- **Long-run entry-and-exit framework**: Use when facilities and firm count can change. Positive economic profit attracts entry; losses cause exit. Long-run equilibrium requires market clearing, profit maximization, and zero economic profit, so `price = ATC` for the typical firm.
- **Increasing- versus constant-cost industry framework**: Use to predict long-run effects of demand changes. In an increasing-cost industry, expanding output raises input prices and the long-run supply curve slopes upward. In a constant-cost industry, input prices remain unchanged and long-run supply is horizontal; demand shocks create a short-run price jump followed by a return to the original price.

## Key Concepts
- **Perfectly competitive market**: A market with many buyers and sellers, a homogeneous product, no entry barriers, and price takers.
- **Price taker**: A buyer or seller that accepts the market price as given.
- **Firm-specific demand curve**: The price-quantity relationship facing one firm.
- **Marginal revenue**: Change in total revenue from one more unit of output.
- **Break-even price**: Price at which economic profit is zero and price equals ATC.
- **Shutdown price**: Minimum AVC, where operating and shutting down are equally costly in the short run.
- **Sunk cost**: A cost already paid or committed and unrecoverable.
- **Short-run market supply**: Total quantity supplied by all firms when facilities are fixed.
- **Increasing-cost industry**: Industry whose average cost rises as total output expands.
- **Constant-cost industry**: Industry with unchanged average cost as output expands.

## Mental Models
- **Use `P = MC` for the quantity, then the demand curve for the price**: A price-taking firm does not choose a price; it chooses the profitable quantity at the given market price.
- **Use `P >= AVC` for today's operation and `P >= ATC` for long-run survival**: Fixed costs are irrelevant to a temporary shutdown but relevant to replacing or maintaining the facility.
- **Use the entry clock after a demand shock**: Expect a large short-run price move with fixed capacity, then entry, facility expansion, and a lower long-run price in an increasing-cost industry.
- **Use the sunk-cost test on irreversible commitments**: Ignore costs that cannot change with today's operation decision.

## Anti-patterns
- **Setting price above the market price to sell more**: A price-taking firm would lose all customers at a higher price.
- **Shutting down whenever accounting profit is negative**: An existing firm can rationally operate at a loss if revenue covers variable cost and contributes toward fixed cost.
- **Using ATC for a short-run shutdown decision**: The relevant comparison is total revenue with variable cost, or price with AVC.
- **Assuming zero economic profit means the entrepreneur earns nothing**: It means revenue covers every opportunity cost, including the owner's time and capital.
- **Treating a short-run supply response as the long-run equilibrium**: Entry, exit, and input-price changes alter the long-run result.

## Worked Example
The textbook's plain T-shirt firm faces a market price of $12 per shirt. At eight shirts per minute, total revenue is `$12 * 8 = $96`, total cost is $63, and economic profit is $33. The marginal rule reaches the same result: the eighth shirt has `MR = P = $12` and `MC = $12`; the ninth costs $16, so producing it would reduce profit by $4. If price falls to $4, the firm produces four shirts where price meets MC, earning total revenue of $16 against total cost of $30 and a $14 loss. It still operates because variable cost is only $13, so `TR > VC`; shutting down would leave the firm with the full fixed cost. The shutdown price is the minimum AVC, $3. Below $3, operating cannot cover variable cost.

## Key Takeaways
1. Identify whether the firm is a price taker before applying the output rule.
2. Choose output where `P = MC`, provided the firm is above its shutdown condition.
3. Compare price with AVC for a short-run operation decision and with ATC for long-run entry or exit.
4. Treat fixed costs that cannot be recovered as sunk when deciding whether to operate today.
5. Expect entry to erase positive economic profit in long-run competitive equilibrium.
6. Distinguish the steep short-run supply response from the flatter long-run response.
