# Chapter 4: Demand, Supply, and Market Equilibrium

## Core Idea
The demand-and-supply model explains how a perfectly competitive market coordinates buyers and sellers. Demand describes what consumers are willing and able to buy, supply describes what firms are willing and able to sell, and the equilibrium price removes excess demand or excess supply. Shifts caused by nonprice variables predict changes in both price and quantity.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Law of demand and demand curve**: Use ceteris paribus to isolate the negative relationship between price and quantity demanded. A price change causes movement along a demand curve; changes in income, related-good prices, tastes, population, advertising, or expectations shift the entire curve.
- **Law of supply and supply curve**: Hold input costs, technology, expectations, and taxes or subsidies fixed to isolate the positive relationship between price and quantity supplied. The supply curve traces marginal cost: a producer supplies the next unit when its price, the marginal benefit, covers that unit's marginal cost.
- **Market aggregation**: Use horizontal summation to construct market demand or supply. At each price, add the quantities demanded by all consumers or supplied by all firms; do not add prices.
- **Equilibrium adjustment and comparative statics**: Equilibrium occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. A price below equilibrium creates excess demand and upward pressure; a price above equilibrium creates excess supply and downward pressure. For a single shift, demand changes price and quantity in the same direction, while supply changes them in opposite directions.

## Key Concepts
- **Quantity demanded**: The amount consumers are willing and able to buy at a particular price and time.
- **Demand schedule/curve**: A table/curve showing price and quantity demanded, ceteris paribus.
- **Law of demand**: Price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, other things equal.
- **Quantity supplied**: The amount firms are willing and able to sell at a particular price and time.
- **Supply schedule/curve**: A table/curve showing price and quantity supplied, ceteris paribus.
- **Law of supply**: Price and quantity supplied move in the same direction, other things equal.
- **Market equilibrium**: The price and quantity where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
- **Excess demand/shortage**: Quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the prevailing price.
- **Excess supply/surplus**: Quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded at the prevailing price.
- **Normal, inferior, substitute, and complement goods**: Demand relationships used to predict shifts from income or related prices.

## Mental Models
- **Use the movement-versus-shift test**: If the product's own price changed, move along the curve; if another determinant changed, shift the curve.
- **Use the equilibrium-pressure test**: Shortage pushes price up, which reduces quantity demanded and increases quantity supplied; surplus does the reverse.
- **Use price-quantity directions as an economic detective**: Price and quantity moving together indicate a demand shift; moving oppositely indicates a supply shift, absent simultaneous shifts.
- **Use the marginal-cost interpretation of supply**: A higher market price makes additional, more costly units worthwhile and invites expansion or entry.

## Anti-patterns
- **Calling a price-driven movement a change in demand or supply**: A lower price changes quantity demanded or supplied, not the underlying curve.
- **Shifting a curve because of a change on its axis**: Price belongs on the curve; income, technology, input prices, and expectations shift it.
- **Assuming every price fall means increased supply**: If equilibrium quantity also falls, decreased demand is the more consistent explanation.
- **Using the one-shift rule when both curves move**: With simultaneous changes, quantity may be predictable while price depends on the relative size of the shifts.
- **Ignoring entry and exit**: Market supply reflects the quantities of all firms, including new producers in the longer run.

## Worked Example
The textbook's pizza market illustrates equilibrium adjustment. At the equilibrium price of $8, consumers demand 30,000 pizzas and firms supply 30,000, so no participant has pressure to change price. At $6, consumers want 36,000 but firms supply only 20,000: the 16,000-pizza shortage lets firms raise prices. Moving upward along demand reduces quantity demanded, while moving upward along supply increases quantity supplied, until the $8 equilibrium returns. At $12, firms offer 50,000 pizzas while consumers buy only 18,000, creating a 32,000-pizza surplus. Firms cut prices; quantity demanded rises and quantity supplied falls until equilibrium is restored. The same logic predicts why a long harmattan that reduces cocoa supply raises the equilibrium price of chocolate inputs.

## Key Takeaways
1. Define the relevant period, product, buyers, sellers, and ceteris paribus assumptions before drawing a curve.
2. Distinguish a change in quantity from a shift in demand or supply.
3. Use shortages and surpluses to explain the direction of price adjustment.
4. For one demand shift, price and quantity move together; for one supply shift, they move oppositely.
5. When demand and supply shift together, identify which outcome is certain and which depends on relative shift size.
