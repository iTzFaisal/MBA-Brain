# Chapter 27: Oligopoly and Strategic Behavior

## Core Idea
An oligopoly has only a few firms, so each firm's action changes the incentives and market share of its rivals. Game theory makes the strategic structure visible: firms may prefer a cartel outcome, but dominant incentives to underprice or advertise can produce a Nash equilibrium that leaves every firm worse off than cooperation.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Concentration and oligopoly screen**: Use to identify a market where strategic behavior matters. An oligopoly can result from government barriers, large economies of scale, or expensive advertising. The four-firm concentration ratio reports the share produced by the largest four firms; the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index is `HHI = sum of squared market shares`.
- **Cartel and duopolists' dilemma**: Use when firms could jointly choose the monopoly price. A cartel can raise profit, but each firm can gain by secretly underpricing and capturing customers. The resulting incentive often makes the low price a dominant strategy.
- **Game-tree and payoff-matrix analysis**: Use for sequential and simultaneous choices. Work backward through a game tree or compare each player's best response in each cell of a payoff matrix. A Nash equilibrium occurs when each player is doing the best it can given the other player's action.
- **Repeated-game cooperation**: Use when rivals interact repeatedly. Grim-trigger, tit-for-tat, and other retaliation strategies make the future cost of cheating larger than its one-period gain, but explicit price fixing remains illegal.
- **Low-price-guarantee mechanism**: Use to test a promise to match a rival's lower price. If both firms promise to match, underpricing no longer captures market share; the promise can eliminate the competitive discipline and support the high price.
- **Limit-pricing and contestability framework**: Use for an insecure monopolist facing possible entry. The incumbent may commit to a large quantity and lower price so a potential entrant cannot cover its minimum efficient scale. If entry and exit are easy, the threat alone can keep prices near competitive levels.
- **Advertisers' dilemma**: Use when advertising mostly shifts customers among firms. Each firm advertises because it benefits when the rival does not, even though both would earn more if neither advertised. Industry-wide generic advertising can solve the collective-action problem.

## Key Concepts
- **Oligopoly**: A market served by a few firms.
- **Duopoly**: A market with two firms.
- **Game theory**: Study of decision making in strategic situations.
- **Cartel**: Firms acting together to coordinate price and quantity.
- **Dominant strategy**: Best action regardless of the rival's action.
- **Nash equilibrium**: Outcome in which each player best responds to the others.
- **Duopolists' dilemma**: Both firms prefer mutual high prices, but each chooses low price.
- **Payoff matrix**: Table showing each player's outcome for every action combination.
- **Limit pricing**: Reducing price to deter entry.
- **Contestable market**: Market with low entry and exit costs.

## Mental Models
- **Use best responses, not promises**: For every possible rival action, identify the action that maximizes the firm's own payoff.
- **Use the time-horizon test for cooperation**: Compare the one-period gain from cheating with the discounted stream of losses after retaliation.
- **Use commitment only when it changes the entrant's payoff**: A threat to cut price is not credible unless capacity, contracts, or another irreversible action makes it optimal.
- **Use industry-profit accounting for advertising**: Distinguish new demand for the whole industry from demand stolen from a rival.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming a cartel agreement is self-enforcing**: Each member has an incentive to underprice, and high cartel prices attract entry.
- **Confusing a Nash equilibrium with the best collective outcome**: Equilibrium describes incentives, not social efficiency or joint profit maximization.
- **Treating a low-price guarantee as consumer protection by default**: Mutual matching can make high prices the equilibrium and the guarantee an empty promise.
- **Believing an announcement deters entry**: Entrants respond to payoffs, not unsupported claims; credible capacity commitments matter.
- **Calling all advertising productive**: If campaigns mostly reallocate sales and total industry revenue rises less than advertising cost, both firms can be worse off.

## Worked Example
In the textbook's airline duopoly, average cost is $100 per passenger. If both firms form a cartel, they charge $400, serve 60 passengers in total, and split the market at 30 passengers each; each earns `($400 - $100) * 30 = $9,000`. If they compete, each faces a smaller firm-specific demand curve, charges $300, serves 40 passengers, and earns $8,000. But if Jill charges $400 while Jack charges $300, Jill serves only 10 passengers and earns $3,000, while Jack serves 60 and earns $12,000. Low price is therefore Jack's best response whether Jill chooses high or low; Jill reaches the same conclusion. The Nash equilibrium is both firms at the low price, even though both would prefer the cartel cell. A low-price guarantee changes the game by removing the reward from underpricing and can restore the high-price outcome.

## Key Takeaways
1. Measure concentration, then model the strategic action that links firms' payoffs.
2. Use a game tree for sequential decisions and a payoff matrix for simultaneous decisions.
3. Find dominant strategies and best responses before predicting a Nash equilibrium.
4. Treat repeated interaction and retaliation as conditions that can sustain cooperation, not as proof that cooperation is legal or stable.
5. Evaluate entry deterrence by comparing the credible limit-pricing profit with the passive duopoly profit.
6. Distinguish firm-level advertising incentives from the total benefit to the industry.
