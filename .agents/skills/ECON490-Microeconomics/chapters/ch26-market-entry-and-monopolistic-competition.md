# Chapter 26: Market Entry and Monopolistic Competition

## Core Idea
Entry attacks incumbent profit from three sides: it lowers the price, reduces each incumbent's quantity, and raises average cost as fixed costs are spread over fewer units. Monopolistic competition combines many firms, differentiated products, and free entry; in long-run equilibrium firms earn zero economic profit but operate with excess capacity, trading some production efficiency for variety, location, and service.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three-sided entry squeeze**: Use when a profitable rival enters. The incumbent's firm-specific demand shifts left, so its price falls, its quantity per firm falls, and its average cost rises. Profit changes as `profit = (price - average cost) * quantity`.
- **Monopolistic-competition equilibrium**: Use for markets such as restaurants, clothing, bread, gasoline, and retail services. Many firms sell close but imperfect substitutes, each has a downward-sloping demand curve, and no artificial entry barrier remains. Entry continues until `MR = MC` and `price = ATC`, so economic profit is zero.
- **Variety-versus-cost trade-off**: Use when comparing differentiated competition with perfect competition. Differentiation can raise average cost above its minimum and create excess capacity, but it supplies variety, convenience, service, and shorter travel distances that consumers value.
- **Location-differentiation framework**: Use when firms sell essentially the same product from different places. Each store has a local monopoly, but nearby alternatives make its demand elastic; entry reduces travel costs while duplicating fixed facilities.
- **Advertising-signaling framework**: Use when advertising supplies little direct product information. A costly campaign can signal expected product quality because an inferior product cannot profitably pay for an expensive campaign if customers do not return.

## Key Concepts
- **Monopolistic competition**: A market with many firms selling slightly different products.
- **Product differentiation**: Distinguishing a product by performance, appearance, service, or location.
- **Firm-specific demand**: Demand facing one differentiated firm after consumers choose among close substitutes.
- **Entry squeeze**: Profit reduction caused by lower price, lower firm output, and higher average cost after entry.
- **Zero economic profit**: Revenue covers all explicit and implicit opportunity costs.
- **Excess capacity**: Operation below the minimum point of average cost.
- **Variety**: Consumer choice among differentiated products.
- **Location differentiation**: Product differentiation created by where a firm operates.
- **Signaling effect**: Information conveyed by a costly action such as advertising or education.
- **Natural monopoly**: A market where scale economies prevent multiple profitable firms from entering.

## Mental Models
- **Use the three-sided squeeze before celebrating entry**: Check price, firm quantity, and average cost, not only the number of firms.
- **Use the zero-profit condition as an entry stop signal**: New firms enter while expected revenue exceeds economic cost; they stop when `P = ATC`.
- **Use variety as a real benefit, not a slogan**: Compare duplicated production cost with consumer gains from choice, quality, convenience, and lower travel.
- **Use advertising cost as a signal-strength test**: Ask whether an inferior product could recoup the campaign cost through repeat purchases.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming free entry makes the market perfectly competitive**: Differentiated products leave each firm with market power and `MR < P`.
- **Calling zero economic profit a business failure**: The firm covers the opportunity cost of the entrepreneur and all inputs.
- **Counting firms without examining substitutability**: A new firm matters through the shift and elasticity of each incumbent's demand.
- **Treating all advertising as wasteful persuasion**: Some advertising conveys prices, product attributes, or a credible quality signal.
- **Ignoring excess capacity**: A differentiated firm can have zero profit while operating above minimum ATC because variety is costly.

## Worked Example
The toothbrush example starts with one firm selling 300 brushes per minute at $2.00 and average cost of $0.90, yielding `($2.00 - $0.90) * 300 = $330` profit. When a second differentiated toothbrush enters, the first firm's demand shifts left. Its profit-maximizing output falls to 200 brushes, price falls to $1.80, and average cost rises to $1.00, so profit becomes `($1.80 - $1.00) * 200 = $160`. Entry therefore squeezes profit from the top through price, from the right through quantity, and from the bottom through average cost. In long-run monopolistic competition, further entry continues until price equals average cost and profit is zero, but the market still supplies more variety than a single standardized firm would.

## Key Takeaways
1. Model entry as a shift in firm-specific demand, not simply as a larger market supply.
2. Track price, quantity per firm, average cost, and total variety after entry.
3. In long-run monopolistic competition, impose both `MR = MC` and `P = ATC`.
4. Expect price to exceed marginal cost and average cost to exceed its minimum because products are differentiated.
5. Evaluate advertising by incremental repeat-customer profit and signaling credibility.
6. Compare variety and convenience benefits with the production cost of many small firms.
