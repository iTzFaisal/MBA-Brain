# Chapter 6: Unemployment and Inflation

## Core Idea
Unemployment and inflation are measured concepts, not everyday impressions. The labor-force definitions determine who is counted as unemployed, the natural rate separates normal matching and skill mismatch from cyclical weakness, and price indexes convert nominal amounts into purchasing power. Both unemployment and inflation impose economic and social costs, but their causes and policy implications differ.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Labor-market accounting**: Use consistent population categories. `Labor force = employed + unemployed`; `unemployment rate = unemployed / labor force * 100`; `labor force participation rate = labor force / population age 16+ * 100`. A person without a job counts as unemployed only if actively seeking work.
- **Alternative unemployment measures**: Use the official rate for the standard labor-market comparison, but inspect discouraged workers, other marginally attached workers, and people working part time for economic reasons when the question concerns unused labor capacity.
- **Categories and natural rate of unemployment**: Use cyclical unemployment for GDP fluctuations, frictional unemployment for job search and matching, and structural unemployment for skill-job mismatch. The natural rate contains frictional plus structural unemployment and therefore remains positive at full employment.
- **Consumer Price Index and real-nominal analysis**: Use a fixed representative basket to compare the cost of living with a base year: `CPI in year K = cost of basket in year K / cost of basket in base year * 100`. Inflation is the percentage change in a price index, not the absolute price level.
- **Inflation-cost diagnosis**: Distinguish anticipated inflation, which creates menu costs, shoe-leather costs, and tax distortions, from unanticipated inflation, which redistributes purchasing power between debtors and creditors and can disrupt contracts.

## Key Concepts
- **Labor force**: Employed plus unemployed people.
- **Unemployment rate**: The percentage of the labor force that is unemployed.
- **Labor force participation rate**: The percentage of the population age 16 and older in the labor force.
- **Discouraged worker**: A person who wants work but stopped looking because a job seemed unavailable.
- **Frictional unemployment**: Short-term unemployment associated with search and normal job changes.
- **Structural unemployment**: Unemployment caused by a mismatch between worker skills and available jobs.
- **Cyclical unemployment**: Unemployment associated with fluctuations in real GDP.
- **Natural rate of unemployment**: Unemployment at full employment, consisting of frictional and structural unemployment.
- **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**: A price index based on the cost of a fixed basket representing a typical consumer.
- **Inflation/deflation**: A rise/fall in the general price level; inflation is a rate of change, not a high price level.

## Mental Models
- **Use the status test before calculating unemployment**: Ask whether the person worked, is actively searching, or has left the labor force; do not infer status from desire alone.
- **Use the composition test when unemployment changes**: Separate cyclical, frictional, structural, and seasonal components before choosing a remedy.
- **Use purchasing-power conversion for pay comparisons**: Multiply a historical amount by the ratio of current CPI to historical CPI.
- **Use expected-versus-actual inflation to locate transfers**: Unexpectedly high inflation favors borrowers and harms lenders on existing nominal contracts; the reverse holds for unexpectedly low inflation.

## Anti-patterns
- **Counting every nonworker as unemployed**: Retirees, students not seeking work, and discouraged workers are outside the official labor force.
- **Treating a falling unemployment rate as unambiguous improvement**: The rate can fall because people stop searching and leave the labor force.
- **Calling high prices inflation**: Inflation is the percentage change in the price level; a high but stable rent is not current inflation.
- **Comparing salaries in nominal dollars across years**: A larger paycheck may buy fewer goods if consumer prices rose faster.
- **Assuming the CPI is a perfect cost-of-living measure**: A fixed basket misses substitution, new products, and quality improvements and tends to overstate inflation.

## Worked Example
Consider a worker deciding whether to move from Dallas to Oakland. The regional price-parity index was 101 for Dallas and 121.3 for Oakland, so Oakland's cost of living was roughly 20 percent higher. A salary offer in Oakland must therefore be about 20 percent higher merely to preserve the worker's real earning power: compare `salary_Oakland / 121.3` with `salary_Dallas / 101`, rather than comparing nominal salaries. Merced's index was 95.8, illustrating that a lower nominal salary in a cheaper location can still provide greater purchasing power.

## Key Takeaways
1. Calculate the labor force before calculating either unemployment or participation.
2. Report alternative measures when discouraged, marginally attached, or involuntary part-time workers matter.
3. Diagnose whether unemployment is cyclical, frictional, structural, or seasonal before proposing policy.
4. Convert nominal wages, benefits, and debts into real purchasing power with a relevant price index.
5. Distinguish anticipated inflation costs from the arbitrary redistributions caused by unexpected inflation.
