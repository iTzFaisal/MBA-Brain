# Chapter 8: Why Do Economies Grow?

## Core Idea
Long-run living standards rise through real GDP per capita growth. The textbook emphasizes two primary mechanisms: capital deepening, which increases capital per worker, and technological progress, which produces more output from given inputs. Human capital, incentives, institutions, and property rights determine whether saving and new ideas become sustained growth.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Growth-rate and rule-of-70 framework**: Use real GDP per capita to compare living standards across populations. `growth rate = (new value - old value) / old value * 100`; with a constant percentage growth rate, `years to double = 70 / percentage growth rate`. Small persistent rate differences compound into large welfare differences.
- **Capital deepening and the Solow logic**: Use when asking how saving and investment change output per worker. In a simple economy, `C + S = Y` and `C + I = Y`, so `S = I`; the capital stock changes by `gross investment - depreciation`. With diminishing returns, capital deepening eventually slows unless technology continues to improve.
- **Technological progress and growth accounting**: Use `Y = F(K, L, A)`, where `A` captures efficiency. Attribute observed output growth first to capital and labor; the residual is technological progress. Growth accounting identifies contributions without claiming that the residual is one particular invention.
- **Sources of technological progress**: Evaluate research and development, temporary monopoly rewards and creative destruction, market scale and trade, induced innovation responding to high input costs, education and human capital, and new growth theory's effort to explain technology inside the growth model.
- **Institutional growth framework**: Use when growth is weak despite aid, resources, or education. Secure property rights, credible contracts, finance, and incentives let people invest, innovate, and adopt technology; without them, valuable assets remain informal and productive investment is discouraged.

## Key Concepts
- **Real GDP per capita**: Real GDP per person, adjusted for price changes and used as a standard living-level measure.
- **Growth rate**: Percentage change in a variable from one period to another.
- **Rule of 70**: Approximation that output doubles in `70 / x` years when it grows at `x` percent per year.
- **Convergence**: Poorer countries growing faster and narrowing the real GDP-per-capita gap with richer countries.
- **Capital deepening**: An increase in the stock of capital per worker.
- **Saving**: Income not consumed.
- **Technological progress**: More efficient organization that raises output without proportional increases in inputs.
- **Human capital**: Knowledge and skills acquired through education and experience and used in production.
- **Growth accounting**: Method estimating the contributions of capital, labor, and technology to growth.
- **Creative destruction**: Innovation that displaces existing products or production methods while creating new ones.

## Mental Models
- **Use the compounding test for growth claims**: Convert a one-year rate into a doubling time before judging whether a small difference matters.
- **Use the capital-per-worker test**: More total capital is not enough; compare capital with the labor force and depreciation.
- **Use the residual carefully**: If capital and labor explain only part of output growth, investigate technology, measurement, energy prices, and institutional changes before labeling the remainder innovation.
- **Use the incentive test for development policy**: Ask whether people can keep the returns from education, investment, invention, and property improvements.

## Anti-patterns
- **Using nominal GDP or total GDP as a living-standard measure**: Population size and inflation can hide falling real output per person.
- **Treating capital deepening as an unlimited growth engine**: Diminishing returns eventually make each added unit of capital less productive.
- **Calling every new device technological progress**: The relevant test is higher output or efficiency from given inputs, not novelty alone.
- **Assuming a trade deficit automatically deepens capital**: Borrowing to buy consumer goods raises current consumption without creating future output to repay the debt.
- **Searching for one magic development intervention**: Aid, machinery, schooling, and population policies fail when property rights and incentives are missing.

## Worked Example
An Indian immunization program illustrates incentive design and evidence-based development. Health officials observed that parents often began, but did not complete, a sequence of child vaccinations. In a controlled intervention described by Duflo and Banerjee, parents received a small amount of dal after each shot and cooking pans after completing the sequence. The incentives substantially increased completion. The lesson is not that food rewards solve development generally; it is that a narrow behavioral constraint can be tested with a targeted incentive, and the social benefit of vaccination can justify the cost when the intervention changes completion rather than merely initial intent.

## Key Takeaways
1. Compare living standards with real GDP per capita and keep growth rates in compounded form.
2. Use saving and investment to build capital per worker, but account for depreciation and diminishing returns.
3. Treat continuing technological progress as necessary for sustained per-capita growth.
4. Use growth accounting to separate capital, labor, and efficiency contributions before choosing policy.
5. Secure property rights, credible institutions, education, and incentives are productive inputs, not background details.
