# Chapter 19: The World of International Finance

## Core Idea
Exchange rates are prices determined by demand and supply in foreign-exchange markets, but the economically relevant price is often the real exchange rate after adjusting for national price levels. International goods transactions and asset transactions are two sides of one balance-of-payments system, while fixed exchange rates trade flexibility for policy discipline and reserve requirements.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Foreign-exchange supply-and-demand model**: Use when explaining an appreciation or depreciation. Demand for dollars comes from foreigners buying U.S. goods or assets; supply of dollars comes from U.S. residents buying foreign goods or assets. Higher U.S. interest rates or lower U.S. prices shift dollar demand right and appreciate the dollar; higher foreign interest rates or lower foreign prices shift dollar supply right and depreciate it.
- **Real-nominal exchange-rate framework**: Use when nominal currency movements and inflation move together. The textbook formula is `real exchange rate = exchange rate * U.S. price index / foreign price index`. A nominal appreciation or higher domestic prices makes domestic goods relatively more expensive; the relevant consequence is usually lower net exports.
- **Purchasing-power-parity and law-of-one-price test**: Use for a rough valuation of currencies and tradable goods. The law of one price predicts equal common-currency prices for easily traded goods; purchasing power parity extends that intuition to a representative basket, but nontraded goods, transport costs, and market segmentation make it imperfect.
- **Balance-of-payments accounting**: Use to connect trade deficits with international asset ownership. `current account + financial account + capital account = 0`. A current-account deficit is offset by a financial-account surplus when foreigners acquire domestic assets or currency.
- **Fixed-versus-flexible exchange-rate framework**: Use when comparing currency regimes. Flexible rates absorb shocks through currency prices. A fixed rate can simplify trade but requires intervention, compatible inflation and interest rates, and enough foreign reserves; persistent imbalances eventually force devaluation or revaluation.
- **Currency-crisis mechanism**: Use when a pegged currency is supported by fragile capital flows or foreign-currency debt. Investor withdrawal forces the central bank to spend reserves; devaluation then raises the domestic burden of dollar-denominated debt and can trigger bankruptcy and further capital flight.

## Key Concepts
- **Exchange rate**: The price at which one currency trades for another.
- **Appreciation**: An increase in a currency's value relative to another currency.
- **Depreciation**: A decrease in a currency's value relative to another currency.
- **Real exchange rate**: The relative price of domestic and foreign goods expressed in a common currency.
- **Purchasing power parity**: The theory that one unit of currency should buy the same quantity of goods in all countries.
- **Current account**: Net exports plus net income from abroad plus net transfers from abroad.
- **Financial account**: Net sales of existing financial and produced assets.
- **Capital account**: Capital transfers and transactions in nonproduced, nonfinancial assets.
- **Fixed exchange-rate system**: A system in which governments peg currency values.
- **Foreign-exchange intervention**: Government purchases or sales of currencies to influence an exchange rate.

## Mental Models
- **Use the currency-price convention first**: State whether the exchange rate is foreign currency per dollar or dollars per foreign currency before interpreting appreciation.
- **Use the real-price test for trade effects**: Ask whether domestic goods became more or less expensive relative to foreign goods after adjusting for inflation, not merely whether the currency moved.
- **Use the accounting mirror**: Treat a current-account deficit as the counterpart of foreigners acquiring domestic assets rather than as an unexplained leak of money.
- **Use the reserve-and-liability test for a peg**: A fixed rate is credible only if the government can meet excess currency supply and borrowers can survive a possible devaluation.

## Anti-patterns
- **Saying both currencies appreciate in the same bilateral movement**: If the dollar appreciates against the euro, the euro depreciates against the dollar.
- **Using nominal exchange rates as purchasing-power measures**: A currency can depreciate while domestic inflation rises enough to leave the real exchange rate unchanged.
- **Treating a current-account deficit as proof of economic failure**: It can finance productive investment, but it also means foreigners acquire claims on domestic assets; inspect sustainability and returns.
- **Assuming purchasing-power parity is exact**: Housing, haircuts, and other nontraded goods prevent common-currency prices from equalizing.
- **Ignoring currency denomination in crisis analysis**: A devaluation helps exports but can sharply increase the domestic value of foreign-currency debts.

## Worked Example
The Big Mac application uses a simple purchasing-power comparison. In January 2015 a Big Mac cost $4.79 in the United States and 6.5 Swiss francs in Switzerland. The exchange rate implied by equal common-currency prices is `6.5 / 4.79 = 1.36 Swiss francs per U.S. dollar`. The actual market exchange rate was about 0.86 Swiss francs per dollar, so the Swiss franc was much more expensive than the Big Mac benchmark predicted and the burger cost more in Switzerland when converted to dollars. The source cautions that Switzerland's safe-haven status and the stress in the euro area can move the market rate away from a simple purchasing-power estimate. The example is a clue, not a complete exchange-rate model.

## Key Takeaways
1. Define the exchange-rate units before reasoning about appreciation or depreciation.
2. Trace exchange-rate changes through import prices, export prices, and net exports.
3. Adjust nominal rates for domestic and foreign price levels when the question concerns competitiveness or purchasing power.
4. Record goods, income, transfers, and asset transactions in the correct balance-of-payments account.
5. Expect a peg to require intervention and reserve use whenever private supply and demand do not match the fixed rate.
6. Treat foreign-currency borrowing as a crisis vulnerability when devaluation is possible.
