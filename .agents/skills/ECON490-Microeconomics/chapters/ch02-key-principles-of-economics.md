# Chapter 2: The Key Principles of Economics

## Core Idea
Five principles provide the recurring decision rules for the textbook: opportunity cost, the marginal principle, voluntary exchange, diminishing returns, and the real-nominal principle. Together they turn scarcity into practical comparisons of alternatives, incremental benefits and costs, productivity, exchange gains, and purchasing power.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Principle of opportunity cost**: Use whenever a resource, dollar, hour, or asset is assigned to one activity. The opportunity cost is what you sacrifice to get it, specifically the best forgone alternative. Include both explicit spending and implicit costs such as forgone wages and interest on invested funds. A production possibilities curve makes the economy-wide trade-off visible and shifts outward with more resources or better technology.
- **Marginal principle**: Use for a choice about how much more or less to do, not an all-or-nothing question. Increase an activity while marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost; choose the level at which marginal benefit equals marginal cost. The relevant cost is the additional cost caused by the next unit, not sunk or unavoidable fixed costs.
- **Principle of voluntary exchange**: Use when two informed parties freely trade. A voluntary exchange makes both parties better off ex ante because each values what it receives more than what it gives up. Markets let people specialize and exchange rather than produce everything themselves.
- **Principle of diminishing returns**: Use when one input increases while at least one other input remains fixed. Beyond some point, output rises at a decreasing rate because additional units must share the fixed facility, machine, land, or other input. It does not apply in the same way when all inputs can expand together.
- **Real-nominal principle**: Use whenever money values are compared across goods or time. What matters is purchasing power, or real value, rather than the face value of money, or nominal value. Compare wages, loans, benefits, and prices using the goods they command.

## Key Concepts
- **Opportunity cost**: The best alternative sacrificed to obtain something.
- **Marginal benefit**: The additional benefit from a small increase in an activity.
- **Marginal cost**: The additional cost from a small increase in an activity.
- **Production possibilities curve (PPC)**: The combinations of goods attainable with fully employed and efficiently used resources.
- **Voluntary exchange**: A freely chosen trade expected to benefit both participants.
- **Diminishing returns**: Output increases at a decreasing rate after one input is expanded with other inputs fixed.
- **Nominal value**: The face-dollar value of money or income.
- **Real value**: The purchasing power of money or income.
- **Purchasing power**: The quantity of goods and services an amount of money can buy.

## Mental Models
- **Use the best-alternative ledger when costing a decision**: List cash outlays, forgone earnings, forgone interest, and the best nonchosen use of the same resource.
- **Use MB versus MC for the next unit**: Continue only while the next unit adds at least as much benefit as cost; stop when the comparison reverses.
- **Use comparative purchasing power when nominal values change**: Divide income by the price of a representative basket instead of inferring welfare from dollars alone.
- **Use the fixed-input check before invoking diminishing returns**: Ask which input cannot adjust over the relevant time horizon.

## Anti-patterns
- **Calling something free because no money changes hands**: Time, forgone wages, and attention still have opportunity costs.
- **Using total or average cost for an incremental decision**: Fixed costs that do not change with the decision are not marginal costs.
- **Specializing according to who is absolutely better**: Exchange gains depend on lower opportunity cost, not merely higher productivity.
- **Expecting output to double when one input doubles**: A fixed bottleneck can create diminishing returns.
- **Reading nominal wage growth as real improvement**: If prices rise faster than wages, purchasing power falls.

## Worked Example
The movie-sequel example applies the marginal principle. The first movie has marginal benefit of $300 million and marginal cost of $125 million, so it adds $175 million in value. The second has benefit of $210 million and cost of $150 million, adding $60 million. The third has benefit of only $135 million but cost of $175 million, losing $40 million. The producer should make two movies, not three: the third unit fails the `MB >= MC` test even though the first two were profitable. The same logic explains why a college should rent an otherwise vacant auditorium for $150 when the extra electricity and janitorial cost is only $100; construction and insurance costs are sunk for that day's decision.

## Key Takeaways
1. Record the best forgone use of time, money, and owned assets as part of economic cost.
2. Make incremental decisions by comparing marginal benefit with marginal cost.
3. Look for gains from specialization even when one party has an absolute advantage in everything.
4. Apply diminishing returns only across a relevant fixed input and time horizon.
5. Convert nominal wages, benefits, and debts into purchasing power before judging who is better off.
