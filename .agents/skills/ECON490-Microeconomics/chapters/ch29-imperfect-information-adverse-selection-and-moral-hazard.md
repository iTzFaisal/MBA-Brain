# Chapter 29: Imperfect Information: Adverse Selection and Moral Hazard

## Core Idea
When one side of a market knows more than the other, prices may no longer coordinate quality and risk. Adverse selection occurs before a transaction as the uninformed side attracts an undesirable mix of goods or customers; moral hazard occurs after a transaction when hidden actions change the probability or cost of an outcome. Information, guarantees, group contracts, deductibles, and search can partly restore exchange.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Lemons downward spiral**: Use when sellers know product quality and buyers cannot distinguish it. A pooled price reflects average expected quality; low-quality sellers are more willing to sell at that price, high-quality sellers withdraw, average quality falls, and buyers lower their willingness to pay again. The extreme equilibrium contains only lemons; a less severe outcome is a thin market with fewer high-quality goods than under full information.
- **Information-and-signaling response**: Use when a quality gap creates gains from identifying good products. Buyers can pay for inspections, ratings, histories, and model information. Sellers can signal quality with warranties, money-back guarantees, repair commitments, licensing, or minimum standards that are less attractive to low-quality sellers.
- **Insurance adverse-selection spiral**: Use when buyers know their risk better than insurers. If a single premium is based on an assumed average mix, high-risk customers are more likely to buy, raising actual average cost and forcing a higher premium that drives out low-risk customers. Group insurance, experience rating, mandates, and better risk information can broaden the pool.
- **Moral-hazard framework**: Use after insurance or another contract changes incentives. When the insured party's action is hidden, coverage reduces the private cost of risk-taking. Deductibles, copayments, monitoring, restrictions, and experience-based pricing make the decision maker bear more of the marginal cost.
- **Optimal-search rule**: Use when prices vary and information is costly. Continue searching while expected savings from the next visit exceed its opportunity cost. The reservation price is where `marginal benefit of search = marginal cost of search`; stop when the discovered price is at or below that threshold.

## Key Concepts
- **Asymmetric information**: One market side has better information than the other.
- **Mixed market**: High- and low-quality goods sell together at one price.
- **Adverse-selection problem**: The uninformed side receives an undesirable mix of goods or customers.
- **Thin market**: Some high-quality goods trade, but fewer than under full information.
- **Guarantee**: Seller promise that refunds or covers repair costs if quality is poor.
- **Experience rating**: Insurance price based on an employer group's past claims.
- **Moral hazard**: Hidden action after a contract creates costly risk-taking.
- **Deductible**: Claim amount paid by the insured before coverage begins.
- **Discovered price**: Lowest price observed so far during search.
- **Reservation price**: Discovered price at which another search visit is just worthwhile.

## Mental Models
- **Use the quality-pool test before trusting an average price**: Ask who enters or exits when the pooled price changes and whether the average quality assumption is self-confirming.
- **Use costly guarantees as signals**: A warranty is credible when a low-quality seller expects more claims and cannot profitably mimic a high-quality seller.
- **Use the contract-timing test**: Diagnose adverse selection from private information before agreement and moral hazard from hidden behavior after agreement.
- **Use expected savings, not the hope of finding the absolute minimum**: Search until `Pr(lower price) * expected savings if lower < discovered price` falls to the opportunity cost of another visit.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming a mixed-market price is a neutral average**: The price changes the composition of sellers or buyers, so the average can move against the original expectation.
- **Solving lemons problems only with disclosure mandates**: Information must be credible and usable; inspections, ratings, warranties, and standards may work better.
- **Pricing insurance from population risk alone**: The insured pool can be riskier than the population when low-risk people opt out.
- **Treating moral hazard as dishonesty alone**: It is an incentive problem created by hidden actions and incomplete cost bearing.
- **Searching until the lowest possible price**: Extra visits have opportunity costs and often produce less expected saving as the discovered price falls.

## Worked Example
The textbook's television-search example gives a usable stopping rule. Prices range uniformly from $100 to $180, and a store visit costs $0.90 in forgone time. At a discovered price of $140, half of stores have lower prices; the expected lower price is the midpoint, $120, so expected savings are `0.50 * ($140 - $120) = $10`, well above cost. At $120, the probability of a lower price is 0.25 and the expected saving conditional on finding one is $10, so the marginal benefit is $2.50. At $110, the probability is 0.125 and the expected gap is $5, giving only $0.625, below the $0.90 search cost. Solving the equality gives a reservation price of $112: continue above $112, stop at or below it.

## Key Takeaways
1. Identify which side has private information and whether the problem occurs before or after contracting.
2. Trace how a pooled price changes the composition of sellers or customers.
3. Use inspections, ratings, guarantees, warranties, standards, and group contracts to reveal or pool quality and risk.
4. Make insured parties bear some marginal cost through deductibles, copayments, monitoring, or experience rating.
5. Compute a reservation price from expected savings and search cost rather than pursuing perfect information.
6. Remember that opportunity cost of time rises with income, product price, and the range of possible prices in different ways.
