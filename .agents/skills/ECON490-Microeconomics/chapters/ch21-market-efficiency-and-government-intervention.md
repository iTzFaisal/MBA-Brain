# Chapter 21: Market Efficiency and Government Intervention

## Core Idea
In a market with no external benefits or costs, adequate information, and perfect competition, equilibrium maximizes total surplus because every mutually beneficial transaction occurs. Government intervention can redistribute surplus, but price controls, quantity controls, and taxes generally shrink the total surplus unless they correct a market failure or pursue an explicitly valued distributional goal.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Surplus accounting**: Use to measure the value created by a market. Consumer surplus is willingness to pay minus the price paid; producer surplus is the price received minus marginal cost or willingness to accept. `total surplus = consumer surplus + producer surplus`.
- **Four-condition invisible-hand test**: Use before calling a competitive equilibrium efficient. Check for no external benefits, no external costs, perfect information, and perfect competition. If all four hold, the equilibrium quantity is where the marginal buyer's willingness to pay matches the marginal seller's cost.
- **Price-control framework**: Use to analyze a binding ceiling or floor. A maximum price below equilibrium creates excess demand, lowers quantity supplied, and prevents high-value transactions. A minimum price above equilibrium creates excess supply and prevents low-cost suppliers from serving willing buyers. The lost surplus is deadweight loss.
- **Quantity-control framework**: Use for licenses, medallions, and import restrictions. Fixing the quantity shifts the market along demand, raises price, transfers surplus to license holders or protected producers, and destroys transactions between excluded buyers and sellers.
- **Tax-shifting and excess-burden framework**: Use when the legal taxpayer is not necessarily the economic bearer. A unit tax shifts supply upward; the price increase shifts part forward to consumers and the lower input price shifts part backward to suppliers. Because behavior changes, the total burden exceeds tax revenue by the deadweight loss.

## Key Concepts
- **Efficiency**: Doing the best possible given limited resources.
- **Willingness to pay**: The maximum amount a consumer will pay for a product.
- **Consumer surplus**: Willingness to pay minus the price actually paid.
- **Willingness to accept**: The minimum payment a producer will accept, equal to marginal cost.
- **Producer surplus**: Price received minus marginal cost.
- **Total surplus**: The sum of consumer and producer surplus.
- **Price ceiling**: A government-set maximum price.
- **Price floor**: A government-set minimum price.
- **Deadweight loss**: The decrease in total surplus caused by a policy that prevents mutually beneficial transactions.
- **Tax shifting**: The movement of a tax burden to consumers and input suppliers through market prices.

## Mental Models
- **Use the marginal buyer-seller test**: A transaction is socially valuable when willingness to pay exceeds the seller's marginal cost; the equilibrium stops exactly when the next transaction would destroy value.
- **Use the pie-versus-slices distinction**: Ask separately whether a policy changes who receives surplus and whether it changes the total amount of surplus.
- **Use the legal-versus-economic taxpayer test**: Follow the supply-and-demand shifts rather than the name on the tax remittance form.
- **Use the excluded-transaction test for controls**: Identify buyers willing to pay more than excluded sellers' costs; their lost gains are the policy's deadweight loss.

## Anti-patterns
- **Calling a market efficient merely because it is competitive**: Externalities, information gaps, and market power can invalidate the invisible-hand result.
- **Treating consumer gains from a price ceiling as a net social gain**: Some are transfers from producers, while transactions that disappear create a loss to both sides.
- **Assuming a license protects consumers by itself**: Licensing can raise prices without improving quality; compare a direct quality rule with a quantity restriction.
- **Equating tax revenue with the total tax burden**: Consumers and producers also lose the surplus from trades that no longer occur.
- **Using redistribution as evidence that a policy improves efficiency**: A policy may be desirable for equity while still making the market pie smaller.

## Worked Example
The textbook's softwood-lumber application illustrates the distributional cost of protection. Import quotas and tariffs reduce the supply of lumber available to domestic buyers, raising the price of lumber used in housing and other products. The policy protects roughly 600 jobs in the domestic softwood-lumber industry but imposes about $632 million per year in costs on consumers. The implied cost is approximately `$632 million / 600 = $1.05 million per job protected each year`. That calculation does not decide the political question, but it makes the opportunity cost visible: consumers pay higher prices and lose some consumer surplus, while protected workers and producers gain. A direct transition policy for displaced workers could target the same distributional concern with less damage to the efficient market outcome.

## Key Takeaways
1. Compute consumer, producer, and total surplus before judging a market policy.
2. Check the four efficiency conditions before invoking the invisible hand.
3. A binding price ceiling creates shortage; a binding price floor creates surplus.
4. Quantity restrictions raise price and reduce transactions even when they are framed as consumer protection.
5. Tax incidence depends on relative elasticities, not on who legally pays the government.
6. When equity is the objective, compare direct transfers or training with interventions that distort an otherwise efficient market.
