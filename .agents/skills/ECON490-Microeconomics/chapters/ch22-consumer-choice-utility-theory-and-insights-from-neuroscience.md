# Chapter 22: Consumer Choice: Utility Theory and Insights from Neuroscience

## Core Idea
Consumers choose the highest-utility affordable bundle, allocating spending until marginal utility per dollar is equal across goods. The traditional model explains the law of demand through substitution and income effects; neuroscience adds a practical account of gut-feeling benefits and costs, cognition, present bias, and commitment devices that shape choices over food, saving, credit, and risk.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Budget-line and budget-set framework**: Use when income and prices constrain a consumer. The budget line contains bundles that exhaust income; the budget set includes all affordable bundles. Its slope is the opportunity cost, the price ratio of the horizontal good to the vertical good.
- **Diminishing marginal utility**: Use when additional units of a good generate progressively smaller increments of satisfaction. Total utility can rise while marginal utility falls, so a consumer's willingness to pay generally declines with quantity.
- **Equimarginal rule**: Use to allocate a fixed budget across goods. Choose an affordable bundle satisfying `MU_m / P_m = MU_b / P_b`; if one good delivers more marginal utility per dollar, shift spending toward it until the ratios equalize.
- **Income and substitution effects**: Use to explain a price change. A lower price makes the good relatively cheaper, causing substitution toward it, and raises real purchasing power, causing more purchases of normal goods. In the source example, a movie-price cut from $3 to $2 changes the utility-maximizing bundle from 6 movies and 9 books to 10 movies and 7 books.
- **Neuroscience benefit-cost model**: Use when observed behavior appears impulsive or inconsistent with long-run goals. The nucleus accumbens and dopamine system represent anticipated rewards; the insula and amygdala represent aversion and spending costs; the prefrontal cortex, especially the DLPFC, incorporates consequences and compares the signals.
- **Present-bias and commitment framework**: Use when current benefits are vivid but future costs or benefits are underweighted. Precommitment, automatic saving, reminders, delayed costs, and self-imposed penalties move consequences into the present and reduce impulsive choices.

## Key Concepts
- **Utility**: Satisfaction generated by consuming a bundle of goods.
- **Budget line**: All combinations of two goods that exhaust a consumer's income.
- **Budget set**: All combinations that are affordable, including those below the budget line.
- **Marginal utility**: The additional utility from one more unit of a good.
- **Marginal utility per dollar**: Marginal utility divided by the good's price.
- **Equimarginal rule**: Equalize marginal utility per dollar across uses of a budget.
- **Substitution effect**: Reallocation toward a good whose relative price has fallen.
- **Income effect**: Change in consumption caused by the change in real purchasing power.
- **Present bias**: Underweighting future benefits and costs relative to present consequences.
- **Cognitive fuel**: Energy needed for deliberative, self-controlled decision making.

## Mental Models
- **Use the bang-per-buck comparison when reallocating a budget**: Move the next dollar toward the good with the higher marginal utility per dollar, not the good with the higher total utility.
- **Use the real-income thought experiment to isolate substitution**: Compensate income so the original bundle remains affordable, then observe the relative-price response before adding the income effect.
- **Use gut feelings as data, not as the whole decision**: Let experience inform benefit and cost signals, but use cognition to include delayed health, debt, and opportunity costs.
- **Use commitment when the decision is temporally asymmetric**: Automate saving, display credit-card balances, or impose a penalty before the tempting action occurs.

## Anti-patterns
- **Confusing a budget line with a demand curve**: One describes affordable combinations of goods; the other describes quantity chosen at different prices.
- **Choosing the good with the highest total utility**: Optimization is marginal; compare the next unit's utility per dollar.
- **Claiming a revenue-neutral tax has no behavioral effect**: A gasoline tax changes the relative price even when an income-tax cut preserves the original bundle's affordability.
- **Treating present-biased behavior as simple irrationality**: The model predicts systematic responses to delayed benefits and costs and suggests commitment-based remedies.
- **Assuming more options always improve choice**: Cognitive depletion can make consumers simplify, impulse-buy, or abandon a decision.

## Worked Example
The movies-and-books example starts with a $27 monthly budget, movies priced at $3 and books at $1. At the utility-maximizing bundle of 6 movies and 9 books, the sixth movie has marginal utility of 36, so `MU_m / P_m = 36 / 3 = 12`; the relevant book has marginal utility 12, so `MU_b / P_b = 12 / 1 = 12`. The consumer spends all $27 and satisfies the equimarginal rule. When the movie price falls to $2, the original bundle produces 18 utils per dollar on movies versus 12 on books, so spending shifts toward movies. Holding real income constant first, the substitution effect moves consumption from 6 to 8 movies; restoring the $6 of purchasing power produces the income effect, moving from 8 to 10 movies and from 7 to 9 books. The full response obeys the law of demand.

## Key Takeaways
1. Write the budget constraint and identify the opportunity cost before comparing goods.
2. Equalize marginal utility per dollar across uses of a fixed budget.
3. Decompose price responses into substitution and income effects rather than relying on intuition alone.
4. Distinguish automatic reward and cost signals from deliberate evaluation of future consequences.
5. Reduce present bias with defaults, precommitment, reminders, and penalties that make future consequences salient now.
6. Treat decision fatigue and excessive choice as economic constraints on cognition.
