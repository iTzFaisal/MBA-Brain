# Chapter 15: Modern Macroeconomics: From the Short Run to the Long Run

## Core Idea
The short run is the period in which wages and prices are sticky and demand can move GDP away from potential; the long run is the period in which wages and prices have adjusted and factor supplies and technology determine output. The adjustment process explains natural recovery, policy trade-offs, monetary neutrality, crowding out, liquidity traps, and the modern connection between Keynesian and classical views.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Short-run versus long-run model**: Use short-run AD-SRAS analysis for demand-driven output changes with sticky prices. Use long-run AD-LRAS analysis when wages and prices have fully adjusted. In the long run, fiscal or monetary demand expansion raises prices rather than potential output.
- **Wage-price spiral and natural adjustment**: Use when output differs from potential. Below full employment, unemployment exceeds the natural rate, wages and costs fall, SRAS shifts down, and output returns to `yp`. Above potential, labor scarcity raises wages and prices, SRAS shifts up, and output falls back to `yp`.
- **Policy-speed and liquidity-trap framework**: Compare doing nothing with expansionary or contractionary policy. Active policy can speed recovery when adjustment is slow, but lags can make it destabilizing. At the zero lower bound, nominal rates cannot fall enough to complete the natural adjustment.
- **Long-run neutrality of money**: Use to distinguish nominal from real effects. A money-supply increase initially lowers rates, raises investment, and pushes output above potential; rising prices later raise money demand, restore rates and investment, and leave only a higher price level.
- **Long-run crowding out/in**: Use when government spending, taxes, or other demand changes occur at full employment. Higher demand raises prices and money demand, raises interest rates, and crowds out private investment. Lower demand does the reverse and can crowd investment in.
- **Classical versus Keynesian adjustment**: Say's law claims supply creates its own demand under flexible wages and prices; Keynes emphasizes persistent demand shortfalls when prices are sticky. The adjustment-process model reconciles the views across time, while liquidity traps and secular stagnation challenge automatic recovery.

## Key Concepts
- **Short run in macroeconomics**: Period when wages and prices change little.
- **Long run in macroeconomics**: Period when wages and prices have fully adjusted.
- **Wage-price spiral**: Rising wages raise costs and prices, which induce further wage demands, or the reverse.
- **Potential output**: Full-employment output determined by factors and technology.
- **Liquidity trap/zero lower bound**: Situation in which nominal interest rates cannot fall further.
- **Political business cycle**: Pre-election stimulation followed by later inflation or contraction.
- **Long-run neutrality of money**: Money supply has no long-run effect on real rates, investment, or output.
- **Crowding out/in**: Long-run reduction/increase in private spending caused by higher/lower government demand.
- **Say's law**: Classical doctrine that supply creates its own demand.
- **Secular stagnation**: Persistent insufficient demand when rates cannot fall enough to restore full employment.

## Mental Models
- **Use the time-horizon switch**: Before forecasting, state whether wages, prices, contracts, and expectations can adjust.
- **Use the below/above-potential rule**: Below potential, expect falling wage and price pressure; above potential, expect a wage-price spiral.
- **Use the neutrality check**: If a policy changes only money and not capital, labor, or technology, expect a long-run price effect rather than permanent real growth.
- **Use the policy-risk clock**: Compare the policy's outside lag with the likely speed of natural adjustment before acting.

## Anti-patterns
- **Using a short-run multiplier as a permanent GDP forecast**: Long-run wage and price adjustment can erase the real-output effect.
- **Assuming automatic adjustment is always fast or guaranteed**: Liquidity traps, falling-price debt burdens, and secular stagnation can delay recovery.
- **Calling all higher nominal wages a real gain**: Prices may rise with wages, leaving real wages unchanged.
- **Ignoring crowding out at full employment**: Fixed output means additional government demand must displace another use unless productive capacity changes.
- **Treating Keynesian and classical models as mutually exclusive**: They describe different adjustment horizons and assumptions about flexibility.

## Worked Example
Suppose a recession leaves output at `y0 < yp`. Unemployment rises above the natural rate, firms can hire easily, wages fall, and lower wages reduce production costs. The short-run aggregate-supply curve shifts downward in stages. Lower prices also reduce money demand, lower interest rates, and raise investment, moving the economy along AD toward `yp`. At full employment, the wage-price decline stops. If policymakers instead use expansionary monetary or fiscal policy, AD shifts right and recovery may be faster, but the eventual price level is higher. If the policy arrives after the natural adjustment, it can overshoot and create a boom.

## Key Takeaways
1. Identify whether a question is short-run demand analysis or long-run capacity analysis.
2. Below potential, falling wages and prices shift SRAS down; above potential, rising wages and prices shift it up.
3. Use money demand, interest rates, and investment to explain how price adjustment restores output.
4. Expect money to affect real output temporarily but prices permanently in the long run.
5. Treat policy timing, the zero lower bound, and crowding out as central constraints, not footnotes.
