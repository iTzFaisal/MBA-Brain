# Actionable Patterns

## Opportunity-Cost Ledger
**When to use**: Comparing projects, jobs, policies, or uses of an owned resource.
**How**: List cash costs, forgone wages, forgone interest, time, and the best alternative use; then compare total benefits with the full opportunity cost.
**Trade-offs**: More complete accounting improves the choice but may require estimates and a value judgment about nonmarket benefits.

## Comparative-Advantage Allocation
**When to use**: Assigning tasks or evaluating trade.
**How**: Compute each producer's opportunity cost for each good, specialize in the lower-cost good, and choose a terms-of-trade ratio between the two opportunity costs.
**Trade-offs**: Total output and consumption possibilities rise, but adjustment can concentrate losses among displaced workers or regions.

## Movement-versus-Shift Test
**When to use**: Explaining a price or quantity change in a market.
**How**: Own-price change means movement along the curve; income, tastes, related prices, technology, input costs, taxes, expectations, or market size shift the curve. Use shortage and surplus to predict adjustment.
**Trade-offs**: One-shift predictions are clean; simultaneous shifts can make price or quantity ambiguous.

## Elasticity-Revenue Test
**When to use**: Setting a price, forecasting a tax base, or estimating a supply shock.
**How**: Calculate percentage elasticity. With demand, `Ed > 1` means price and revenue move oppositely; `Ed < 1` means they move together. Include substitutes, time, budget share, and both sides' elasticities.
**Trade-offs**: Elasticity is decision-relevant but estimates vary by market definition and time horizon.

## Surplus-and-Incidence Audit
**When to use**: Evaluating controls, taxes, quotas, tariffs, or licensing.
**How**: Draw the initial equilibrium, identify who gains and loses, count transfers separately, then identify excluded mutually beneficial trades as deadweight loss. Follow relative elasticities to locate the economic tax burden.
**Trade-offs**: A direct transfer may target equity with less efficiency loss, but it may be less politically durable or administratively simple.

## Equimarginal Budget Allocation
**When to use**: Choosing a bundle with limited income, time, or attention.
**How**: Write the budget set and shift the next dollar or hour toward the use with the highest `MU/P` until marginal utility per dollar is equal across uses.
**Trade-offs**: The rule assumes measurable marginal comparisons; habit, present bias, and cognitive limits can require commitment devices.

## Firm Output and Shutdown
**When to use**: Deciding output or whether an existing firm operates today.
**How**: A price taker chooses `P = MC`; operate in the short run if `P >= AVC`, and exit in the long run if revenue cannot cover `ATC`. Ignore unrecoverable fixed costs for today's operation decision.
**Trade-offs**: Operating at a loss can preserve a contribution to fixed cost, but persistent losses signal exit or restructuring.

## Monopoly and Price Segmentation
**When to use**: Analyzing a firm with market power or differentiated customer groups.
**How**: Choose quantity at `MR = MC`, read price from demand, and calculate profit. If groups are identifiable and resale is blocked, charge more to less-elastic demand and less to more-elastic demand.
**Trade-offs**: Price discrimination can expand output and recover fixed costs, yet monopoly power can transfer surplus and create deadweight loss.

## Entry and Strategic Response
**When to use**: A rival may enter, firms interact repeatedly, or a merger changes concentration.
**How**: Shift the incumbent's firm-specific demand and track price, quantity, and average cost. In a game, list best responses, dominant strategies, and Nash equilibrium; test whether threats are credible and legal.
**Trade-offs**: Cooperation can raise joint profit but may be unstable or illegal; differentiation creates variety but leaves excess capacity.

## Information-and-Contract Diagnosis
**When to use**: Quality, insurance, warranties, or search outcomes look worse than an average-price model predicts.
**How**: Before contracting, test for adverse selection and use inspection, ratings, guarantees, or group pooling. After contracting, test for moral hazard and use monitoring, deductibles, copayments, or experience rating. Stop search when expected savings fall below time cost.
**Trade-offs**: More information and monitoring improve incentives but consume resources and can exclude valuable high-risk or low-income participants.

## Least-Cost Environmental Control
**When to use**: Choosing pollution abatement or transport pricing.
**How**: Set abatement where marginal benefit equals marginal cost. For a fixed reduction, assign more abatement to firms with lower marginal abatement costs. Use a tax for flexible adjustment and permits for a hard emissions cap.
**Trade-offs**: Taxes give price certainty but uncertain quantity; permits give quantity certainty but a volatile permit price. Uniform standards are predictable but often costly.

## Public-Good Provision Test
**When to use**: A project has broad benefits and voluntary financing is failing.
**How**: Classify rivalry and excludability, add external benefits to private benefits, compare total marginal social benefit with marginal cost, then test free riding, voting incentives, and concentrated lobbying.
**Trade-offs**: Tax finance can provide an efficient nonexcludable good, while government choice can introduce waste, special-interest capture, or poor information.

## GDP and Real-Measure Audit
**When to use**: Comparing output, income, or welfare across time or countries.
**How**: Count current final production once, use value added, check `Y = C + I + G + NX`, and deflate nominal values. Add unpaid work, leisure, distribution, underground activity, and environmental effects before making a welfare claim.
**Trade-offs**: GDP is standardized and useful for production, but no single index captures living standards completely.

## Output-Gap and Policy Clock
**When to use**: Considering fiscal or monetary stabilization.
**How**: Compare actual GDP with potential, choose policy direction, size the dose with the relevant multiplier or transmission chain, and compare inside and outside lags with expected natural adjustment.
**Trade-offs**: Discretion can speed a slow recovery but can arrive late, overshoot, increase debt, or raise inflation; automatic stabilizers are slower to target but timely.

## AD-AS Time-Horizon Switch
**When to use**: A shock changes output, unemployment, and prices.
**How**: In the short run, use sticky-price SRAS and diagnose demand shocks versus supply shocks. In the long run, use vertical LRAS at potential output and trace wage, price, and expectation adjustment.
**Trade-offs**: Short-run stimulus can reduce a recessionary gap, but long-run demand expansion mainly raises prices and can crowd out private spending.

## Present-Value Investment Gate
**When to use**: Comparing a current cost with uncertain future cash flows or choosing between financial assets.
**How**: Discount each future benefit using `PV = K/(1+i)^t`, compare PV with current cost, and use a real rate with real cash flows or a nominal rate with nominal cash flows. Inspect leverage, liquidity mismatch, and correlated risk.
**Trade-offs**: Discounting disciplines optimism, but the result is sensitive to the rate, horizon, probability estimates, and shared shocks.

## Growth and Institutions Test
**When to use**: Explaining persistent differences in living standards.
**How**: Compare real GDP per capita and doubling time (`70 / growth rate`), then separate capital deepening, labor, technology, human capital, and the institutional incentives to invest and innovate.
**Trade-offs**: Capital can raise output quickly but faces diminishing returns; technology and institutions support durable growth but are harder to measure and build.
