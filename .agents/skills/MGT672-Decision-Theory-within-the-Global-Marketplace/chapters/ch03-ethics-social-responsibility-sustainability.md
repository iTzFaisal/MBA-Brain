# Chapter 3: Ethics, Social Responsibility, and Sustainability

## Core Idea
Ethics studies morality and standards of conduct; corporate social responsibility (CSR) is voluntary action to benefit society beyond law and the firm's direct interests. MNCs must adapt to local cultures without using cultural relativism to excuse abuse, and must connect ethical conduct, human rights, governance, environmental stewardship, and economic performance.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Kantian ethics, Aristotelian virtue ethics, utilitarianism, and Eastern philosophy**
  - When to use: Evaluate a difficult international decision such as offshoring, labor sourcing, or market withdrawal.
  - How: Kantian analysis tests whether the decision's maxim could be a universal law, regardless of expected consequences. Aristotelian virtue ethics asks what action expresses and develops good character and human flourishing. Utilitarianism compares total benefits and harms for everyone affected and chooses the greatest good for the greatest number. Eastern philosophy treats people as inseparable from nature and favors an integrated, longer-term view of social and environmental effects.
- **Integrative Social Contracts Theory (ISCT)**
  - When to use: Home-country and host-country moral expectations conflict.
  - How: Respect legitimate local community norms but draw a boundary at universal or near-universal **hyper norms**, such as respect for human life and abstention from cheating, lying, and violence. Use ISCT to avoid both unrestricted relativism and rigid cultural absolutism; recognize that it offers a direction rather than precise answers for every case.
- **Triple bottom line: people, planet, profits**
  - When to use: Build sustainability into strategy, supply chains, product design, and performance evaluation.
  - How: Evaluate social outcomes, environmental impacts, and economic viability together. Patagonia, L'Oreal, and Unilever illustrate how sustainability can support brand value, innovation, customer loyalty, and growth rather than sit outside the business model.
- **Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC)**
  - When to use: Form a hypothesis about how development may change environmental harm.
  - How: Expect an inverted-U relationship in which pollution and resource use rise during early industrialization and decline after an income inflection point. Treat this as a contested hypothesis, not a universal law; later evidence did not generalize it across resources and pollutants.
- **UN Global Compact**
  - When to use: Establish a common global minimum for corporate conduct.
  - How: Use its ten principles covering human rights, labor, environment, and anticorruption: respect and avoid complicity in abuses; freedom of association; no forced labor, child labor, or discrimination; precaution and environmental responsibility; environmentally friendly technology; and opposition to corruption, extortion, and bribery.

## Key Concepts
- **Cultural relativism**: The belief that ethical standards are based on the culture that created them and lack universal application.
- **Business ethics**: The moral code guiding business behavior, with both legal and right-wrong implications.
- **CSR**: Firm actions that benefit society beyond legal requirements and direct firm interests.
- **Human rights**: Basic claims including life, freedom from slavery or torture, expression, and nondiscrimination.
- **Sustainability**: Development that meets human needs without harming future generations.
- **NGOs**: Private not-for-profit organizations serving social, political, or economic interests.
- **Corporate governance**: The system directing and controlling corporations and distributing rights and responsibilities.
- **Fair trade**: A market-based social movement seeking better trading conditions for developing-country producers and sustainability.

## Mental Models
- Use a **compliance floor plus moral screen**: legality is necessary but not sufficient when safety, dignity, or human rights are at stake.
- Treat the supply chain as part of the firm's ethical footprint. Unseen subcontractors can create the same reputational and human consequences as owned operations.
- Use multiple lenses before deciding: principles, character, aggregate consequences, and ecological interdependence.
- Ask whether a CSR action changes operations and incentives or merely produces favorable publicity.

## Anti-patterns
- **"When in Rome, do as the Romans do" without limits**: Cultural relativism can become permission to exploit gaps in labor, safety, or human-rights standards.
- **A code of conduct without controls**: Volkswagen had a formal code yet manipulated emissions testing; policy language cannot substitute for governance and accountability.
- **Treating CSR as disconnected philanthropy**: Social and environmental programs are stronger when embedded in products, supply chains, and measurable operating goals.
- **Ignoring intermediaries**: Agents, suppliers, and joint-venture partners can transfer corruption risk even when headquarters pays nothing directly.

## Worked Example
Consider an investment bank deciding whether to offshore back-office work to India or the Philippines. A Kantian review asks whether the bank would apply the same transparent employment rule wherever it operates and whether the decision fits its code of conduct. A virtue-ethics review asks whether the move supports flourishing communities rather than only executive gain. A utilitarian review counts jobs, lost jobs, income, service quality, and transition costs. An Eastern-philosophy review adds long-term effects on workers, communities, and the natural environment. The manager can then design safeguards: fair selection and severance, training for displaced workers, reliable local employment standards, environmental controls, and transparent reporting.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ethics is about standards of conduct; CSR is action beyond law; sustainability links present operations to future capacity.
2. Use Kantian, virtue, utilitarian, and Eastern lenses to expose different consequences and obligations.
3. ISCT allows local variation within limits set by hyper norms.
4. Human rights and labor conditions extend through the global supply chain, not just the headquarters.
5. Governance, anticorruption controls, NGO pressure, and transparent standards make ethical commitments operational.
