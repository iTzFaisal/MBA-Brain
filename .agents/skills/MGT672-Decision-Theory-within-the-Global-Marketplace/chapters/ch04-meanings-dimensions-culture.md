# Chapter 4: The Meanings and Dimensions of Culture

## Core Idea
Culture is acquired knowledge used to interpret experience and generate social behavior. It is learned, shared, transgenerational, symbolic, patterned, and adaptive. Cultural models help managers form testable expectations about values and behavior, but country profiles are starting points, not substitutes for learning about individuals, subcultures, and changing practices.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three-layer model of culture**
  - When to use: Diagnose why a visible behavior or management practice makes sense locally.
  - How: Start with observable artifacts and products such as language, food, buildings, and art; examine formal and informal norms and values; then probe implicit basic assumptions that organize behavior and problem solving.
- **Hofstede's cultural dimensions**
  - When to use: Compare national tendencies that may affect hierarchy, risk, rewards, communication, and time horizons.
  - How: Assess **power distance**, **uncertainty avoidance**, **individualism versus collectivism**, **masculinity versus femininity**, **time orientation**, and **indulgence versus restraint**. Translate the profile into management choices: high power distance often calls for more centralized, tall structures; high uncertainty avoidance for more rules; collectivism for group-oriented rewards; long-term orientation for saving and investment; and restraint for stronger regulation of gratification.
- **Trompenaars's five relationship orientations**
  - When to use: Understand how partners interpret rules, status, emotion, boundaries, and group membership.
  - How: Compare **universalism versus particularism**, **individualism versus communitarianism**, **neutral versus emotional cultures**, **specific versus diffuse cultures**, and **achievement versus ascription cultures**. Also examine attitudes toward time and the environment.
- **GLOBE (Global Leadership and Organizational Behavior Effectiveness)**
  - When to use: Connect national culture to organizational practices and leadership expectations.
  - How: Compare nine dimensions: uncertainty avoidance, power distance, societal collectivism, in-group collectivism, gender egalitarianism, assertiveness, future orientation, performance orientation, and humane orientation. Distinguish **values** (how people think things should be) from **practices** (how things actually are).

## Key Concepts
- **Culture**: Acquired knowledge that forms values, creates attitudes, and influences behavior.
- **Values**: Convictions about what is right or wrong, good or bad, and important or unimportant.
- **Power distance**: The extent to which less powerful members accept unequal power distribution.
- **Uncertainty avoidance**: The extent to which people feel threatened by ambiguity and create beliefs or institutions to avoid it.
- **Individualism and collectivism**: A continuum from caring primarily for self and immediate family to belonging to groups in exchange for loyalty.
- **Masculinity and femininity**: A contrast between success, money, and things and caring for others and quality of life.
- **Country clusters**: Groups of countries with overlapping value patterns that provide a preliminary comparison.
- **Stereotype**: An exaggerated cultural extreme that can replace evidence about the actual person or situation.

## Mental Models
- Use cultural dimensions as **diagnostic questions**, not labels: ask how this partner handles authority, uncertainty, rewards, time, status, and disagreement.
- Compare dimensions in pairs or clusters; one score rarely predicts behavior by itself.
- Separate **values from practices**. A society may want less hierarchy while still practicing high power distance.
- Assume culture is patterned but adaptive. Technology, age, experience, migration, and economic change can shift values.
- Treat the individual as the unit of interaction and the national profile as a hypothesis to verify.

## Anti-patterns
- **Country score equals individual**: National averages hide subcultures, organizational variation, and personal experience.
- **One model as universal truth**: Hofstede, Trompenaars, and GLOBE use different samples, definitions, and methods; compare them carefully.
- **Stereotyping from a single dimension**: High power distance does not tell the whole story about risk, gender, time, or relationships.
- **Assuming values are static**: Japanese managers abroad, rising individualism in Japan, and renewed Confucian interest in China show value transition.

## Worked Example
The Takeda-Shire merger joins a Japanese pharmaceutical firm with an Irish company. Managers should not assume that the new organization will automatically behave as one unit. Use power distance, uncertainty avoidance, individualism-collectivism, time orientation, and Trompenaars's achievement-ascription and neutral-emotional distinctions to ask how employees report problems, challenge authority, and build trust. Use GLOBE to separate desired leadership practices from current ones. Then create a shared culture around quality, transparency, and patient safety while preserving local practices that support effective coordination. The aim is not to erase difference but to make difference discussable and useful.

## Key Takeaways
1. Culture is learned and shared, but it is also adaptive and layered beneath visible behavior.
2. Values influence decision authority, risk, rewards, procedures, loyalty, cooperation, time horizon, and innovation.
3. Hofstede gives six dimensions; Trompenaars gives five relationship orientations plus time and environment; GLOBE connects culture to leadership and practice.
4. GLOBE's values-versus-practices distinction prevents managers from confusing aspirations with current behavior.
5. Use research-based profiles to prepare, then validate assumptions through local observation and dialogue.
