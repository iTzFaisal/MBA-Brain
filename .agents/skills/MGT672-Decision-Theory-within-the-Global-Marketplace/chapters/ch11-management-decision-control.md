# Chapter 11: Management Decision and Control

## Core Idea

Decision making chooses among alternatives; controlling evaluates results against plans or objectives and determines corrective action. In international operations, the two functions are inseparable: decisions must fit local economic, political, and cultural conditions, while controls must detect when the strategy no longer fits. Centralize what requires scale, protection, or coordination; decentralize what requires local speed, knowledge, and adaptation.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Decision-Making Process (Figure 11-1):** Use the process to define a problem, consider alternatives, select and implement action, and evaluate results, while looping back when evidence changes. The locus depends on centralization versus decentralization. Centralize when the subsidiary is large, strategically important, capital-intensive, technologically sophisticated, interdependent, geographically close, or managing homogeneous products and valuable brands. Decentralize when it is small, locally differentiated, geographically distant, stable, less interdependent, and staffed by competent local managers.
- **Total Quality Management (TQM):** Use TQM when quality is a strategic source of competitiveness. Make quality everyone's job and use **concurrent engineering/interfunctional teams**, employee **empowerment**, rewards and recognition, ongoing training, statistical quality control, supplier involvement, and **kaizen** (continuous improvement). **ISO 9000** examines design, process control, purchasing, service, inspection/testing, and training.
- **Internal and External Control:** An internal control orientation starts from what the MNC does well and imposes or coordinates a strategy. An external control orientation starts from customer, partner, and market behavior and builds from what works locally. Trompenaars' four control statements range from discovering and combining successful customer-level strategies to designing a central strategy and cascading it; rank them to reveal the firm's orientation.
- **Direct Controls vs. Indirect Controls:** Use **direct controls** through face-to-face meetings, executive visits, staffing, and reporting relationships when personal understanding and intervention matter. Use **indirect controls** through operating reports, balance sheets, income statements, cash budgets, ratios, and written communication for frequent, lower-cost monitoring. Combining monthly indirect control with periodic direct control is usually practical.
- **Performance Evaluation:** Evaluate financial performance through profit and **return on investment (ROI = profit/assets)** or return on owners' investment; quality through **quality control circles (QCCs)** and process data; and personnel through appraisals, satisfaction, development, and **assessment centers**. Interpret ROI in light of currency changes, tax structures, transfer pricing, and other external conditions.

## Key Concepts (5-10)

- **Ringisei** is Japanese decision making by consensus; **tatemae** is doing what the norm requires, while **honne** is what one really wants to do.
- **Codetermination** requires workers and managers to discuss major decisions.
- Empowerment gives people resources, information, and authority to improve quality.
- Financial control is common across MNCs even when marketing, production, and R&D autonomy varies by country.
- U.S. MNCs tend to emphasize quantifiable output control; European MNCs often rely more on behavioral, qualitative, and expatriate-based control.
- Direct control is information-rich but expensive; indirect control is scalable but can miss context.
- QCCs make quality data visible to operating employees and support early correction.
- Personnel evaluations can contain nationality and cultural bias; cross-cultural comparability requires care.

## Mental Models

- **Decision-control loop:** Choose, act, measure, compare, correct. A control system that cannot trigger a decision is reporting, not management.
- **Control should match the problem:** Protect proprietary technology and coordinate interdependent scale centrally; delegate customer adaptation to capable local operators.
- **Numbers are signals, not verdicts:** Profit and ROI show outcomes, but exchange rates, tax, transfer prices, labor structures, and market stage may distort attribution.
- **Build quality in:** Empowerment, supplier learning, training, and QCCs prevent defects earlier than inspection alone.

## Anti-patterns

- Centralizing every decision because the firm is large, thereby sacrificing local response and market learning.
- Decentralizing without competent managers, clear boundaries, information, or accountability.
- Using ROI alone to judge a unit exposed to currency devaluation, transfer pricing, or politically imposed costs.
- Relying on monthly reports while never visiting or speaking with the operating unit.
- Transplanting individual rewards, participation, or control rituals without checking cultural meaning.
- Treating control as proof that an old strategy is still correct, as BlackBerry did while the smartphone market moved to touchscreens.

## Worked Example

Amazon and Alibaba show two control choices in online retail. Amazon acts as a merchant and facilitator: inventory, warehouses, fulfillment, and direct selling give it control over delivery and quality but require heavy fixed investment. Alibaba mainly facilitates third-party transactions: it gains asset-light flexibility and scale but cannot directly control merchants' shipping mistakes. A manager should choose metrics that match the model: Amazon needs inventory age, fulfillment speed, and warehouse productivity; Alibaba needs seller quality, dispute rates, platform reliability, and partner compliance. Both also need local market feedback because geographic growth potential differs sharply.

## Key Takeaways

1. Decision authority should reflect investment, interdependence, technology, distance, product diversity, and local capability.
2. TQM links decision rights to frontline knowledge and continuous improvement.
3. Direct and indirect controls are complementary, not competing substitutes.
4. Financial, quality, and personnel measures provide a balanced control portfolio.
5. International control works when it detects contextual change early enough to revise decisions.
