# Chapter 5: Managing Across Cultures

## Core Idea
Management practices that work at home cannot be assumed to work abroad. MNCs need a contingency approach that combines global integration with national or regional responsiveness, tests similarities as well as differences, gives capable subsidiaries autonomy, and replaces stereotypes with local evidence.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Four strategic predispositions**
  - When to use: Diagnose how an MNC currently makes cross-border decisions and choose a better fit.
  - How: An **ethnocentric predisposition** lets parent-company values and interests guide decisions. A **polycentric predisposition** tailors decisions to each host-country culture. A **regiocentric predisposition** blends parent and subsidiary interests by region. A **geocentric predisposition** integrates a global systems approach. Successful firms may mix them as conditions change.
- **Globalization imperative versus national responsiveness**
  - When to use: Decide what to standardize and what to adapt.
  - How: Test industry standards, local customer tastes, the value of being an insider or buying local, the coordination burden of global operations, and the distinctive capabilities of subsidiaries. Standardize where scale or a homogeneous niche matters; adapt products, messages, and authority where local meaning matters.
- **Parochialism and simplification diagnosis**
  - When to use: Detect why a home-country practice is producing poor results abroad.
  - How: Look for **parochialism**, viewing the world through one's own perspective, and **simplification**, using the same orientation with different cultural groups. Replace both with a contingency comparison of people, relationships, time, status, decision authority, and work values.
- **High-context versus low-context management**
  - When to use: Adapt communication, trust building, contracts, and feedback.
  - How: In high-context cultures, relationships are durable, messages implicit, authority personal, agreements often spoken, and insiders distinct from outsiders. In low-context cultures, relationships are more task-focused, messages explicit, authority diffuse, agreements written, and outsiders more readily included.
- **Contingency approach to international HRM**
  - When to use: Design recruitment, training, appraisal, compensation, and labor relations for a host country.
  - How: Keep a common purpose but adjust the practice. Group incentives may fit collectivist settings; individual incentives may fit individualistic ones. A hybrid often works: local practices for rank-and-file employees and parent-company practices for senior management.

## Key Concepts
- **Ethnocentric predisposition**: Parent-country values guide strategy.
- **Polycentric predisposition**: Each country's culture shapes strategy.
- **Regiocentric predisposition**: Parent and subsidiary interests are blended regionally.
- **Geocentric predisposition**: Decisions use an integrated global-systems view.
- **Globalization imperative**: The belief that one worldwide approach is the key to efficiency and effectiveness.
- **National responsiveness**: Adaptation to local customers, institutions, and cultural expectations.
- **Parochialism**: Viewing the world through one's own eyes and perspective.
- **Simplification**: Exhibiting the same orientation toward different cultural groups.
- **Guanxi**: Chinese "good connections" that can build trust and business access.

## Mental Models
- Use **global core, local execution**: centralize what creates scale; decentralize what requires local knowledge.
- Treat similarities as hypotheses to test, not excuses to copy. Organizational behavior modification and some commitment drivers can transfer, while HR practices often cannot.
- Give subsidiaries autonomy when they possess local capabilities and information, then connect them through shared learning systems.
- Build relationships before demanding speed in high-context environments; a contract may be the output of trust rather than its substitute.

## Anti-patterns
- **Copying headquarters abroad**: A successful domestic product, appraisal system, or incentive plan can carry the wrong assumptions overseas.
- **Simplifying all cultures into "foreign"**: British, Chinese, Russian, Indian, French, Brazilian, and Arab practices differ within and across clusters.
- **Treating relationship time as waste**: In China, Russia, Brazil, and Arab countries, patience and personal trust can be prerequisites for the transaction.
- **Using stereotypes as operating rules**: The chapter warns that values and HR behavior change over time and vary within a country.

## Worked Example
Renault moved beyond a narrow France-centered model by acquiring interests in Nissan, Samsung Motors, Dacia, and AvtoVAZ. It marketed the Logan under the brand most familiar to each market, integrated Renault and Nissan sales organizations in Europe, produced Nissan models in Brazil, and formed cross-cultural teams to share synergies and best practices. It also introduced the low-cost Kwid in India, then adapted it for Indonesia and Brazil. The strategy combined geocentric learning, regional integration, and local responsiveness instead of forcing one brand, product, or management method everywhere.

## Key Takeaways
1. Choose among ethnocentric, polycentric, regiocentric, and geocentric tendencies deliberately rather than by habit.
2. The globalization imperative must be balanced with local customer, institutional, and subsidiary needs.
3. Diagnose parochialism and simplification before blaming local employees or markets.
4. Modify HRM, compensation, communication, and relationship-building practices by culture.
5. Use local autonomy and cross-cultural teams to turn subsidiaries into sources of innovation, not just execution units.
