# Chapter 2: The Political, Legal, and Technological Environment

## Core Idea
International managers operate where political authority, legal rules, regulation, and technology change quickly and interact. Before entering or expanding in a country, assess the political system, legal foundations and jurisdiction, regulatory constraints, and technological capabilities rather than assuming that economic openness means institutional predictability.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Two dimensions of political systems**
  - When to use: Compare countries and anticipate how government authority will affect business.
  - How: Place the system on an ideological continuum from democratic to totalitarian and on an individualism-collectivism continuum. These are ideal types, not pure categories. Democracy gives citizens control directly or through elections; totalitarianism places political and human life under one representative party. Individualism prioritizes freedom to pursue economic and political ends; collectivism prioritizes society's goals over individual desires.
- **Four legal foundations**
  - When to use: Start legal due diligence in a new host country.
  - How: Identify the role of **Islamic law**, **socialist law**, **common law**, and **civil or code law**. Then examine the actual statutes, regulators, courts, and enforcement practices rather than relying on the label alone.
- **International jurisdiction principles**
  - When to use: Determine which country can regulate conduct or hear a dispute.
  - How: Apply the **nationality principle** to citizens wherever they act, the **territoriality principle** to conduct within a nation's territory, and the **protective principle** to conduct abroad that harms national security. Add the **principle of sovereignty**, **doctrine of comity**, and **act of state doctrine** when assessing enforcement and recognition of foreign governmental acts.
- **Technology leapfrogging and global shifts in production**
  - When to use: Assess how technology changes market access, operations, and employment in developing economies.
  - How: Examine connectivity, e-business, telecommunications, biotechnology, the Internet of Things, blockchain, artificial intelligence, machine learning, robotics, outsourcing, and offshoring. Look for both productivity gains and new fragilities.
- **National treatment and most-favored-nation treatment**
  - When to use: Evaluate trade fairness and the effect of tariffs, subsidies, local-content rules, or regional blocs.
  - How: Check whether foreign firms receive nondiscriminatory treatment, whether local partners or workers are mandated, and whether antidumping or countervailing duties are being used for legitimate correction or protectionism.

## Key Concepts
- **Individualism**: Political freedom to pursue economic and political activities with limited constraint.
- **Collectivism**: Political priority given to society's goals over individual desires.
- **Socialism**: Government ownership of institutions where profit is not the ultimate goal.
- **Privatization**: Transfer of state-owned activity or ownership to private investors, often creating MNC and joint-venture opportunities.
- **Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA)**: U.S. law prohibiting influence over foreign officials through personal payments or political contributions.
- **Sovereignty**: A government's right to rule itself.
- **E-business**: Internet-enabled commercial activity, including B2B, B2C, e-retailing, and digital financial services.
- **Technological leapfrogging**: Moving directly to newer systems, such as mobile telecommunications without first building extensive landlines.

## Mental Models
- Use the **political-system map** as a risk hypothesis, not a country stereotype; institutions can combine market reforms with political control.
- Apply **person, place, security** to jurisdiction: whose national law follows the actor, where did the conduct occur, and does it threaten national security?
- Treat regulation as part of the operating model. Legal differences can eliminate scale economies, slow time to market, and raise cost.
- Technology is a double-edged network: it integrates markets and can also magnify cyberattacks, infrastructure failures, surveillance, and job displacement.

## Anti-patterns
- **Equating privatization with deregulation**: The state may sell ownership while retaining heavy controls or strategic influence.
- **Assuming one home-country compliance program is enough**: MNCs face host-country rules plus laws that follow the firm or its managers abroad.
- **Treating bribery as a local custom**: The FCPA and comparable rules make intermediaries, disguised fees, and political payments serious legal risks.
- **Counting connections without testing resilience**: Digital dependence on a small number of cables, platforms, or suppliers creates systemic fragility.

## Worked Example
Social media during the Syrian conflict illustrates the political-legal-technology interaction. Protesters used Facebook and YouTube to organize demonstrations, share safety information, and make events visible internationally. Refugees used online groups to exchange route, weather, and supply information. The government responded with Internet shutdowns, censorship, malware, and attacks on accounts. For an MNC, the lesson is practical: political change can move through digital networks faster than formal institutions can respond. A country assessment should therefore include connectivity, censorship, cyber risk, emergency communications, employee safety, and the possibility that a service or platform becomes part of a political conflict.

## Key Takeaways
1. Political systems combine ideology with the degree of individualism or collectivism; no country fits a pure type.
2. Legal foundations are only a starting point; enforcement, bureaucracy, and jurisdiction determine practical risk.
3. Sovereignty, nationality, territoriality, protection, comity, and act of state rules can place one transaction under several legal perspectives.
4. FCPA compliance must extend through agents, consultants, suppliers, distributors, and joint-venture partners.
5. Mobile connectivity, e-business, AI, blockchain, and robotics create opportunities while reshaping work and increasing dependence.
