# Chapter 4: Behavioral Approach
## Core Idea
The behavioral approach studies what leaders do rather than who they are or what capabilities they possess. Research converged on two broad dimensions: task behaviors that structure work and facilitate goal accomplishment, and relationship behaviors that build comfort, trust, respect, and cooperation. Ohio State, Michigan, and Blake and Mouton's work made these dimensions observable and assessable. The approach is a practical framework or mirror for diagnosing and changing behavior, not a universal prescription: evidence has not established one best style or consistently connected behavior combinations to performance outcomes.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Ohio State studies and Leader Behavior Description Questionnaire (LBDQ; Hemphill & Coons, 1957; LBDQ-XII, Stogdill, 1963):** Use follower ratings to assess initiating structure (organizing work, roles, schedules, and expectations) and consideration (camaraderie, respect, trust, and liking). The two dimensions are independent, so a leader can be high or low on both.
- **University of Michigan studies:** Use employee orientation to assess attention to workers' individuality and needs, and production orientation to assess technical and production demands. The initial model treated them as opposite ends of one continuum; later work treated them as independent, like Ohio State's dimensions.
- **Blake and Mouton's Leadership Grid (formerly Managerial Grid; 1964, 1978, 1985; Blake & McCanse, 1991):** Plot concern for results/production horizontally and concern for people vertically, each from 1 to 9. The five styles are authority-compliance (9,1), country-club management (1,9), impoverished management (1,1), middle-of-the-road management (5,5), and team management (9,9). Also diagnose paternalism/maternalism (unintegrated 1,9 and 9,1) and opportunism (shifting styles for personal advancement). Leaders may have a dominant style and a pressure-time backup style.
- **Leadership Behavior Questionnaire:** Use its 20 items for a general profile. Sum odd items for task behavior and even items for relationship behavior; each score ranges from 10-50, interpreted from very low to very high. Compare self-ratings with coworker ratings.

## Mental Models
- **Use** two independent questions: What structure does this group need, and what relationship climate does it need? Do not assume more of one requires less of the other.
- **Think** diagnose before prescribing: use follower feedback and outcomes to decide whether to add direction, support, participation, or both.
- **Use** the Grid as a development map, not a moral label; identify a dominant style and the backup behavior triggered by pressure.
- **Think** high task plus high relationship is a strong hypothesis, not a universal law; complexity, follower capability, and task demands still matter.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating task and relationship behavior as one tradeoff prevents leaders from intentionally increasing both.
- Assuming the 9,9 style always wins ignores contradictory and inconclusive findings and situational demands.
- Measuring behavior without checking morale, satisfaction, productivity, or follower perceptions mistakes activity for effectiveness.
- Using paternalistic "family" behavior to reward loyalty and punish dissent, or opportunistically changing style for personal gain, undermines trust.
- Relying only on self-report can hide the gap between intended and experienced leadership.

## Worked Example
**Mark Young, hospital painting department:** Mark inherited 20 union employees and spent four months analyzing direct and indirect costs. He began as "all task": he reorganized the department, created a scheduling procedure, set performance standards, and sought little input because hospital errors were costly. Over time he delegated to two crew leaders, stayed in contact with employees, and built relationships through informal weekly meals and humor. He described himself as a coach who wanted workers to experience doing good work independently. The department became the hospital's most productive maintenance unit and earned a 92% customer rating. His trajectory illustrates movement from high initiating structure toward a high-task/high-relationship pattern, plausibly near team management (9,9), while showing that behavior can change as the situation stabilizes.

## Key Takeaways
1. Leadership behavior has task and relationship dimensions.
2. Ohio State names the dimensions initiating structure and consideration; Michigan names parallel orientations employee and production orientation.
3. The Leadership Grid makes concern for results and people visible through five numbered styles.
4. The approach broadened research, has substantial evidence, and offers a useful heuristic for development.
5. It has not identified a universal effective style or a reliable behavior-to-outcome formula.
6. Effective application requires behavioral assessment, feedback, adaptation, and attention to context.
