# Chapter 16: Culture and Leadership
## Core Idea
Culture is the learned, shared beliefs, values, rules, norms, symbols, and traditions that give a group its way of life. Globalization makes cross-cultural competence essential, but leaders must avoid both cultural superiority and simplistic national stereotypes. The GLOBE research program shows that cultures differ in what leadership behaviors they endorse, while some attributes are broadly desirable or undesirable everywhere. Its strength is unusually broad, quantitative, standardized cross-cultural evidence; its weaknesses are the absence of one unified theory, vague dimensions, broad measures, a perception-centered leadership concept, and universal attributes that can ignore situation.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **GLOBE cultural dimensions (House, Hanges, Javidan, Dorfman, & Gupta, 2004):** Use the nine dimensions to diagnose expectations before leading across cultures: uncertainty avoidance (rules and predictability), power distance (accepted inequality of power), institutional collectivism (societal collective action), in-group collectivism (family/organizational loyalty), gender egalitarianism, assertiveness, future orientation, performance orientation, and humane orientation. GLOBE studied about 17,000 managers in 950 organizations across 62 societies using questionnaires, interviews, focus groups, and media analysis.
- **GLOBE clusters and leadership profiles:** Use clusters as hypotheses to test, not labels for individuals. The 10 clusters are Anglo, Germanic Europe, Latin Europe, Sub-Saharan Africa, Eastern Europe, Middle East, Confucian Asia, Southern Asia, Latin America, and Nordic Europe. Desired profiles differ: Eastern Europe favors autonomous and self-protective leadership; Latin America charismatic/value-based, team-oriented, and self-protective; Latin Europe charismatic, team-oriented, participative, and self-protective; Confucian Asia self-protective, team-oriented, and humane with low participation; Nordic Europe charismatic, participative, and team-oriented with low self-protection; Anglo charismatic, participative, humane, and team-oriented with low self-protection; Sub-Saharan Africa humane, caring, collaborative, and non-autonomous; Southern Asia self-protective, charismatic, humane, and team-oriented with low participation; Germanic Europe autonomous, charismatic, participative, and low self-protective; Middle East self-protective, status/face-saving, and autonomous, with less emphasis on charismatic, team, and participative behavior.
- **Six global leadership behaviors:** Charismatic/value-based leadership inspires and expects performance through values; team-oriented leadership builds a collaborative common purpose; participative leadership involves others and is nonautocratic; humane-oriented leadership is supportive, considerate, compassionate, generous, modest, and sensitive; autonomous leadership is independent and individualistic; self-protective leadership preserves safety, status, face, and procedure but can be self-centered or conflict inducing. Use the profile match to adapt style while retaining integrity.
- **Universal attributes:** GLOBE respondents broadly endorsed trustworthy, just, honest, dependable, foresighted, encouraging, motivational, confidence-building, informed, intelligent, decisive, communicative, team-building, administratively skilled, coordinating, excellence-oriented, effective bargaining, and win-win problem solving. They rejected loner/asocial, noncooperative, egocentric, irritable, nonexplicit, ruthless, and dictatorial attributes. The list is a useful baseline, not a context-free trait theory.
- **Cross-cultural competence and instruments:** Adler and Bartholomew emphasize understanding global business/political/cultural environments, multiple perspectives, simultaneous work across cultures, adaptation, and equality rather than superiority; Ting-Toomey adds transcultural visions. Hofstede's *Culture in the Workplace* questionnaire measures individualism, power distance, certainty, and achievement. The abbreviated *Dimensions of Culture Questionnaire* adapts GLOBE items across nine dimensions; it is for reflection, not research, and yields individual perceptions rather than societal scores.

## Key Concepts
- **Ethnocentrism:** Treating one's own culture as more natural or superior; it narrows understanding.
- **Prejudice:** Fixed, faulty judgments that filter out evidence and other perspectives.
- **Multiculturalism:** Taking multiple cultures or subcultures into account.
- **Implicit leadership theory:** Leadership as the attributes observers expect and recognize in leaders (Lord & Maher).
- **Cultural dimension:** A patterned axis on which groups differ in values, norms, or practices.
- **Cultural cluster:** A statistically supported regional grouping used for cautious comparison.
- **Culturally endorsed leadership profile:** The relative desirability of the six global behaviors within a cluster.
- **Transcultural vision:** Communication and purpose that can operate across cultural boundaries.
- **Power distance/collectivism:** Expectations about unequal authority and individual versus group priority.

## Mental Models
- **Use** GLOBE dimensions and cluster profiles as questions: ask what local followers expect, then verify with behavior rather than stereotyping a country.
- **Think** adapt expression, not ethical foundations; cultural humility means relating as an equal and preserving trust, dignity, and fairness.
- **Use** the universal desirable/undesirable list as a floor, then calibrate participation, autonomy, status, directness, and humane behavior to the setting.
- **Think** perceived leadership and performed leadership are different evidence; measure outcomes and relationships as well as endorsement.

## Anti-patterns
- Ethnocentrism: imposing one culture's rules as universally correct and treating difference as incompetence.
- Prejudice and fixed national stereotypes that ignore within-cluster, organizational, individual, and intersectional variation.
- Assuming one global leadership style, especially universal participation or autonomy, will work in every cluster.
- Confusing GLOBE's implicit, perception-based model with a complete theory of what leaders actually do.
- Using the abbreviated Dimensions of Culture Questionnaire for societal or organizational research, or treating its scores as personality diagnoses.

## Worked Example
Samira Tanaka, a proactive campus leader, interns at a Japanese automotive company with a hybrid Japanese and North American culture. Japanese advisers emphasize consensus and careful planning; North American workers are seen as moving quickly, and feedback from both sides is indirect. Samira invites participation, offers process improvements, follows up on late work, and initiates tasks, but her performance review calls her pushy, not a team player, and prone to speaking out of turn. She had learned to downplay ambition because assertive female leaders were stereotyped in school. The case shows a mismatch between her campus leadership script and a Confucian Asia-influenced workplace that values group harmony, status-sensitive coordination, and consensus. Her next step is not to abandon initiative: she should clarify decision protocols, observe when and how dissent is voiced, seek culturally informed feedback, and adapt timing and directness while retaining dependable planning, competence, and participation as strengths.

## Key Takeaways
1. Culture shapes leader expectations and communication, but dimensions are diagnostic tendencies rather than prescriptions.
2. GLOBE expands Hofstede with nine dimensions, 10 clusters, six behavior profiles, and universal positive and negative attributes.
3. Cross-cultural leadership requires humility, adaptation, equality, empathy, and a transcultural vision.
4. Integrity, trust, competence, and interpersonal skill are broadly valued; autonomy, participation, status, and humane expression vary.
5. GLOBE's scope is impressive, but its perception-centered measures and context limits require cautious application.
