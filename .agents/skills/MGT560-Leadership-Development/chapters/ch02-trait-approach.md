# Chapter 2: Trait Approach
## Core Idea
The trait approach explains leadership by examining who leaders are and which personal characteristics distinguish them from nonleaders or shape perceptions of leadership. Early "great man" theories treated traits as innate, but Stogdill's reviews showed that traits alone do not make someone a leader: their relevance depends on the situation and relationship. Later work restored a qualified role for traits. Intelligence, self-confidence, determination, integrity, and sociability are recurring major traits, but no universal checklist predicts effective leadership in every context. The approach is most useful for selection, self-awareness, and development planning when treated as evidence about tendencies rather than destiny.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Great Man theory:** Use as the historical starting point for the trait approach. It searches for innate qualities in exceptional social, political, and military leaders; its limitation is the assumption that leadership belongs only to the gifted few.
- **Stogdill's trait-situation findings (1948, 1974):** Use traits together with situational requirements. The 1948 review emphasized that no consistent universal set separates leaders from nonleaders; the 1974 review concluded that both traits and situations determine leadership.
- **Major Leadership Traits (Kirkpatrick & Locke, 1991; text synthesis):** Assess intelligence, self-confidence, determination, integrity, and sociability as useful benchmarks. They may be born, learned, or both, but they do not by themselves specify what a leader should do.
- **Big Five personality model (Goldberg, 1990; Judge et al., 2002):** Use as an organizing framework for personality assessment. The factors are neuroticism, extraversion, openness, agreeableness, and conscientiousness. In the meta-analysis, extraversion had the strongest leadership association, followed by conscientiousness, openness, and low neuroticism; agreeableness was weakly associated.
- **Emotional intelligence:** Mayer, Salovey, and Caruso define it as perceiving, using, understanding, and managing emotions in oneself and relationships; the MSCEIT measures these abilities. Goleman's broader model includes self-awareness, self-regulation, conscientiousness, motivation, empathy, communication, and conflict management. Shankman and Allen organize emotionally intelligent leadership around context, self, and others.
- **Leadership Trait Questionnaire (LTQ):** Use for self-awareness and observer comparison. The leader and five familiar raters score 14 characteristics; average observer ratings are compared with the self-rating to reveal strengths, weaknesses, and perception gaps. Other named instruments include the MMPI and Myers-Briggs Type Indicator.

## Mental Models
- **Use** traits as a role-fit hypothesis: define what the position and situation demand, then compare evidence rather than selecting the person with the longest trait list.
- **Think** emergence is not the same as effectiveness: a trait may make someone appear influential without improving team productivity or satisfaction.
- **Use** self and observer ratings to locate blind spots, then pair each gap with a behavioral or skills-development plan.
- **Think** emotional intelligence is promising but contested; make modest claims and distinguish ability measures such as MSCEIT from broad competency claims.

## Anti-patterns
- Searching for one definitive list of leadership traits ignores contradictory findings and situational effects.
- Treating extraversion, intelligence, or confidence as sufficient can reward visibility while missing integrity, fit, or follower outcomes.
- Using subjective self-help trait lists as if they were validated research produces arbitrary selection decisions.
- Assuming traits are fixed makes development seem impossible; assuming they are infinitely malleable overstates what assessment can change.

## Worked Example
**Choosing a new director at Great Lakes Foods:** Sandra Coke must select among three equally credentialed managers. Alexa Smith has broad company experience, creativity, insight, persistence, and four successful product launches. Kelsey Metts is exceptionally bright, highly sociable, and has helped launch two products. Thomas Santiago is trusted for strategic vision, commitment to company values, honesty, and integrity, with three product launches. A trait-based comparison makes the competing strengths visible, but it cannot produce a context-free answer. Sandra should define the director role's demands, gather multiple perspectives, and test how each candidate's traits fit the research division. The case also exposes the approach's weakness: a credible trait profile does not establish future performance or account for situational and behavioral requirements.

## Key Takeaways
1. Traits contribute to leadership, but traits alone do not create leadership across situations.
2. The recurring major traits are intelligence, self-confidence, determination, integrity, and sociability.
3. Big Five research links leadership most strongly with extraversion and then conscientiousness, openness, and low neuroticism.
4. Emotional intelligence connects emotional awareness and regulation to leadership, but its scope and impact remain debated.
5. Trait assessments help with selection and self-awareness, not deterministic labeling.
6. The approach is strong in research breadth and intuitive benchmarks but weak in universal prediction, outcome linkage, context, and trainability.
