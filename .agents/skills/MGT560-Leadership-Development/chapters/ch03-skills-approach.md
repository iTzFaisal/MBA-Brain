# Chapter 3: Skills Approach
## Core Idea
The skills approach shifts attention from largely fixed personality characteristics to knowledge and abilities that can be learned and developed. Katz (1955) presents a practical three-skill approach: technical, human, and conceptual skills. Mumford and colleagues later developed a capability model in which competencies explain leadership outcomes, while individual attributes, career experiences, and environmental influences shape those competencies. The approach makes leadership accessible and useful for development, but its broad model is more descriptive than predictive and includes several trait-like variables.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Katz's Three-Skill Approach (1955):** Use technical skill for specialized work and tools, human skill for working cooperatively with people, and conceptual skill for ideas, organizational meaning, vision, and strategy. Technical skill matters most at lower levels; all three matter in middle management; conceptual and human skills dominate at the top.
- **Mumford et al. Skills Model (Mumford, Zaccaro, Harding, Jacobs, & Fleishman, 2000):** Use it to diagnose capability for complex organizational problems. Core competencies are problem-solving skills, social judgment skills, and knowledge; these produce effective problem solving and performance. General cognitive ability, crystallized cognitive ability, motivation, and personality influence competencies, while career experiences and environmental influences also affect performance.
- **Social judgment skills:** Assess perspective taking, social perceptiveness, behavioral flexibility, and social performance. These capabilities help a leader understand constituencies, adapt behavior, communicate and persuade, mediate conflict, and coach implementation.
- **Skills Inventory:** Use as a pragmatic self-help instrument. Eighteen items produce technical, human, and conceptual scores by summing six specified items in each category; scores of 23-30 are high, 14-22 moderate, and 6-13 low.

## Mental Models
- **Use** role-level matching: diagnose whether the current challenge requires hands-on expertise, coordination, or system-level meaning, then develop the corresponding skill mix.
- **Think** through an ill-defined problem in sequence: define it, gather information, build new understanding, generate prototypes, and test time, stakeholder, career, and external constraints.
- **Use** social judgment before imposing a solution: take perspectives, read constituencies, adapt behavior, and communicate or mediate implementation.
- **Think** performance is capability in context: career assignments can build competence, while technology, follower expertise, communication, economics, politics, or disasters can enable or constrain results.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating skills as innate traits defeats the approach's central promise that capabilities can be learned through education and experience.
- Solving only the technical problem ignores social judgment, implementation support, and the perspectives of affected groups.
- Treating the Mumford model as a precise prediction equation overclaims a framework that is primarily descriptive and weak on causal specification.
- Transferring findings from more than 1,800 Army officers to every organization without testing context ignores the model's generalizability criticism.
- Blaming a leader for outcomes without examining environmental influences confuses capability with conditions.

## Worked Example
**Lieutenant Colonel Adams and Base X conversion:** Adams had strong engineering knowledge and followed the closure orders, early-retirement rules, deadlines, and required staffing targets. He independently imposed additional cuts in housing, family services, recreation, and military positions. The government then accepted every early-retirement applicant, producing shortages in critical areas; family support collapsed, overtime and stress rose, and the base struggled to meet its operational mission. Adams could execute a technically compliant plan, but he did not sufficiently model interdependencies, stakeholder perspectives, or the larger system. Coaching should strengthen problem definition and scenario planning, social perceptiveness toward families and branch chiefs, behavioral flexibility after new information, and communication about tradeoffs. The base commander's diagnosis that Adams forgot the bigger picture captures a skills-model failure, not simply a lack of effort.

## Key Takeaways
1. Skills describe what leaders can accomplish; traits describe who they are.
2. Katz's technical, human, and conceptual skills should be balanced according to organizational level.
3. Mumford's core competencies are problem-solving skills, social judgment skills, and knowledge.
4. Individual attributes, career experiences, and environmental influences shape how competencies become outcomes.
5. Challenging assignments, mentoring, training, and hands-on experience can develop leadership capability.
6. The approach is learnable, expansive, and education-friendly, but broad, weakly predictive, partly trait-driven, and context-limited.
