# Chapter 12: Psychodynamic Approach
## Core Idea
The psychodynamic approach adds a clinical, emotional, and irrational lens to leadership. Observable decisions are often shaped by unconscious motives, early relationships, anxiety, fantasies, defenses, and group dynamics. Effective leaders therefore need to understand their own inner drivers, read the emotional pulse of individuals and groups, and make hidden patterns discussable without reducing organizational problems to personality alone. The approach is strong for deep, systemic insight through case work, coaching, and 360-degree feedback. Its limits include subjective and difficult-to-test concepts, origins in atypical clinical cases, lack of standardized training, and a tendency to underweight structure; the best application integrates psychodynamic and organizational analysis.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Clinical Paradigm:** Use as organizational detective work. Assume every act has a rationale even when irrational; much mental life is unconscious and includes blind spots and a shadow side; emotions organize identity and relationships; and development is inter- and intrapersonal, with early caregivers continuing to shape adult patterns. Form hypotheses from repeated behavior and relational evidence rather than rushing to labels.
- **Inner theatre and Core Conflictual Relationship Themes (CCRTs; McDougall, 1985; Luborsky & Crits-Christoph, 1998):** Treat early influential relationships as an internal cast. Repeated wishes, needs, and fears become scripts that people project onto workplace superiors, peers, and followers; they react to anticipated reactions, not necessarily actual ones. Use CCRT exploration to identify psychic prisons and develop more productive relationship patterns.
- **Bion's group assumptions and social defenses:** Scan groups for unconscious dependency, fight-flight, or pairing assumptions. Anxiety may be managed through splitting, projection, displacement, or denial; bureaucratic routines can become social defenses that create safety while obscuring reality. Also watch mirroring/idealizing, identification with the aggressor, scapegoating, and folie a deux (shared delusion).
- **Narcissism and the leadership shadow:** Constructive narcissism can supply conviction, vitality, empathy, introspection, and inspiration. Reactive narcissism centers on power, status, superiority, and revenge for perceived slights; it resists criticism and loses reality testing. Use the distinction to preserve ambition while installing feedback and controls.
- **Group coaching and leadership archetypes:** A facilitator combines interviews, 360-degree feedback, self-portraits, narrative, a "hot seat," peer challenge, public commitments, and action plans to expose blind spots and support behavior change. Kets de Vries's validated Leadership Archetype Questionnaire (LAQ), including strategist, change catalyst, transactor, builder, innovator, processor, coach, and communicator, maps complementary role constellations; results are prompts for reflection, not fixed diagnoses.

## Key Concepts
- Clinical Paradigm: framework for interpreting conscious and unconscious organizational behavior.
- Inner theatre: internal cast of influential relationship figures and scripts.
- CCRT: recurring wish, expected response, and response pattern in relationships.
- Group assumptions: dependency, fight-flight, and pairing processes described by Bion.
- Social defenses: splitting, projection, displacement, denial, and bureaucratic containment of anxiety.
- Mirroring and idealizing: collusive affirmation in which leaders and followers sustain an image over reality.
- Identification with the aggressor: threatened followers adopt a powerful leader's threatening behavior.
- Folie a deux: leader-follower shared delusion that sacrifices criticism and reality testing.
- Narcissism: continuum from healthy self-interest and conviction to destructive self-absorption.
- Leadership archetype: personality-rooted style that is most effective when complemented by other styles.

## Mental Models
- Use the detective question, "What anxiety, wish, or relational history could make this repeated behavior rational?" Test the hypothesis against present evidence.
- Think present relationships may be colored by inner-theatre scripts; separate what a leader expects others to do from what they actually do.
- Use a group-level scan for dependency, fight-flight, pairing, and defenses before assigning blame to one individual.
- Think in complementary constellations: use 360 feedback and the LAQ to pair strengths and constraints, not to freeze people into one archetype.

## Anti-patterns
- Casual diagnosis: labeling a colleague narcissistic, bipolar, or traumatized from limited observation is unsafe and exceeds the framework's evidence.
- Psychodynamic tunnel vision: explaining everything through unconscious motives ignores roles, incentives, technology, power, and structure.
- Collusive mirroring: leaders accepting constant admiration and followers seeking reflected status can replace organizational reality with mutual fantasy.
- Reactive narcissism without reality testing: suppressing dissent, scapegoating critics, and making grandiose decisions endangers the system.
- Archetype determinism: treating LAQ results as permanent identity or assuming one leader can perform every charismatic and architectural role blocks team design.

## Worked Example
- **Tim's fear of success:** Tim excelled at an elite consulting firm, earned a top MBA, joined a pharmaceutical company, and became CEO after only eight years. Once he occupied the number-one position, he delayed major decisions, focused on minor problems, missed opportunities, and sometimes arrived drunk at important meetings. The board dismissed him within two years. A psychodynamic reading links the collapse to an internalized relationship with a father who repeatedly failed in business and told Tim he lacked the ability to succeed. Tim's achievement guilt and impostor fears remained dormant while he could stand behind others, then surfaced under CEO visibility. Coaching would explore that internal narrative, compare self-perception with 360 feedback, build reality-based alternatives to "success harms my father and exposes me," and pair accountability with support rather than simply moralizing the symptoms.

## Key Takeaways
1. Leadership behavior has both rational and hidden emotional causes that should be examined together.
2. Early relationship patterns can repeat at work through CCRTs, transference, and group assumptions.
3. Anxiety creates defenses and collusions that may temporarily stabilize a group while undermining reality testing.
4. Constructive narcissism can energize leadership; reactive narcissism requires feedback, boundaries, and controls.
5. Use coaching, 360 feedback, and archetype mapping as reflective tools, while integrating structural and systemic analysis.
