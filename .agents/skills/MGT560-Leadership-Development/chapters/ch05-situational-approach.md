# Chapter 5: Situational Approach

## Core Idea
Situational Leadership treats effectiveness as a fit between the leader's behavior and a follower's development on a specific goal. Leaders diagnose competence and commitment, then vary directive and supportive behavior as the follower and task change.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Situational Leadership II (SLII)** (Hersey and Blanchard; refined by Blanchard): Use when a follower's skill or motivation differs by goal or changes during a project. Diagnose the goal-specific development level, then match style: D1 -> S1, D2 -> S2, D3 -> S3, D4 -> S4.
- **Four leadership styles**: S1 directing is high directive/low supportive and specifies what, how, who, and when; S2 coaching is high directive/high supportive and explains, encourages, solicits input, while retaining the final decision; S3 supporting is low directive/high supportive and listens, praises, facilitates problem solving, and leaves day-to-day control with the follower; S4 delegating is low directive/low supportive and transfers responsibility after goals and roles are agreed.
- **Four development levels**: D1 means low competence/high commitment (an eager novice); D2 means some competence/low commitment (learning but discouraged); D3 means moderate-to-high competence/variable commitment (capable but uncertain); D4 means high competence/high commitment (skilled and motivated). These are temporary, goal-specific diagnoses, not labels.
- **Situational Leadership Questionnaire**: Use 12-20 work situations to identify a preferred style, primary and secondary styles, flexibility, diagnostic ability, and effectiveness. Expanded and multisource forms compare self, boss, associate, and follower perceptions.

## Key Concepts
- **Directive behavior**: One-way clarification of goals, roles, methods, standards, timelines, and evaluation.
- **Supportive behavior**: Two-way social and emotional support through listening, praise, input, information, and problem solving.
- **Development level**: The competence and commitment available for one goal or activity.
- **Competence**: Task knowledge and skill needed to accomplish the goal.
- **Commitment**: Motivation and confidence associated with pursuing the goal.
- **Developmental continuum**: Followers can move forward or regress across D1-D4, even within one project.
- **Diagnostic ability**: Accurately identifying what the follower needs before choosing a style.
- **Flexibility**: Moving among S1-S4 rather than leading with a fixed style.

## Mental Models
- Use the follower's competence and commitment for this goal, not tenure or a personality stereotype, as the diagnostic input.
- Think of directive and supportive behavior as independent dials: raise direction for missing skill and raise support for missing confidence or motivation.
- Use S1 for an enthusiastic novice, S2 when initial enthusiasm drops during learning, S3 for a capable but hesitant follower, and S4 when capability and commitment are both high.
- Think of regression as normal; reassess after setbacks, reassignment, or a new task rather than assuming yesterday's match still works.

## Anti-patterns
- **One style for everyone**: A fixed approach ignores the model's central claim that needs vary by follower, goal, and phase.
- **Delegating to an eager novice**: High commitment does not compensate for low competence; insufficient direction can produce avoidable errors.
- **Treating D-levels as labels**: A D4 painter may be D1 in carpentry; broad labels obscure the task-specific diagnosis.
- **Overclaiming the prescription**: The approach is practical, prescriptive, and widely used in training, but published research provides limited and inconsistent support. The weighting of competence and commitment, the logic of commitment changes, demographic effects, and group-level application remain unclear.
- **Using a biased questionnaire as proof**: Situational instruments force answers into the four prescribed styles and can favor the model by construction.

## Worked Example
- **Rene's restoration crew**: Ashley, trained for seven years, is highly competent but still seeks help with carpentry, so Rene shifts between S3 supporting and S4 delegating depending on the assignment. Levi is developed in painting but D1-D2 in other restoration skills; Rene normally coaches with S2 and returns to S1 directing when the work is complex and requires detailed training. Anton is a willing but inexperienced D1, so Rene and Ashley use S1 to specify preparation, cleanup, and procedures. Levi dislikes being directive and sometimes gives Anton S3 support instead, illustrating a style mismatch. The same employee can require different styles across tasks, and development can change quickly.

## Key Takeaways
1. Diagnose the follower's development for the particular goal before acting.
2. Match S1-S4 to D1-D4, but keep reassessing as competence and commitment move.
3. Use flexibility to help followers learn skills and confidence, not merely to control performance.
4. The model is easy to teach and apply across work, school, and family settings, but its prescriptions should be treated as useful heuristics rather than settled empirical law.
5. Individual matching is clearer than group matching; a group average can underserve members whose development differs sharply from the mean.
