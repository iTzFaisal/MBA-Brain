# Chapter 4: Defining Performance and Choosing a Measurement Approach

## Core Idea
Performance is evaluative, multidimensional behavior, not simply the results produced. Effective PM diagnoses why performance is weak, includes task and contextual dimensions, accounts for the work situation, and selects behavior, results, or trait measures according to the causal and strategic context.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Performance determinants model**: `Performance = Declarative Knowledge x Procedural Knowledge x Motivation`.
  - When to use: Diagnose a performance problem before prescribing training, coaching, incentives, or discipline.
  - How: Inspect facts and requirements, capability to execute, and choice/level/persistence of effort. Because the relationship is multiplicative, a near-zero determinant can dominate the outcome.
- **Task and contextual performance dimensions**: Task performance directly produces goods/services or enables the transformation process; contextual performance sustains the social and psychological environment.
  - When to use: Design complete criteria and prevent citizenship behavior from influencing ratings informally.
- **Job performance in context model**: Performer traits + work situation -> behaviors -> results.
  - When to use: Interpret outcomes fairly by checking tools, supervision, coworkers, equipment, and other constraints.
- **Three measurement approaches**: Behavior measures how work is done; results measure outputs; trait measures relatively stable attributes.
  - When to use: Choose based on skill level, behavior-result link, timing, controllability, and the decision purpose. Behavior and results can be combined.
- **Deliberate practice**: Aim to improve, focus on what is happening and why, seek expert feedback, build mental models, and repeat.
  - When to use: Develop expertise where practice and feedback are available.

## Key Concepts
- **Declarative knowledge**: Facts, principles, goals, and task requirements.
- **Procedural knowledge**: Cognitive, physical, perceptual, motor, and interpersonal ability to perform.
- **Motivation**: Choice, effort level, and persistence.
- **Task performance**: Role-prescribed activities that produce or enable work.
- **Contextual performance**: Extra-role behavior supporting the work environment.
- **Voice behavior**: Constructive challenge intended to improve the organization.
- **Results as proxy**: Outcome used as indirect evidence of behavior when the causal link is defensible.
- **Situational attribution**: Interpreting performance in relation to the actual work context.

## Mental Models
- Use the **multiplicative diagnosis** before assigning blame: knowledge, capability, and motivation are distinct interventions.
- Treat results as **evidence, not proof**. High results can reflect favorable conditions; low results can reflect constraints.
- Use behavior measures when causality is ambiguous or outcomes are delayed; use results when skilled employees have a clear behavior-result link and control the outcome.

## Anti-patterns
- **Results-only evaluation**: Attributes uncontrollable conditions to the employee.
- **Training by default**: Sends an employee to training when motivation, practice, supervision, or work conditions are the real issue.
- **Task-only measurement**: Omits cooperation, helping, rule following, and constructive challenge.
- **Implicit contextual criteria**: Lets supervisors reward or punish citizenship behavior without defined standards.
- **Trait attribution**: Treats personality or ability as a substitute for observable performance.

## Worked Example
**Jane's performance diagnosis:** Jane knows the merchandise and can perform the physical and cognitive work, so declarative and procedural knowledge are high. She avoids approaching customers and exerts effort mainly when the manager is present. The multiplicative model points to motivation, not product training. The manager should investigate the motivational or environmental cause and select an intervention accordingly.

## Key Takeaways
1. Define performance as evaluated behavior, then use results when they are valid evidence.
2. Diagnose knowledge, capability, motivation, HR practices, and work conditions separately.
3. Measure task and contextual performance explicitly.
4. Adjust behavior/result emphasis to strategy, timing, control, and causal clarity.
5. Define contextual and voice behaviors with observable examples.
