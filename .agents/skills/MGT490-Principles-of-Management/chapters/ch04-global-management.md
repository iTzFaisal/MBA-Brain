# Chapter 4: Global Management: Managing across Borders

## Core Idea
Globalization makes national economies and organizations more interdependent, but cross-border management is not simply domestic management at greater scale. International managers must choose expansion modes, manage trade and currency constraints, and adapt to cultural context without losing strategic coherence.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **International manager orientations**: Ethnocentric managers impose the home-country approach; polycentric managers defer to each host country; geocentric managers seek the best global approach while adapting to local conditions.
  - When to use: Use the geocentric orientation when global integration and local responsiveness both matter.
- **International expansion modes**: Global outsourcing; importing/exporting/countertrading; licensing and franchising; joint ventures; and wholly owned subsidiaries.
  - When to use: Select by balancing control, capital, speed, local knowledge, intellectual-property risk, and political exposure.
- **Expansion motives**: Companies seek cheaper or more plentiful supplies, new markets, lower labor costs, access to finance capital, and avoidance of tariffs or import quotas.
- **Hofstede model**: Compare national cultures through individualism/collectivism, power distance, uncertainty avoidance, and masculinity/femininity.
- **GLOBE Project**: Adds power distance, uncertainty avoidance, institutional and in-group collectivism, gender egalitarianism, assertiveness, future orientation, performance orientation, and humane orientation.
- **Cross-cultural practice**: Listen and observe, become aware of context, and choose something basic before assuming shared meaning.

## Key Concepts
- **Globalization**: The trend toward a more interdependent world economy.
- **Global village**: The shrinking of time and distance through travel and electronic communication.
- **Free trade**: Movement of goods and services without political or economic obstructions.
- **Trade barriers**: Tariffs, import quotas, and sanctions/embargoes.
- **Trading bloc**: Countries that agree to remove trade barriers.
- **Exchange rate**: The rate at which one currency can be exchanged for another.
- **BRICS**: Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa, five important emerging economies discussed as global competitors.
- **Culture shock**: Discomfort and disorientation when entering another culture.
- **Low-context/high-context culture**: Meaning carried mainly by explicit words versus situational cues and shared context.

## Mental Models
- Go global in ambition but local in interpretation; the same message, incentive, or process may carry different meanings.
- Before choosing an entry mode, ask what must be controlled and what must be learned locally.
- Treat language, space, time, religion, law, and political stability as operating variables, not etiquette details.

## Anti-patterns
- **Ethnocentric export**: Assuming a successful home-country practice will transfer unchanged.
- **Cultural stereotyping**: Treating a country model as a prediction about every individual.
- **Ignoring institutional risk**: Evaluating market size while overlooking sanctions, regulation, political stability, or currency exposure.
- **Cultural reductionism**: Treating a Hofstede or GLOBE score as a complete description of an individual or country.

## Worked Example
Huawei's global expansion illustrates the interaction of technology, politics, trade, and culture. The company had strong Chinese growth and a need for new markets, but allegations about government ties and security risks led several countries to restrict its 5G equipment. A geocentric manager would separate product capability from institutional trust, adapt communication and governance to each market, and compare the cost of local partnerships or alternative suppliers before expanding. The manager would also assess exchange rates, trade barriers, local laws, and political stability rather than treating BRICS membership as a guarantee of market access.

## Key Takeaways
1. Choose a geocentric mindset: integrate global learning with local adaptation.
2. Make entry-mode choices explicit about control, risk, capital, and knowledge.
3. Research the host context before arrival; listen more than you speak.
4. Never confuse legal permission to enter a market with social or political acceptance.
