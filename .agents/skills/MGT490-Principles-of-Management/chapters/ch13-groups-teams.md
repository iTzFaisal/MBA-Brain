# Chapter 13: Groups and Teams: Increasing Cooperation, Reducing Conflict

## Core Idea
A group shares norms, goals, and identity; a team adds a common purpose, performance goals, and mutual accountability. Managers build effective teams by designing trust, goals, composition, roles, norms, interdependence, and processes, then use conflict constructively.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Team types**: Work, project, cross-functional, self-managed, and virtual teams.
- **Tuckman's Five-Stage Model**: Forming, storming, norming, performing, and adjourning.
- **Punctuated equilibrium**: A stable group can be disrupted by a major event, then establish new norms, roles, and objectives.
- **Eight effective-team considerations**: Collaboration; trust; measurable performance goals and feedback; mutual accountability/interdependence; composition; roles; norms; effective team processes.
- **Trust Triangle**: Authenticity, logic, and empathy.
- **Five conflict-handling styles**: Avoiding, obliging, dominating, compromising, and integrating.
- **Programmed conflict**: Devil's advocacy assigns a critic; the dialectic method structures debate between opposing viewpoints.

## Key Concepts
- **Formal group**: Organization-established group with a leader and productive purpose.
- **Informal group**: Social group formed without an officially appointed leader.
- **Team composition**: Jobs, personalities, values, knowledge, experience, and skills represented.
- **Norms**: Often unwritten rules of acceptable behavior.
- **Team charter**: Agreement covering purpose, roles, norms, decision rights, and accountability.
- **Functional conflict**: Disagreement serving organizational purposes.
- **Dysfunctional conflict**: Disagreement hindering performance or organizational interests.
- **Team reflexivity**: Collective reflection followed by adaptation.

## Mental Models
- Do not call a unit a team until members share purpose, performance goals, and accountability.
- Too little conflict produces apathy; too much produces warfare. The goal is constructive disagreement.
- Match conflict style to stakes, time pressure, power, relationship, and complexity.

## Anti-patterns
- **Team label without interdependence**: Calling individually accountable workers a team.
- **Stage determinism**: Assuming every team progresses through stages on the same schedule.
- **Virtual-team neglect**: Removing structure, documentation, trust-building, and feedback.
- **Dominant voice**: Allowing one or two people to control information and decisions.
- **Personality conflict**: Attacking people instead of discussing observable behavior and task effects.

## Worked Example
COVID-19 forced late-night television teams from studios into homes. Writers lost rapid face-to-face collaboration and live audience feedback; production crews had to use phones, videoconferencing, new roles, and new norms. The disruption illustrates punctuated equilibrium, possible regression to earlier Tuckman stages, the value of nonverbal cues, and the need to rebuild process, trust, feedback, and accountability.

## Key Takeaways
1. Design the team before asking it to perform.
2. Establish trust, measurable goals, roles, norms, and feedback early.
3. Allow minority dissent and use structured debate to improve decisions.
4. Treat conflict as potentially useful information, not automatically as failure.
5. End projects with a post-mortem and carry learning into the next charter.
