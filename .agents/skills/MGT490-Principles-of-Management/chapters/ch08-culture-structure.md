# Chapter 8: Organizational Culture and Structure: Drivers of Strategic Implementation

## Core Idea
Strategy becomes behavior through alignment among organizational culture, structure, and HR practices. Managers must diagnose what people actually assume and do, choose structures that coordinate work, and use multiple levers when changing culture.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Culture-structure-HR alignment**: Align organizational culture, formal structure, and HR practices with strategy; alignment shapes work attitudes, behavior, and performance.
- **Schein's three culture levels**: Observable artifacts; espoused values; basic, taken-for-granted assumptions.
  - When to use: To distinguish visible symbols from what is truly rewarded and believed.
- **Culture transmission**: Symbols, stories, heroes, rites and rituals, and organizational socialization.
- **Competing Values Framework**: Internal/external focus crossed with flexibility/stability creates clan, adhocracy, market, and hierarchy cultures.
- **Twelve culture-change levers**: Formal statements; slogans; rites; stories; leader reactions to crises; role modeling/training/coaching; physical design; rewards/titles/promotions/bonuses; goals and performance criteria; measurable activities; structure; systems and procedures.
- **Seven organizational features**: Common purpose, coordinated effort, division of labor, hierarchy of authority, span of control, authority/accountability/responsibility/delegation, and centralization versus decentralization.
- **Eight structures**: Simple, functional, divisional, matrix, horizontal, hollow, modular, and virtual.

## Key Concepts
- **Organizational culture**: Shared, taken-for-granted assumptions that shape perception, thought, and reaction.
- **Organizational structure**: Formal task and reporting relationships that coordinate and motivate members.
- **Espoused values**: What the organization says it values.
- **Enacted values**: What the organization actually rewards and practices.
- **Person-organization fit**: Compatibility between a person's values and the organization's context.
- **Delegation**: Assigning responsibility and authority while retaining accountability.
- **Span of control**: Number of people reporting directly to a manager.

## Mental Models
- Read culture by comparing artifacts, speeches, rewards, crisis responses, and everyday trade-offs.
- Change culture through a portfolio of mutually reinforcing levers, not a slogan.
- Choose structure from strategy and coordination needs; no structure is universally best.

## Anti-patterns
- **Stated-value illusion**: Believing the mission statement while ignoring incentives and leader behavior.
- **Single-lever culture change**: Launching slogans without changing rewards, staffing, systems, or routines.
- **Flatness without clarity**: Removing layers while leaving decision rights and accountability ambiguous.
- **Chart literalism**: Treating a formal organization chart as a complete map of influence.

## Worked Example
Wells Fargo's "Going for Gr-Eight" sales culture rewarded bankers and managers for cross-selling accounts. The practice improved reported growth but created pressure to open unauthorized accounts, including for family members, and led to major fines and reputational damage. The case shows how goals, rewards, leader expectations, and measurable activities can embed a culture that contradicts formal policies and ethics.

## Key Takeaways
1. Align strategy, culture, structure, and HR practices before expecting execution.
2. Diagnose the gap between espoused and enacted values.
3. Use multiple culture levers and measure behavior, not only attitudes.
4. Match structure to size, specialization, speed, accountability, and coordination needs.
5. Assess person-organization fit as a two-way context question, not as similarity to incumbents.
