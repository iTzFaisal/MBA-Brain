# Chapter 9: Management of Quality

## Core Idea
Quality is the ability of a product or service to consistently meet or exceed customer expectations, so it is customer-dependent rather than an abstract property. Effective quality management translates customer wants into measurable specifications, designs quality into products and processes, prevents defects, reduces variation, and involves the entire organization and supply chain in continuous improvement.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Quality dimensions and determinants**: Product quality is judged through performance, aesthetics, special features, conformance, reliability, durability, perceived quality, serviceability, and consistency; price is not itself a quality dimension. Service quality adds convenience, reliability, responsiveness, time, assurance, courtesy, tangibles, consistency, and meeting expectations. SERVQUAL audits tangibles, reliability, responsiveness, assurance, and empathy. The four determinants are quality of design, quality of conformance, ease of use, and service after delivery.
- **Quality gurus**: Shewhart introduced control charts and variation reduction. Deming's 14 points make management responsible for improving the system, reducing variation, distinguishing common from special causes, developing people, removing fear and barriers, and replacing inspection, quotas, and slogans with leadership and learning. Juran defined quality as fitness-for-use and proposed the quality trilogy: planning, control, and improvement; he also emphasized that much quality loss is management-controllable. Feigenbaum treated quality as a total field defined by the customer. Crosby advocated prevention, zero defects, "do it right the first time," and quality is free because prevention can cost less than poor quality. Ishikawa developed cause-and-effect diagrams, quality circles, and the internal-customer idea. Taguchi's loss function treats every deviation from target as a cost to society. Ohno and Shingo advanced kaizen, or continuous improvement.
- **Cost of quality**: Prevention costs reduce the chance of defects; appraisal costs inspect, test, audit, and measure; internal failure costs arise before delivery through scrap, rework, downtime, and investigation; external failure costs arise after delivery through returns, warranties, complaints, liability, lost goodwill, and lost sales. Return on quality evaluates improvement projects as investments rather than assuming every quality expenditure is automatically worthwhile.
- **Awards and certification**: The Baldrige Award uses a broad excellence framework covering leadership, strategy, customers, measurement/knowledge, workforce, operations, and results. The European Quality Award recognizes organizational excellence. The Deming Prize is narrower, emphasizing statistical quality control and deeply deployed quality programs. ISO 9000 addresses process-based quality management; ISO 14000 addresses environmental management; ISO 24700 addresses performance and conformity of office equipment containing reused components. ISO certification requires documented procedures, assessment, continuing audits, and periodic re-registration.
- **TQM and Six Sigma**: Total quality management is a culture of customer satisfaction, everyone’s involvement, and never-ending improvement. Six Sigma applies data and structured projects to reduce variation and defects, with a statistical goal of no more than 3.4 defects per million opportunities. Its DMAIC cycle is Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control.
- **PDSA and process improvement**: Plan-Do-Study-Act provides a repeating improvement loop: understand the current process and measures, test a change, study results against goals, then standardize or revise. Process improvement maps inputs, outputs, people, decisions, time, cost, waste, safety, quality, and satisfaction; analyzes value and waste at each step; then redesigns and measures the result.

## Key Concepts
- **Quality at the source**: Each worker owns, checks, and corrects the quality of work before passing it to the next internal customer.
- **Fail-safing (poka-yoke)**: Design features that make an incorrect action difficult or impossible.
- **Continuous improvement (kaizen)**: Ongoing improvement of equipment, methods, materials, and people, even when the process is already functioning.
- **Special versus common causes**: Special causes are identifiable and correctable; common causes are random variation inherent in the system.
- **Supply-chain quality**: Vendor materials, methods, lead times, and delivery reliability affect final customer quality and organizational liability.
- **Ethics and quality**: Concealing known defects, delaying recalls, or withholding safety information shifts harm to customers and is unethical.

## Mental Models
- **Prevent rather than inspect**: Inspection finds some failures after resources have been consumed; prevention changes design, inputs, training, or process conditions so failure is less likely.
- **The system creates the result**: Before blaming an operator, examine design, equipment, methods, materials, training, incentives, and management conditions.
- **Critical few first**: Use the Pareto effect to identify the small number of causes or categories producing most of the loss, then verify improvement with new data.
- **Quality is an end-to-end promise**: Customer expectations begin with design, continue through use and service, and depend on suppliers as well as internal operations.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating quality as inspection or a quick fix**: It encourages reactive correction, misses root causes, and increases failure costs.
- **Blaming workers, using quotas, slogans, or fear**: These hide system problems and undermine learning, pride, and improvement.
- **Launching TQM without strategy or leadership**: A companywide definition, resources, education, empowerment, communication, and visible top-management commitment are prerequisites.
- **Optimizing quality blindly**: Quality efforts must connect to strategy, market results, and economics; incremental improvement is insufficient when a breakthrough is needed.
- **Ignoring suppliers or hiding defects**: Outsourcing does not transfer liability, and concealing known problems magnifies customer, safety, and legal consequences.

## Worked Example
For 20 sheriff’s tickets, a check sheet counts parking violations (10), excessive speed (5), improper turns (3), and expired inspections (2). A Pareto chart orders the categories from highest to lowest, making parking violations the first improvement target rather than treating all infractions equally. The team would define a reduction goal, investigate likely causes with a cause-and-effect diagram and targeted questions, implement a focused change, and use a follow-up check sheet or run/control chart to test whether the dominant category actually declines. The example shows the sequence: collect trustworthy data, prioritize the critical few, investigate causes, act, and verify.

## Key Takeaways
1. Define quality from customer expectations, then convert expectations into measurable design and process requirements.
2. Build quality into design and conformance; use inspection as information, not as the primary quality strategy.
3. Prevention, capable processes, and early correction usually reduce total cost more effectively than allowing external failures.
4. TQM is an organizational culture supported by PDSA, quality at the source, teams, empowerment, facts, benchmarking, and supplier partnership.
5. Use simple tools in sequence: flowchart and check sheet for process and data, histogram and scatter diagram for patterns, Pareto for priorities, fishbone for causes, and run/control charts for time-based verification.
6. Quality improvement is strategic only when leadership, employees, suppliers, customer value, and business results are aligned.
