# Chapter 2: Competitiveness, Strategy, and Productivity

## Core Idea
Competitiveness is an organization's effectiveness relative to rivals in meeting customer needs. It depends on coherent choices about price, delivery time, quality, differentiation, flexibility, service, and productivity. Strategy turns the mission into coordinated decisions; productivity shows how effectively resources are converted into output and therefore directly affects cost and competitive position.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three primary business strategies**: compete through **low cost**, **responsiveness**, and/or **differentiation**. Differentiation may be based on quality, newness, variety, service, convenience, or location. Use one dominant strategy when focus matters; combine strategies only when the organization can support the resulting complexity.
- **Mission-to-operations hierarchy**: mission (reason for existence) -> goals (scope and detail) -> organizational and functional strategies (roadmaps) -> tactics (specific methods) -> operations (actual work). Functional strategies must support, not conflict with, the organization's mission and goals.
- **Strategy formulation**: (1) link strategy to mission or vision, (2) assess **SWOT** and identify core competencies, (3) identify **order qualifiers** and **order winners**, then (4) select one or two strategic priorities. Use environmental scanning and, where useful, **Porter's five forces** to test external threats and opportunities.
- **Operations strategy alignment**: translate organizational priorities into choices about product/service design, capacity, process and layout, work design, location, quality, inventory, maintenance, scheduling, supply chains, and projects. Operations strategy is narrower than organization strategy but makes it executable.
- **Quality-based, time-based, and agile strategies**: quality-based strategies improve quality throughout the system. Time-based strategies reduce planning, design, processing, changeover, delivery, and complaint-response time. **Agile operations** blends cost, quality, reliability, and flexibility so the system can adapt to changing volume, mix, and customer requirements.
- **Balanced Scorecard (BSC)**: transform an existing vision and strategy into objectives, initiatives, performance measures, and targets across financial performance, customer/stakeholder satisfaction, internal business processes, and organizational knowledge/innovation. It balances financial/nonfinancial, internal/external, and past/future performance; it supports execution, not strategy formulation.
- **Productivity measurement**: use partial productivity for one input, multifactor productivity for selected inputs, and total productivity for all inputs. Choose the measure for the decision, and use usable output when defects or scrap matter.

## Key Concepts
- **Competitiveness**: relative success in satisfying customers compared with organizations offering similar goods or services.
- **Core competencies**: distinctive attributes or abilities that create a competitive edge.
- **Order qualifier**: minimum acceptable characteristic needed to be considered for purchase.
- **Order winner**: characteristic that makes an offering preferable to competitors.
- **Environmental scanning**: monitoring competitors, customers, technology, legal, political, economic, supplier, market, and environmental developments.
- **Operations strategy**: the operations approach, consistent with organization strategy, governing products, processes, resources, quality, cost, lead time, and scheduling.
- **Productivity**: effective resource use, expressed as output relative to input.
- **Process yield**: good output divided by raw-material input, or the process-specific equivalent for services.
- **Efficiency**: getting the most from a fixed resource set; productivity is broader because it can include changing the resources or technology.

## Mental Models
- **Qualifiers are the ticket; winners are the reason to buy.** Quality, price, speed, and reliability can be either depending on the market, and an order winner can become a qualifier over time.
- **Treat strategy as a fit problem.** Match customer priorities and competitor baselines to internal capabilities, then make operations, marketing, finance, and supply chain reinforce the same choice.
- **Optimize the system, not a local ratio.** Improving one step does not improve total output if another step remains the bottleneck. A two-operation system limited to 20 units per hour stays at 20 even if the first operation becomes faster.
- **Time reduction compounds.** Shorter cycle, changeover, delivery, and response times can reduce cost, raise productivity and quality, accelerate innovation, and improve service simultaneously.

## Anti-patterns
- **Neglecting operations strategy** or treating it as subordinate to marketing or short-term finance; this leaves the organization unable to deliver its promise.
- **Designing products without designing the process**, or failing to invest in capital, people, R&D, and cross-functional communication.
- **Trying to be everything to everyone** without enough capability or focus; multiple strategic priorities can produce no defensible advantage.
- **Deploying technology without fit or planning**; learning curves, inflexibility, high costs, or mismatched processes can lower productivity.
- **Confusing labor intensity with poor productivity** or rewarding apparent output while ignoring quality, scrap, burnout, turnover, and system constraints.

## Worked Example
Productivity calculations must use the relevant input and usable output:

- Four workers install 720 square yards in eight hours: labor input is `4 x 8 = 32` labor-hours, so labor productivity is `720 / 32 = 22.5` square yards per labor-hour.
- A machine makes 70 pieces in two hours, but two are unusable: usable-output productivity is `(70 - 2) / 2 = 34` good pieces per hour.
- If output is 7,040 units and labor, materials, and overhead cost $1,000, $520, and $2,000, multifactor productivity is `7,040 / (1,000 + 520 + 2,000) = 2` units per dollar of input.
- Productivity growth is `(current productivity - previous productivity) / previous productivity x 100`; an increase from 80 to 84 is 5%.

The strategic logic appears in the examples. Dell gained agility by selling directly, outsourcing components, and carrying little inventory: lower inventory cost and less obsolete stock enabled faster adoption of new chips. A soft-cookie bakery raises productivity through automation in mixing, longer ovens, diagonal cuts, sequencing batches to reduce cleaning, heat reclamation, and reuse of scrap, while retaining manual packing to protect local employment. USPS combined automation, ZIP codes, reorganization, customer feedback, cost control, quality, and service to respond to electronic substitution and private delivery competitors.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start with customer needs and distinguish order qualifiers from order winners.
2. Align mission, goals, organization strategy, functional strategy, tactics, and daily operations.
3. Use SWOT, core competencies, environmental scanning, and competitive forces before committing resources.
4. Every strategy creates operations consequences: low-cost/high-volume favors standardization, while variety and customization require flexibility, capacity, skills, inventory, and more complex scheduling.
5. Measure productivity consistently, include quality and usable output, and improve the whole system rather than an isolated activity.
6. Higher productivity lowers unit cost, supports competitive pricing or higher profit, limits inflationary pressure, and contributes to national living standards.

## Connects To
- **Supply chain strategy**: supplier relationships, outsourcing, delivery, sustainability, and agility must reinforce business strategy.
- **Quality and process management**: quality, standardization, scrap reduction, and continuous improvement affect both productivity and competitiveness.
- **Capacity, inventory, scheduling, technology, and work design**: each is an operations decision through which strategy becomes observable performance.
- **Sustainability strategy**: environmental, community, and employee effects belong in governance, objectives, measurement, and operating choices rather than being treated as external to competitiveness.
