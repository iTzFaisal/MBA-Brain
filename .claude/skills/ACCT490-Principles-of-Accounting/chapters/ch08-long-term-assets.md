# Chapter 8: Reporting and Analyzing Long-Term Assets

## Core Idea
Long-term assets provide benefits across several accounting periods, so accounting first measures the cost of acquiring and preparing them, then allocates that cost to the periods benefited. The central decisions are whether a cost is an asset or expense, which allocation pattern fits usage, and what disposal or analysis reveals about asset productivity.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Plant-asset cost procedure**: Use when acquiring machinery, equipment, buildings, land improvements, or land. Capitalize normal, reasonable, necessary costs to get the asset in place and ready for intended use: invoice price net of discount, taxes, freight, insurance in transit, assembly, installation, testing, and necessary site preparation. Expense damage, fines, maintenance, and other costs that do not prepare the asset.
- **Lump-sum allocation**: Use when one price buys several assets. Allocate the total price using each asset's relative appraised or market value: `asset share = total price x asset appraisal / total appraisals`. Keep land, land improvements, and buildings in separate accounts because land is normally not depreciated.
- **Depreciation allocation**: Use for a plant asset with a finite useful life. Determine cost, estimated salvage value, and useful life; depreciable cost is `cost - salvage value`. Depreciation allocates cost, not market-value decline or physical deterioration, and begins when the asset is in service.
- **Straight-line method**: Use when benefits are expected to be even across periods. `Annual depreciation = (cost - salvage value) / useful life in periods`; debit Depreciation Expense and credit Accumulated Depreciation.
- **Units-of-production method**: Use when output or usage drives consumption. `Depreciation per unit = (cost - salvage value) / estimated total units`; `period expense = rate per unit x units used`. Do not reduce book value below salvage value.
- **Double-declining-balance method**: Use an accelerated pattern when earlier periods receive more benefit. `DDB rate = 2 / useful life`; multiply the rate by beginning book value, but cap the charge so ending book value does not fall below salvage value.
- **Partial-year and estimate revision procedure**: Use month-based depreciation for purchases and disposals during a year; the source convention treats days 1-15 as the first of the month and days 16-end as the next month. If useful life or salvage changes, spread the current book value less revised salvage over the revised remaining life prospectively; do not restate prior periods.
- **Subsequent-expenditure test**: Expense ordinary repairs that maintain normal condition. Capitalize betterments and extraordinary repairs that increase productivity or extend useful life, then depreciate the revised amount over the remaining benefit period.
- **Four-step disposal procedure**: (1) update depreciation to disposal date, (2) remove the asset and accumulated depreciation, (3) record cash or other consideration, and (4) compare value received with book value to record a gain or loss.
- **Depletion and amortization**: For natural resources, compute `(cost - salvage value) / estimated units of capacity`, then assign the per-unit amount to units extracted: sold units become expense/COGS and unsold units become inventory. For limited-life intangibles, normally use straight-line amortization over the shorter of useful and legal life; indefinite-life intangibles are not amortized but are tested for impairment.
- **Total asset turnover analysis**: Use to assess how efficiently total resources generate sales: `Net sales / average total assets`. Compare with prior years and similar companies; capital-intensive businesses normally turn assets less frequently than labor-intensive businesses.

## Key Concepts
- **Plant assets**: Tangible operating assets used for more than one accounting period.
- **Depreciable cost**: Plant-asset cost less estimated salvage value.
- **Salvage value**: Expected proceeds from disposal less expected disposal costs.
- **Useful life**: Period an asset is productively used by the company, which may be shorter than physical life.
- **Book value**: Asset cost less accumulated depreciation, depletion, or amortization.
- **Accumulated depreciation**: Credit-balance contra asset that preserves original cost while tracking allocated cost.
- **Natural resources**: Wasting assets physically consumed through extraction, cutting, or pumping.
- **Intangible assets**: Nonphysical operating rights or advantages such as patents, copyrights, licenses, leaseholds, and goodwill.
- **Capital expenditure**: Future-benefit cost recorded as an asset rather than current expense.
- **Impairment**: Permanent decline requiring a write-down when an asset's carrying amount exceeds the applicable recoverable or fair amount.

## Mental Models
- **Use the readiness test**: Capitalize a cost only if it is normal, reasonable, and necessary to put the asset into intended service; otherwise ask which current period received the benefit.
- **Match the pattern to consumption**: Use straight-line for even passage of benefit, units-of-production for variable usage, and DDB for front-loaded benefits. The method changes timing, not total depreciable cost.
- **Think of disposal as a balance-sheet cleanup**: Update first, remove both gross cost and accumulated depreciation, then let proceeds versus book value determine gain or loss.
- **Read turnover in context**: A low ratio may reflect capital intensity rather than poor management; compare it with margins, asset age, operations, and competitors.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating depreciation as a valuation estimate**: It allocates historical cost and does not automatically track market price.
- **Depreciating land or ignoring salvage**: Land normally has an indefinite life, and salvage limits total depreciation.
- **Expensing a betterment or capitalizing ordinary repairs**: Either choice distorts the timing of income and the asset balance.
- **Using the unadjusted book value at disposal**: Omitted partial-period depreciation creates a false gain or loss.
- **Amortizing goodwill or indefinite-life intangibles automatically**: Test for impairment instead; amortization applies to limited lives.
- **Recording depletion entirely as expense when extracted material remains unsold**: The unsold portion belongs in inventory until sold.

## Worked Example
An athletic-shoe inspection machine costs $10,000, has $1,000 salvage value, a five-year life, and an estimated 36,000-shoe capacity. Its depreciable cost is $9,000. Straight-line depreciation is `$9,000 / 5 = $1,800` each year. If the first year inspects 7,000 shoes, units-of-production depreciation is `($9,000 / 36,000) x 7,000 = $1,750`. DDB uses `2 / 5 = 40%`: first-year depreciation is `$10,000 x 40% = $4,000`; later charges are 40% of beginning book value, except the final charge is capped at the amount needed to reach $1,000 salvage. The DDB schedule is $4,000, $2,400, $1,440, $864, and $296, totaling the same $9,000. Under straight-line, the annual entry is `Dr Depreciation Expense $1,800 / Cr Accumulated Depreciation $1,800`; the asset's book value after two years is `$10,000 - $3,600 = $6,400`.

## Key Takeaways
1. Classify the asset by how the company uses it, then capitalize only normal and necessary readiness costs.
2. Allocate a lump-sum purchase by relative market values, not by arbitrary account amounts.
3. State cost, salvage, useful life, and usage assumptions before calculating depreciation.
4. Revise depreciation estimates prospectively using the current book value and remaining life.
5. Expense ordinary repairs; capitalize productivity improvements and life-extending repairs.
6. Update depreciation before every disposal and compare proceeds with book value.
7. Use total asset turnover with business-model and competitor context, not as a standalone score.
