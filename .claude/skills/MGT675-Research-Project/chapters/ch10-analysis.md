# Chapter 10: What is an Analysis?

## Core Idea

Analysis examines the integrity of an article: whether its foundation, design, evidence, interpretation, and conclusions are sound enough to use. It is not a summary and it is not the final decision about relevance. First decide whether the article addresses the question; then analyze the quality and limits of what it contributes.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Article Analysis Checklist**: Test internal soundness before assigning evidentiary weight.
  - When to use: Apply after an article passes the basic relevance screen and before using it in comparison, contrast, synthesis, or integration.
  - How: Ask whether the logic and theoretical framework are sound; whether the question is sufficiently narrow and testable; whether the research design fits the question; whether the sample is appropriate, sufficiently large, and biased or unbiased; whether the sources are sufficient and fairly used; whether the data were interpreted appropriately; whether conclusions are clear, limited, and supported; and whether the contributor has relevant credentials and uses quality sources.
- **Analysis as a Value Judgment**: Preserve partial value without hiding weaknesses.
  - When to use: Use when an article has serious limitations but raises a question or offers a useful perspective.
  - How: State the important finding briefly, identify strengths and weaknesses, explain how the design and evidence affect confidence, and specify whether the article is useful as evidence, as a tentative lead, or only as a perspective that the review considered and bounded.

## Key Concepts

- **Integrity**: The soundness of an article's logic, evidence, method, and conclusions.
- **Theoretical framework**: The assumptions and prior research that establish why the study and question make sense.
- **Research design**: The method used to answer the question.
- **Sample**: The people, organizations, documents, or cases from which evidence is gathered.
- **Interpretation**: The author's explanation of what the data mean.
- **Limitation**: A condition that restricts confidence, scope, or applicability.
- **Correlation**: Co-occurrence that does not by itself demonstrate causation.
- **Article value**: The usefulness of an article's sound evidence, partial insight, or research lead to the current review.

## Mental Models

- Use **method limits conclusion**: the strength and scope of a claim cannot exceed what its design and evidence support.
- Separate **relevance from integrity**. A perfectly conducted study on another question is not useful evidence for the current claim; a relevant but weak study must be used cautiously.
- Treat a weak article as a **question generator**, not necessarily as useless. Its limitations may prevent a conclusion while its possibility merits further research.
- Read credentials as **context**, not proof. A respected author can still make a poorly designed or overgeneralized argument.

## Anti-patterns

- **Summarizing without judging**: Readers learn what the article said but not why they should trust or use it.
- **Accepting a result because it sounds plausible**: Plausibility does not repair an unsuitable sample, measure, or design.
- **Confusing correlation with causation**: A relationship observed in a limited sample cannot establish a universal causal mechanism.
- **Rejecting every imperfect article**: Omitting a weak but relevant perspective can make the review look selective and incomplete.
- **Writing a credentials-based verdict**: Authority cannot replace examination of evidence and logic.

## Worked Example

The chapter's caffeine-and-ADHD example illustrates two analyses. In the weak version, a study reports a correlation between teenagers' coffee consumption and ADHD behavior, but draws participants from three classrooms in one inner-city high school, does not describe other caffeine sources or relevant life factors, gives no clear threshold for "high" consumption, and does not report correlation strength or measure reliability. The evidence cannot support a broad conclusion, although the possible relationship warrants further study.

In the stronger version, the researcher measures multiple caffeine sources with a Likert-style scale, conducts a complete correlational analysis, and reports a preliminary positive association while acknowledging that the sample is too small for a definitive relationship. A useful review paragraph would state the finding, assess the design and sample, preserve the limitation, and assign the article a bounded role rather than calling it proof.

## Key Takeaways

1. Start with logic, question, design, sample, evidence, interpretation, and conclusion.
2. State limitations as part of the article's meaning, not as an afterthought.
3. Do not let credentials substitute for method and evidence.
4. A flawed article can identify a worthwhile question without proving its answer.
5. Analysis determines how an article may be used in later critical-thinking operations.
