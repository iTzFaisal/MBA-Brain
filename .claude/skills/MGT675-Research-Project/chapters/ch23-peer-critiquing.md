# Chapter 23: Peer Critiquing

## Core Idea
Peer critique creates distance from a draft so weaknesses in logic, content, organization, and presentation become visible. When another reviewer is unavailable, simulate the process objectively through a **Critique of Literature Reviews (COLR)**, a structured outline, deliberate time away, and focused reading.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Critique of Literature Reviews (COLR)**: Use to review another person's literature review, a published review, or your own draft. Outline the writer's points and cited authors' points, then test each supporting article for appropriateness, intended content, concision, integration, and effectiveness of the point.
- **Peer review of your own work**: Use after a break of two weeks or more when possible. Read for methods, content, and logic rather than familiarity, and work in sections if the paper is long.
- **Peer critique outline**: Use a hierarchical outline that records the introduction, topic question and subquestions, why the issue matters, topic sections, source strengths and weaknesses, examination points, and the writer's conclusions.
- **Objectivity through reading boundaries**: Use one sitting for a selected review section when preserving flow is important; for your own long paper, read no more than about 20 pages at a time to avoid skimming and fatigue.

## Key Concepts
- **Peer critique**: A deliberate review of a literature review to identify effective and ineffective reasoning and presentation.
- **COLR**: The structured Critique of Literature Reviews process and outline.
- **Objectivity**: The ability to inspect a familiar draft as a reader rather than protect it as its author.
- **Logic pattern**: The sequence by which questions, evidence, comparisons, and conclusions support one another.
- **Article appropriateness**: Whether a source is suitable for the point it is used to support.
- **Integration quality**: Whether evidence is connected so the reader can follow the argument.
- **Examination point**: The reviewer's conclusion about how sources fit, differ, or support an observation.
- **Narrative abstract**: A concise account of the review, limited in the chapter activity to no more than 250 words.

## Mental Models
- **Critique is a learning loop.** Examine another review, reflect on the weaknesses you notice, and convert those insights into a plan for your own draft.
- **Outline the argument before judging the prose.** Mapping claims, evidence, and transitions exposes missing or unsupported steps that line editing can hide.
- **Use distance as an instrument.** Time away, section limits, and attentive reading reduce the familiarity that makes writers complete unfinished thoughts in their heads.

## Anti-patterns
- Reviewing immediately after drafting and mistaking familiarity for quality.
- Reading a long review in one exhausted pass and calling the resulting skim an objective critique.
- Commenting on grammar without testing article choice, evidence, logic, integration, and conclusions.
- Giving general reactions such as "unclear" without locating the claim, source, or transition that fails.
- Critiquing only another person's work and failing to apply the same questions to your own review.

## Worked Example
For a review asking whether Fortune 500 management training could improve teachers' classroom management, a COLR outline records the main question, subquestions about teacher and business-manager roles, current training, effectiveness, and similarities between programs. Under a topic section, the outline notes that one source describes teachers as caregivers but uses a small sample limited to younger students, while another describes teachers as perceptive and interviews teachers across age groups. The critique then marks their roles as complementary, notes the sample limitation, and asks whether the writer connected the evidence to classroom-management effectiveness. This outline gives the writer a concrete revision plan rather than a vague verdict.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use COLR to review methods, content, logic, and presentation together.
2. Create distance before self-review; two weeks is the preferred break when time permits.
3. Outline questions, source claims, evidence quality, examination points, and conclusions.
4. For each article, ask whether it is appropriate, accurate to its intended use, concise, integrated, and effective.
5. Turn critique findings into a concrete plan to improve your own work.
6. Rewrite the review narrative in a short form when you need to clarify the argument for yourself or others.
