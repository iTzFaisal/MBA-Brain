# Chapter 27: Formulating Introductions

## Core Idea
An effective literature-review introduction catches attention while orienting the reader immediately to the basic topic, its importance, and the writer's perspective or interest. It should flow into the review rather than announce the topic mechanically, and each paragraph should move from a focused topic sentence through support to a transition.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three-part literature-review introduction**: Use the opening to state or clearly describe the **topic**, explain why it is **important**, and identify the **perspective or interest** that guides the review. Then segue into the literature.
- **Topic sentence-detail-transition paragraph**: Use in the introduction and throughout the review. Start with the paragraph's main point, add sentences that describe or explain it, and end with a sentence that leads to the next paragraph or topic.
- **Direct analytic introduction**: Use for a literature review, article, or study when readers need a simple and direct entry into the problem and scope.
- **Literary introduction**: Use sparingly for a larger dissertation or substantial work when the consumer accepts a brief narrative opening; it is normally not the best opening for a Chapter 2 literature review.

## Key Concepts
- **Introduction**: The opening that establishes topic, importance, perspective, and direction.
- **Topic**: The subject or problem the review examines.
- **Importance**: The reason the topic deserves attention and the answer to the initial "so what?"
- **Perspective**: The angle, setting, population, or interest through which the review examines the topic.
- **Topic sentence**: The sentence that tells the reader what a paragraph is about.
- **Supporting detail**: Explanation, evidence, or context that develops the topic sentence.
- **Transition**: A closing or linking statement that carries the reader into the next idea.
- **Consumer**: The supervisor, committee, client, or reader whose expectations shape an acceptable opening.

## Mental Models
- **Make the first paragraph a contract.** Tell readers what matters, what viewpoint is being used, and what evidence they are about to encounter.
- **Use the framework as the hook when it is stronger than personal biography.** A clear theoretical or practical implication can create interest without announcing "this is my topic."
- **Think in paragraph umbrellas.** Every supporting sentence should fit under the topic sentence, and the final sentence should prepare the next umbrella.

## Anti-patterns
- Opening with a dry label such as "This paper is about..." and never explaining why the topic matters.
- Beginning with a dramatic literary scene when a short literature review requires direct scope and purpose.
- Introducing a claim, population, or perspective in the opening that the body never examines.
- Packing several unrelated topics into one paragraph so no topic sentence can control it.
- Delaying the topic until after background material and forcing the reader to guess the review's purpose.

## Worked Example
An introduction about democratic classrooms can begin with the contrast between autocratic management and student participation, explain that democratic practices may affect belonging, communication, respect, and classroom behavior, and then identify the review's interest in whether a particular school's model improves student attitudes. The next paragraph can move to limits and competing influences such as family support, prior school experiences, socioeconomic conditions, and work demands. A direct literature-review opening gives the reader the problem and perspective; a longer literary scene about historical events would belong, if at all, in a larger dissertation introduction rather than this review opening.

## Key Takeaways
1. Identify the topic in the initial paragraph without relying on a formulaic announcement.
2. Explain why the topic matters and what perspective guides the review.
3. Use topic sentences, supporting detail, and transitions to make the opening easy to follow.
4. Prefer a direct analytic opening for a literature review; reserve literary openings for larger works and suitable consumers.
5. Read introductions to peer-reviewed articles and dissertation reviews to see legitimate variation in form.
