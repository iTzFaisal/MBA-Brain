# Chapter 2: The Literature Review Process

## Core Idea

A literature review is a systematic examination of scholarly and research-based information about a specific topic. Its job is to represent what is known, including competing perspectives and remaining gaps, so that a research study, program, proposal, or decision rests on reality rather than assumption.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Three Types of Literature Reviews**: Match the review to its purpose.
  - When to use: Choose before searching, because purpose determines the expected rigor and source mix.
  - How: A **simple literature review** gives a brief overview and may use popular sources, while labeling their type and value. An **applied literature review** supports business, government, nonprofit, or professional planning; combine practical or local information with research-based sources and emphasize objectivity and accuracy. An **academic literature review** supports coursework or a study and normally requires peer-reviewed sources, thoroughness, strong analysis, organization, and depth.
- **Question and Subquestion Narrowing**: Turn an interesting problem into a workable review.
  - When to use: Apply when a topic involves too many factors, locations, populations, or time periods to examine thoroughly.
  - How: Draft several versions of the main question. Identify the subquestions needed to answer each version. Limit the population, setting, time period, factors, and literature type until the review can be comprehensive without becoming unmanageable. Keep only the dimensions that serve the purpose of the review.
- **Local Sources Versus Research-Based Sources**: Use each source type for the work it can legitimately do.
  - When to use: Apply when the topic has both general research and situation-specific information.
  - How: Use peer-reviewed research to establish evidence and broader knowledge. Use local sources to describe events, context, statistics, opinions, or human reactions, but evaluate their bias and do not treat them as equivalent to objective research. The appropriate ratio depends on the review type and its goal.

## Key Concepts

- **Literature review**: An examination of available knowledge and research-based theory on a defined topic.
- **Body of knowledge**: The accumulated research and informed understanding that a new review or study can extend.
- **Objectivity**: Considering relevant evidence and perspectives without allowing personal interests or an initial position to control the result.
- **Research question**: The question that defines what the review must discover and explain.
- **Subquestion**: A smaller question whose answer is necessary to address the larger question.
- **Peer-reviewed source**: Research evaluated by field reviewers for its method, evidence, analysis, and conclusions before publication.
- **Local source**: Situation-specific material such as news reports, records, histories, broadcasts, or participant accounts.
- **Research gap**: Knowledge still needed after the existing literature has been examined.

## Mental Models

- Use a literature review as a **map of reality**, not as a collection of support for a preferred conclusion.
- Think of narrowing as **scope control**: a smaller question that can be answered thoroughly is stronger than a broad question treated superficially.
- Use the **spectrum-of-perspectives test**: after finding several sources that agree, deliberately search for different conclusions, limitations, and explanations.
- Treat the review as the foundation for action. If the foundation is inaccurate or biased, a polished proposal or research design will still address the wrong problem.

## Anti-patterns

- **Starting with an unbounded problem**: The number of variables and sources makes thorough analysis impossible.
- **Writing a research report instead of a review**: Selecting only literature that supports the initial perspective creates advocacy, not an objective account.
- **Treating popular or local material as peer-reviewed evidence**: These sources may reveal reactions or context, but they may also be biased or incomplete.
- **Reading everything available**: More material does not create a better review when peripheral sources dilute the question and obscure the main logic.

## Worked Example

Suppose the initial question is, "What factors make female adolescents in urban areas less likely to enter mathematics and science higher-education programs?" The question is too broad if it requires every cultural, family, school, community, and economic factor across every urban setting. Narrow it by selecting a population and a setting, then create manageable subquestions such as: What educational factors appear in the literature? What cultural or family factors are repeatedly associated with the decision? Which factors have been studied in comparable urban populations?

The resulting review can distinguish what is known from what remains unknown. It can use peer-reviewed studies for patterns and explanations, local or institutional sources for the specific setting, and opposing studies to avoid assuming that gender, location, or school type has one uniform effect. The final question should be narrow enough to support a defensible review and useful enough to guide the next study or decision.

## Key Takeaways

1. Define the purpose and type of review before deciding what counts as adequate evidence.
2. Ask what is known, what is missing, and what competing perspectives exist.
3. Narrow the topic by population, setting, time, factors, and purpose until thorough coverage is possible.
4. Weight peer-reviewed research more heavily, while using local sources for context and human dynamics.
5. Keep the review tied to the question; relevance matters more than volume.
