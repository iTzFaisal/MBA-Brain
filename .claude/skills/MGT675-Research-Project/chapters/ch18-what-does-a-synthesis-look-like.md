# Chapter 18: What Does a Synthesis Look Like?

## Core Idea

A synthesis looks beyond a pair of article characteristics to a larger commonality across the literature. It can close a section, link factors, or establish a theoretical framework. A strong synthesis summarizes only evidence already examined, preserves population boundaries, uses citations where the framework depends on them, and distinguishes study-specific past findings from generalizable ongoing conclusions.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Synthesis Paragraph Pattern**: Turn compatible evidence into a readable framework statement.
  - When to use: Use after the relevant comparisons and analyses have been developed.
  - How: Open with the topic or question. State the larger common pattern. Identify the groups, settings, or conditions associated with each part of the pattern. Cite the relevant literature and explain the implication for the review. Do not introduce an unsupported population or conclusion merely because it would make the paragraph smoother.
- **Multi-Location Synthesis**: Use synthesis as a set of links through the review.
  - When to use: Use when the review examines several factors whose relationships become clearer over time.
  - How: Place smaller syntheses at the end of sections to connect the evidence considered so far. Add a larger synthesis before the conclusion or framework to show how the factors combine. Each synthesis should increase, not merely repeat, the reader's understanding.
- **Synthesis Tense and Citation Rules**: Preserve time and attribution accurately.
  - When to use: Apply while drafting and editing synthesis paragraphs.
  - How: Use past tense for studies, findings, and conclusions that were reported in the past. Use present tense for an ongoing, generalizable conclusion rather than for a particular study report. Cite the literature supporting a framework; only omit a repeated citation when the immediately preceding, clearly cited discussion makes attribution unambiguous and the required style permits it.

## Key Concepts

- **Synthesis paragraph**: A concise explanation of how previously discussed evidence forms a larger common picture.
- **Commonality**: A recurring relationship at a higher level than a single comparison.
- **Section synthesis**: A summary link that closes one part of the review.
- **Framework synthesis**: A culminating account used to justify a study, action plan, or conclusion.
- **Theoretical framework**: A source-grounded explanation used to design or interpret further research.
- **Past tense**: The usual tense for reporting completed studies and their findings.
- **Generalizable conclusion**: An ongoing statement intended to apply to a defined group beyond one study event.
- **Logic trail**: The traceable sequence from discussed evidence to synthesized claim.

## Mental Models

- Treat synthesis as a **capstone**, not a recap. It should show what the combined evidence means at a larger level.
- Use multiple syntheses as **stepping stones**: each one links prior sections and prepares the next question.
- Think of citations as **load-bearing supports** in a framework. The more consequential the claim, the less acceptable it is to leave its support ambiguous.
- Use tense as a **scope signal**: past tense identifies a study event; present tense signals an ongoing claim.

## Anti-patterns

- **Calling a two-source similarity a full synthesis**: Synthesis requires a broader commonality or complementary picture.
- **Generalizing across unstudied groups**: Smooth prose cannot supply missing evidence.
- **Dropping citations in the final framework**: Readers cannot trace the logic back to the literature.
- **Mixing study reports and general claims without tense control**: The scope of the evidence becomes unclear.
- **Introducing new findings in the synthesis**: A synthesis should draw together analyzed material, not surprise the reader with uncited evidence.

## Worked Example

A compact synthesis of college motivation might read conceptually as follows: Studies of rural and urban students found that many viewed college as a chance to escape boredom and experience new challenges. Research on low-income urban students added escape from poverty as a motivation, while research on high-income students identified a desire to avoid disappointing parents and a belief that no other postsecondary option was acceptable.

This works because the paragraph identifies the common topic, groups the compatible findings, and preserves the populations attached to each motivation. It does not claim that every rural student wants to escape poverty, because that group was not addressed. The source's examples also demonstrate that the synthesis can appear at the end of a section, in a later framework, or in an article's theoretical framework. In each location, the writer must protect the logic trail and use the correct tense.

## Key Takeaways

1. Move from individual comparisons to a larger but bounded commonality.
2. Identify the population and context attached to every component of the synthesis.
3. Use section syntheses to build progressively toward the framework.
4. Cite the evidence that carries the framework and preserve source attribution.
5. Use past tense for completed studies and present tense only for defined ongoing conclusions.
