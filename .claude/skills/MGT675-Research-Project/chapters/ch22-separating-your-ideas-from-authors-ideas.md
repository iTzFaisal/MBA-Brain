# Chapter 22: Separating Your Ideas from Authors' Ideas

## Core Idea
A literature review must make it obvious which statements come from cited authors and which are the reviewer's observations, comparisons, evaluations, or inferences. Use citation placement, attribution language, quotation boundaries, and cautious verbs to prevent confusion, misattribution, and doubts about integrity.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Citation-as-boundary method**: Use a citation between the source's information and your assessment. Put the author's finding first, attach the citation to the material it supports, and begin your observation after the citation with a clear transition such as "however" or "based on this information."
- **"According to this research" boundary marker**: Use attribution language when paraphrasing begins after your own introductory sentence. It is technically acceptable, but inspect the paragraph for remaining ambiguity about who made a comparison or inference.
- **Quoted evidence followed by cautious interpretation**: Use a short quotation or precise paraphrase with its page number, then place your own interpretation after the citation. Words such as **appears**, **seems**, and **is possible** signal that the inference belongs to the reviewer rather than the source.
- **Author-named attribution**: Use "Smith (2004) stated..." or an equivalent construction when the source's conclusion itself is important. Add the page number to the source material and then state your separate critique.

## Key Concepts
- **Source idea**: A claim, method, finding, or conclusion made by an author being reviewed.
- **Writer idea**: The reviewer's observation, comparison, evaluation, conjecture, or application.
- **Citation boundary**: Placement of the citation where the source material ends and the writer's reasoning begins.
- **Attribution**: Language that identifies whose statement or conclusion is being reported.
- **Paraphrase**: Restatement of source material in new wording that still requires citation.
- **Direct quotation**: Exact source wording enclosed in quotation marks with a citation and page number.
- **Cautious verb**: A word such as appears or seems that limits an inference to what the evidence supports.
- **Misattribution**: Making a reader think the source made the reviewer's claim, or vice versa.

## Mental Models
- **Treat every citation as a boundary marker, not a paragraph decoration.** The reader should know exactly which preceding claims it supports.
- **Use voice shifts deliberately.** Move from "Smith found" to "these data suggest" only when responsibility for the statement has moved from the source to you.
- **Think of attribution as auditability.** A reader should be able to reconstruct both the source's argument and your independent assessment without guessing.

## Anti-patterns
- Placing one citation at the end of a paragraph containing several sources' claims and the reviewer's conclusions.
- Writing "Smith says" and then adding an uncited evaluation without a clear boundary.
- Using "according to this research" while still leaving unclear who made the comparison or analogy.
- Presenting a paraphrase as common knowledge or failing to cite it because the wording is your own.
- Stating an inference as a fact when the source only supplies indirect or limited evidence.

## Worked Example
An unclear paragraph says that brownies are highly addictive, that people should avoid them under an addiction standard, and that the study has inconsistent analysis, ending with one citation. The reader cannot tell which claims belong to Smith and which belong to the reviewer. A clearer version separates the moves: state that Smith (2004) reported brownies as highly addictive and cite the relevant page; then state that, **based on that information**, the applicable standard would suggest avoidance; finally state that the study's data analysis appears inconsistent, identifying this as the reviewer's critique. The same method works with a locust study: cite the reported feeding cycle first, then cautiously infer what it may imply about damage rather than attributing the comparison to the source.

## Key Takeaways
1. Separate source evidence from your reasoning at the sentence or clause where responsibility changes.
2. Place citations where they support the source material, not merely at the end of a mixed paragraph.
3. Use author-named attribution, page numbers, quotation marks, and cautious verbs when needed.
4. Review every paragraph for possible misattribution before submission.
5. Make your evaluation explicit; hiding it inside a source summary weakens both clarity and integrity.
