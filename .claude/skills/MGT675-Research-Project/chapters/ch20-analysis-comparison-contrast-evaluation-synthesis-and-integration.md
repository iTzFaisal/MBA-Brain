# Chapter 20: Analysis, Comparison, Contrast, Evaluation, Synthesis, and Integration

## Core Idea
The six higher order thinking activities are interconnected lenses, not repetitive chores. Analyze each article, judge its value, compare and contrast the valuable articles, synthesize and integrate their relationships, and evaluate the resulting understanding against the specific situation or event the review addresses.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The parts of a literature review (Figure 20.1)**: Use the sequence as a reasoning map: article analysis informs value decisions; selected articles support comparisons and contrasts; those relationships produce syntheses and integrations; all levels are evaluated against the focal topic or situation.
- **Six higher order thinking skills**: Use **analysis**, **comparison**, **contrast**, **evaluation**, **synthesis**, and **integration** as distinct questions about the same evidence. Change the lens rather than repeating the same summary.
- **Critical Thinking Chart workflow**: Use the chart to preserve article analysis, comparison and contrast points, evaluations, syntheses, and integrations. Re-examine the chart for missing relationships and contradictions as the draft evolves.

## Key Concepts
- **Analysis**: Examination of an article's internal logic, evidence, method, interpretation, and conclusions.
- **Comparison**: Identification of similarities among frameworks, assumptions, designs, samples, results, or conclusions.
- **Contrast**: Identification of differences in those same structural or substantive dimensions.
- **Evaluation**: Assessment of literature against an outside element, usually the review's smaller and more specific topic or situation.
- **Synthesis**: Combination of compatible findings into a comprehensive picture.
- **Integration**: Reconciliation of apparently conflicting findings by considering their different circumstances.
- **Value determination**: Decision about whether an article is sufficiently sound and relevant to support the review.
- **Objectivity**: Considering multiple perspectives and using each lens for its proper purpose rather than defending a preferred result.

## Mental Models
- **Use six lenses, not six summaries.** The same article can be accurate internally, similar to one source, different from another, useful for a local program, and part of a broader synthesis.
- **Think of the process as cumulative construction.** A weak analysis contaminates later comparisons; a missing contrast makes the synthesis look more certain than it is.
- **Keep the focal situation visible.** Evaluation asks not only what the literature says, but what it means for this classroom, program, organization, population, or event.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating analysis, comparison, contrast, evaluation, synthesis, and integration as interchangeable labels.
- Including an article in a synthesis before checking its method, logic, sample, interpretation, and relevance.
- Evaluating a source against a broad interest instead of the specific situation the review must inform.
- Writing the final synthesis without revisiting contradictions in the Critical Thinking Chart.
- Assuming the process requires six isolated sections; a topic-organized review can apply several lenses within one section.

## Worked Example
Suppose two articles examine a management-training program. **Analysis** finds that Article A uses a large representative sample and a clear measure, while Article B uses a small convenience sample and makes a broad conclusion. **Comparison** shows that both examine manager confidence and use similar training content. **Contrast** shows that A measures behavior after training whereas B measures immediate self-report. **Evaluation** asks which finding applies to the focal company, whose managers resemble A's population but whose implementation period is shorter. **Synthesis** combines the evidence that the program may improve confidence and behavior when training content is reinforced. **Integration** explains why B's weak immediate effect may reflect the timing and measure rather than disprove A's longer-term result. The chain produces a conditional recommendation instead of a winner-take-all conclusion.

## Key Takeaways
1. Analyze article quality before using it in comparisons or syntheses.
2. Use comparison for reinforcement and contrast for difference; inspect both structural and substantive dimensions.
3. Evaluate the literature against the specific situation, not only against the general topic.
4. Use synthesis and integration as the culmination of prior assessment, not as unsupported opinion.
5. Revisit the Critical Thinking Chart whenever the draft reveals a contradiction or missing link.
6. Organize the writing by topic when that makes the relationships easier to follow; the reasoning lenses can still operate inside each topic.
