# Chapter 25: Expanding and Revising

## Core Idea
Expansion and revision are a conscious improvement plan built from peer critique or self-critique. Read the draft as an evaluator or editor, diagnose clarity, logic, evidence, interpretation, and conclusions, then convert the answers into specific additions, cuts, conditional statements, and rewrites.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Expansion and Revision Plan**: Use after a critique. Record what needs additional research, what should be strengthened, what should be made conditional, what should be cut, and the order in which changes will be made.
- **Evaluator/editor perspective**: Use to create distance from attachment to your own words. Answer the diagnostic questions as written rather than defending the intention behind a weak passage.
- **Expansion and Revision Questions**: Check the initial question, subquestions, assumptions, evidence limitations, interpretation, logic of conclusions, clarity of conclusions, and the link back to the question's importance or "so what?"
- **Evidence-fit test**: Use for every subquestion argument. Ask whether the article's group resembles the review's target group, whether unusual periods or events limit transfer, and whether the evidence was interpreted correctly.

## Key Concepts
- **Expansion**: Adding research, explanation, logic, or qualification needed to support a point.
- **Revision**: Rewriting or restructuring existing material to improve effectiveness.
- **Initial question**: The main question that defines the review's scope.
- **Subquestion**: A logically ordered component of the main question.
- **Assumption**: An unstated premise that can weaken an argument if it is not supported or made conditional.
- **Evidence limitation**: A difference in population, period, event, design, or data that restricts a source's use.
- **Conclusion clarity**: The reader's ability to see what was concluded, why, and how it answers the questions.
- **"So what?" link**: The explicit connection between conclusions, importance, and the next research or action step.

## Mental Models
- **Revision is diagnosis before treatment.** Do not start rewriting until the question, evidence, logic, and conclusion problem is identified.
- **Treat claims as load-bearing structures.** If a conclusion carries more scope than its evidence, add support, narrow the claim, make it conditional, or remove it.
- **Work from questions outward.** A strong revision traces each conclusion back to the initial question and forward to its practical or research consequence.

## Anti-patterns
- Expanding a weak paragraph with more words instead of stronger evidence or clearer logic.
- Treating every source as transferable regardless of its population, period, event, or method.
- Leaving assumptions unconditional when the evidence supports only a possibility or concern.
- Keeping a conclusion because it sounds important even though it does not answer the question or fit the data.
- Making revisions without recording the decision, resulting in repeated reading and inconsistent changes.

## Worked Example
A self-review finds that the main question about a training program is clear, but two subquestions are out of sequence and one is supported only by studies of a different population. The revision plan therefore reorders the subquestions, adds a source that matches the target population, and changes a categorical claim about effectiveness to a conditional claim because the available evidence is observational. The conclusion is rewritten to restate the question, summarize the supported findings, acknowledge the evidence limitation, and explain what a pilot study should test next. A source that cannot support any part of the revised argument is cut rather than padded around.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use peer or self-critique as the input to a written revision plan.
2. Check clarity, sequence, evidence, assumptions, interpretation, conclusion logic, and the "so what?" link.
3. Match every source to the population, period, event, and question for which it is being used.
4. Add evidence, narrow claims, make them conditional, or remove them when the support is inadequate.
5. Record the changes and rewrite the passages that the diagnosis identifies.
