# Chapter 11: Creating Structure for an Analysis

## Core Idea

An effective article analysis is selective, specific, and easy to follow. It gives just enough summary to identify the relevant finding, then explains strengths, weaknesses, design, sample, conclusions, and topic-specific value. Analysis can stand alone or be embedded in a comparison or contrast when that structure makes the logic clearer.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Analysis Paragraph Structure**: Separate what the article reports from what the reviewer concludes about its quality.
  - When to use: Use for each relevant article or group of articles when the review needs a clear record of evidence quality.
  - How: Briefly summarize only the article characteristics related to the question. Analyze its strengths and weaknesses, including design, sample size and composition, evidence, and conclusions. Explain what the limitations mean for the current topic and whether the point deserves further research or a bounded role.
- **Selective Minimalism**: Include only information needed to make the quality judgment understandable.
  - When to use: Use when an article contains more detail than the review's question requires.
  - How: Remove background, methods, and results that do not affect the reader's ability to judge the article's integrity or relevance. Brevity is valuable when it preserves the logic rather than hiding it.
- **Embedded Analysis**: Combine article assessment with comparison or contrast.
  - When to use: Use when the quality difference between sources changes the strength of the comparison or contrast.
  - How: State each source's conclusion, identify the relationship, and then explain why one source carries more or less weight because of its sample, method, evidence, or limitations.

## Key Concepts

- **Selective minimalism**: Deliberate omission of information that does not support the analytical judgment.
- **Analysis paragraph**: A concise unit that reports relevant source information and assesses its trustworthiness and use.
- **Sample composition**: The characteristics of participants or cases that can affect how findings are interpreted.
- **Design strength**: The degree to which a study's method fits its question and supports its conclusions.
- **Topic-specific value**: The usefulness of a source after considering both quality and relation to the review question.
- **Embedded analysis**: An article-quality judgment placed inside a comparison or contrast rather than in a separate section.
- **Evidence path**: The chain from source, through method and data, to conclusion and use.

## Mental Models

- Use a paragraph as a **logic unit**: summary identifies the claim; analysis explains how much confidence the claim deserves.
- Think of selective minimalism as **signal protection**. Detail is useful only when it changes a reader's judgment about integrity or applicability.
- Treat the sample as the **boundary of the claim**. Missing demographic or selection information is not a minor editorial issue when it affects possible bias.
- Use embedded analysis as a **weighting mechanism**: sources can point in the same direction while deserving different influence.

## Anti-patterns

- **Article dump**: A sequence of summaries forces the reader to perform the analysis the review promised to provide.
- **Critique without orientation**: Listing flaws without stating the article's relevant finding makes the evaluation hard to interpret.
- **Over-detailing**: Reproducing every method and result buries the point and increases length without increasing confidence.
- **All-or-nothing source decisions**: A source may have partial value, raise a question, or serve as a counter-perspective.
- **Separating quality from the argument**: If a weak source is used in a comparison, its weakness must affect how the comparison is presented.

## Worked Example

A fictitious study by Thompson examines parent participation in third grade students' school experiences. It interviews 30 parents in each of three middle-income elementary schools and reports that parents receiving papers about participation activities at least twice weekly were more involved in the PTA, classrooms, and homework.

The analysis should then note that demographic information is absent or undisclosed and that the process for selecting the supposedly random participants is not explained. Without knowing ethnicity, sex, employment, or selection procedures, the sample may be skewed and the conclusions may not transfer to other schools. The paragraph can still identify a potentially useful relationship and ask whether the communication technique merits further study. It should not present the result as a reliable general rule for all parents or schools.

## Key Takeaways

1. Summarize only what the reader needs to understand the analytical judgment.
2. Explain how design, sample, evidence, and conclusions affect trustworthiness.
3. Preserve partial value while clearly bounding weak evidence.
4. Embed analysis in comparisons when source quality changes the interpretation.
5. Make every paragraph answer why the article is useful and how it should be trusted.
