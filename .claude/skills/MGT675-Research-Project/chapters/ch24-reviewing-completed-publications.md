# Chapter 24: Reviewing Completed Publications

## Core Idea
Reviewing a completed publication gives you a working model of a finished literature review's structure, wording, sources, logic, and conclusions. Read it like a debater using a flow sheet: chronicle each point, the evidence supporting it, the quality of that evidence, and whether the conclusion follows.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Completed-publication comparison**: Use when you need a model for how a final review is structured and phrased. Compare its content and format with your own review, then identify techniques worth adapting and weaknesses worth avoiding.
- **Flow sheet critique**: Use to record each argument in sequence, its evidence, whether the evidence is peer reviewed, unbiased, or local when appropriate, and whether the point supports the author's side or proposed conclusion. Keep enough detail to return to the critique later.
- **Logic-and-documentation path**: Use to trace every small step from source to claim to conclusion. Judge whether the cited documents support the argument and whether the conclusion is valid for the data and methods used.
- **Distance review**: Use a day or so after completing the critique, then reread the critique for missing questions, unclear notes, or conclusions you accepted too quickly.

## Key Concepts
- **Completed publication**: A published article, dissertation proposal, grant application, or other finished work containing a literature review.
- **Flow sheet**: A detailed outline that chronicles points, evidence, quality, and argumentative direction.
- **Overview**: A high-level section that should contain enough information to support a strong logic flow.
- **Source quality**: The appropriateness, credibility, neutrality, and fit of the sources used.
- **Logic path**: The series of small evidence-to-claim connections that leads to a conclusion.
- **Documentation path**: The citation trail that lets the reader verify each claim.
- **Relative value**: The usefulness of the reviewed work for future research or change projects.
- **Plagiarism prevention**: Rewriting critique notes in your own words rather than copying striking phrases.

## Mental Models
- **Large conclusions hide small errors.** Inspect the unobvious links where a source may have been misread, a limitation omitted, or a conclusion stretched.
- **Read a finished review as a reusable artifact.** Record not only whether it is good, but how its overviews, headings, transitions, evidence, and citations produce that effect.
- **Critique twice: flow first, points second.** The outline shows whether the argument moves; detailed questions show whether individual claims can carry the weight assigned to them.

## Anti-patterns
- Reading only for the final conclusion and skipping the evidence path that supports it.
- Recording impressions in short notes that will be meaningless after reading several more publications.
- Ignoring source quality because the publication is complete or peer reviewed.
- Copying the publication's wording into your notes and risking later plagiarism.
- Allowing the critique's organizational form to drift until you no longer know which level of the argument you are reviewing.

## Worked Example
To review a completed dissertation literature review, first flow its introduction, headings, topic sentences, source claims, comparisons, and conclusions. Mark whether each source is appropriate and whether the author's conclusion is proportionate to the method and sample. A polished overview may promise a comparison of two programs, but the flow sheet may reveal that one program is supported by peer-reviewed studies while the other relies on advocacy reports. The final critique should state that the overall question is worthwhile, identify the uneven evidence and missing transition, and explain how those details weaken the comparison. After a day's distance, reread the notes and write a short narrative of strengths, weaknesses, conclusions, and relative value.

## Key Takeaways
1. Study completed reviews to learn both effective technique and avoidable weakness.
2. Use a flow sheet to preserve the order and quality of every important logic link.
3. Check citations and source quality before accepting an author's conclusion.
4. Search for small interpretation errors, not only obvious flaws in the main argument.
5. Write critiques in your own words and revisit them after a short break.
