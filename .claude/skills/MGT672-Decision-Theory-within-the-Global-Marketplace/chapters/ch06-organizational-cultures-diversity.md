# Chapter 6: Organizational Cultures and Diversity

## Core Idea
Employees bring national culture into an organization; they also learn an organizational culture made of shared assumptions, values, norms, roles, and climate. MNCs succeed when they manage the interaction rather than expecting corporate culture to erase national differences, and when they design diverse teams for tasks that benefit from multiple perspectives.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Schein's organizational culture model**
  - When to use: Diagnose what "fit" means inside an MNC or why a merger's practices clash.
  - How: Identify observed behavioral regularities, norms, dominant values, philosophy, rules, and organizational climate. At the deepest level, examine shared basic assumptions learned through external adaptation and internal integration and taught to new members as the correct way to perceive, think, and feel.
- **DOCSA (Diagnosing Organizational Culture for Strategic Application)**
  - When to use: Compare the culture of headquarters with subsidiaries or alliance partners.
  - How: Map the dimensions of activities (consistent and precise versus pioneering), relationship (job versus person), identity (corporate versus professional), communication (open versus closed), control (tight versus loose), and conduct (conventional versus pragmatic). Use the differences to coordinate expectations rather than declare one side superior.
- **Trompenaars's four organizational cultures**
  - When to use: Select management mechanisms for an organization or post-merger unit.
  - How: Use the equity-hierarchy and person-task continua. **Family culture** is hierarchical and person-oriented; **Eiffel Tower culture** is hierarchical and task-oriented; **guided missile culture** is egalitarian and task-oriented; **incubator culture** is egalitarian and person-oriented. Real organizations mix these pure types.
- **Adler's international corporation evolution**
  - When to use: Anticipate where cultural diversity will affect a firm's growth stage.
  - How: Track the shift from domestic corporation to international corporation, multinational corporation, and global corporation. International firms face strong external adaptation; multinationals emphasize internal diversity; global firms need both internal and external cross-cultural capability.
- **Multicultural team development: entry, working, action**
  - When to use: Lead a diverse or virtual team through a project.
  - How: In entry, build trust, cohesion, and respect. In working, define and analyze the task and exploit different perspectives. In action, build consensus and implement; the **nominal group technique (NGT)** lets members contribute individually before group interaction.

## Key Concepts
- **Organizational culture**: Shared values and beliefs that help members understand roles and organizational norms.
- **National-organizational interaction**: National values often persist and may become more pronounced inside an MNC rather than disappearing.
- **Family culture**: Hierarchy and person orientation with paternal leadership and loyalty.
- **Eiffel Tower culture**: Hierarchy and task orientation with formal roles and impersonal efficiency.
- **Guided missile culture**: Equality and task orientation with temporary, expertise-driven project teams.
- **Incubator culture**: Equality and person orientation with self-expression, innovation, and little formal structure.
- **Homogeneous, token, bicultural, multicultural groups**: Group types ranging from similar backgrounds to three or more cultural backgrounds.
- **Groupthink**: Social conformity and pressure to reach consensus.
- **Diversity**: A potential source of creativity and better decisions that also raises mistrust, stereotyping, and communication costs.

## Mental Models
- Diversity is a **performance multiplier only when managed**; unmanaged diversity can be worse than homogeneity.
- Match team composition to the task: multicultural teams are especially valuable for innovation and less valuable for routine work.
- Distribute power by task contribution, not ethnicity, nationality, or headquarters status.
- Build trust before demanding consensus. Make the goal explicit, surface stereotypes, establish equal professional standing, and reinforce progress with feedback.

## Anti-patterns
- **Believing corporate culture overwrites national culture**: Research found Germans becoming more German and Americans more American inside MNCs.
- **Imposing headquarters culture after an acquisition**: Tata's success with Daewoo contrasts with Walmart's failed attempt to export its German retail formula.
- **Using token diversity as inclusion**: One outsider may be isolated rather than empowered.
- **Deploying a multicultural team for routine work without a reason**: The communication and coordination cost may outweigh the benefit.
- **Letting cultural dominance decide**: Equal participation and mutual respect are prerequisites for useful disagreement.

## Worked Example
Panasonic uses a macro-micro approach. Its common global guidelines include respecting local cultures and languages, providing strong manufacturing technology, reducing expatriate dependence, allowing plants to adapt processes to local skills, creating local R&D, and encouraging competition across units. In Malaysia, the company adapted the local culture through Malaysian, Chinese, and Indian food and prayer rooms with two prayer sessions per shift. Less than 1 percent of the 30,000-person Malaysian workforce was Japanese, yet the plants later outperformed Japanese operations in quality and efficiency. The lesson is to standardize purpose and capability while letting local units build a productive culture.

## Key Takeaways
1. Organizational culture consists of visible practices and deeper assumptions; both must be diagnosed.
2. National culture persists inside MNCs, so integration requires negotiation, not cultural replacement.
3. Family, Eiffel Tower, guided missile, and incubator cultures imply different authority, motivation, change, and coordination methods.
4. Diversity produces more ideas, better customer insight, and less groupthink when trust and equal power are present.
5. Lead diverse teams differently at entry, working, and action stages; use NGT when consensus is difficult.
