# Chapter 9: Entry Strategies and Organizational Structures

## Core Idea

International expansion requires two linked choices: how to enter and own an operation, and how to organize the resulting activities. Entry mode trades off investment, risk, control, speed, local knowledge, and political acceptance. Structure should follow strategy and evolve as international sales, geographic diversity, product complexity, and coordination needs increase.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Entry Strategies and Ownership Structures:** Use **export/import** to test a market with minimum investment and risk; add overseas marketing facilities as commitment grows. Use a **wholly owned subsidiary**, including greenfield or de novo investment, when total control, clear communication, and managerial efficiency justify concentrated risk. Use **merger/acquisition** for rapid access to resources, brands, customers, or market position, but plan for cultural and transition costs. Use an **alliance** or **joint venture (JV)** when partners can share risk, knowledge, scale, technology, market access, or political legitimacy. Distinguish a nonequity venture, where one party provides a service, from an equity joint venture, where partners invest and share control. Use **licensing** to transfer a patent, trademark, or proprietary information for a fee, especially for mature products, resource-constrained firms, or markets with high investment barriers. Use **franchising** when a repeatable brand, product line, and operating method can be transferred with local adaptation.
- **Organizational Expectations of Internationalization:** Begin with an export manager or a local subsidiary when international activity supplements domestic operations. Move to an **international division structure** when overseas activity needs a dedicated senior manager. With broader integration, choose a **global product division** when product expertise and differentiation dominate; a **global area division** when regional tastes and mature, narrow product lines dominate; or a **global functional division** when tightly integrated production and centralized functional expertise dominate. Use a **mixed organizational structure** or matrix when no single orientation is sufficient, accepting multiple reporting lines and coordination costs.
- **Transnational Network Structure:** Use this when the MNC needs both global economies and local responsiveness. Build around **dispersed subunits** located for cost, knowledge, technology, or market access; **specialized operations** focused on particular products, research, or markets; and **interdependent relationships** that share resources and information. Local units make many decisions, while nodes coordinate product, functional, and geographic knowledge.
- **Organizational Characteristics:** Diagnose **formalization** (defined systems for decision making, communication, and control), **specialization** (assignment to defined tasks), and **centralization** (important decisions made at the top). Distinguish **horizontal specialization** by function from **vertical specialization** by hierarchical group responsibility. Push authority down through decentralization only when local capability and accountability support it.
- **Toyota product-development mechanisms:** Use mutual adjustment, technically skilled supervisors, integrative leadership, in-house functional training and rotation, standard milestones with simple forms, and standards maintained by the people doing the work. This preserves functional expertise while coordinating flexible product development.

## Key Concepts (5-10)

- Greater ownership and investment usually increase control and exposure to risk; cooperative modes trade some control for local knowledge and legitimacy.
- M&A speed does not remove integration work; cultural differences, unclear goals, and transition costs can destroy the expected value.
- Alliances create value through complementary resources but also require value-claiming negotiation over authority and profit.
- **Keiretsu** refers to cooperative, often vertically integrated group relationships highlighted as a nontraditional arrangement.
- Electronic network organizations use freelancers, outsourcing, and digital coordination for temporary or specialized work.
- Internal networks usually have a shared vision, clear mandates, stronger systems, and clear authority; external networks emphasize people, reciprocity, and often ambiguous authority.
- Global product divisions optimize a product worldwide; global area divisions optimize regional responsiveness; global functional divisions optimize functional control.
- Franchising transfers a tested system, while licensing transfers an industrial property right; neither eliminates the need for quality governance.

## Mental Models

- **Control-investment-local knowledge triangle:** Exporting minimizes commitment; ownership maximizes control; alliances maximize access to local knowledge and political relationships. Choose the point that fits the strategic problem.
- **Structure follows strategy:** Do not draw the organization first. Decide whether the firm competes through product, geography, function, or network integration, then design authority and information flows.
- **Network, not pyramid:** In a transnational network, value may originate in any subsidiary. Headquarters coordinates rather than monopolizes expertise.
- **Cooperation needs boundaries:** A JV works only when partners agree on scope, responsibilities, authority, performance targets, knowledge protection, and exit conditions.

## Anti-patterns

- Choosing a wholly owned subsidiary solely because control feels safer, without pricing political, capital, and concentration risk.
- Buying a foreign company and assuming ownership automatically produces integration.
- Forming a JV with a prestigious partner without testing complementary resources, objectives, trust, and incentives.
- Adding matrix layers until managers have too many bosses and no clear accountability.
- Preserving an international division after the business needs global product, area, or network coordination.

## Worked Example

Airbnb shows how a digital platform can bypass asset-heavy entry modes. In Cuba it connected travelers with local casa particular hosts, used local photographers to improve listings, and worked with collection agencies to navigate payments. In China it struggled against Tujia and Xiaozhu, so it targeted outbound Chinese travelers as guests and differentiated through a global, higher-quality experience. The model resembles a transnational electronic network: Airbnb supplies technology and brand, while dispersed hosts and local partners supply assets, knowledge, and service.

## Key Takeaways

1. Entry mode is a control-risk-knowledge choice, not a standard sequence that every MNC must follow.
2. Cooperative entry is valuable when local knowledge, regulation, and legitimacy matter, but governance must define authority and exit.
3. Organizational design evolves from export arrangements to international divisions and then to global or network structures.
4. Formalization, specialization, and centralization describe how a structure actually operates, not merely how it appears on an organization chart.
5. A lean network can outperform a complicated matrix when information and accountability remain clear.
