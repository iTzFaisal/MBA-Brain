# Chapter 12: Motivation Across Cultures

## Core Idea

Motivation is a psychological process in which unsatisfied needs create drives aimed at goals or incentives. The process is broadly universal, but the content of needs, the meaning of fairness, the value of work, and the form of a useful reward vary by culture, occupation, generation, and situation. Use motivation theories as diagnostic lenses, then validate the package with local employees.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Basic Motivation Process:** Diagnose the sequence of **needs -> drives -> goal attainment**. Separate **intrinsic** motivation, fulfillment from the activity or helping others, from **extrinsic** motivation, competition, compensation, or incentives. Use the sequence to ask what is unsatisfied and what outcome has high valence, not merely what reward is available.
- **Content Theories and Process Theories:** Content theories explain what arouses or energizes behavior; process theories explain how behavior is initiated, redirected, and halted. The source emphasizes three content theories and three process theories for international HR.
- **Hierarchy-of-Needs Theory:** Maslow's hierarchy runs from **physiological**, **safety**, **social**, **esteem**, to **self-actualization** needs. Use it to map a workforce's likely needs, remembering the assumptions that lower needs generally precede higher needs, a satisfied need loses motivational force, and higher needs have more satisfaction routes. Nevis's collectivistic Chinese hierarchy instead places **belonging**, **physiological**, **safety**, and **self-actualization in the service of society** in ascending order.
- **Two-Factor Theory of Motivation:** Herzberg separates **hygiene factors** (salary, technical supervision, policies and administration, interpersonal relations, working conditions) from **motivators** (achievement, recognition, responsibility, advancement, and the work itself). Fix hygiene deficiencies to prevent dissatisfaction; add motivators to create satisfaction.
- **Achievement Motivation Theory:** McClelland's high achiever seeks personal responsibility, moderate risk, concrete feedback, and challenging success, but may be a poor people manager. Achievement is learned and can be developed. Societies with weak uncertainty avoidance and moderate-to-high masculinity tend to support achievement-oriented behavior more strongly.
- **Process theories:** **Equity theory** compares inputs, outcomes, and relevant others; perceived inequity motivates restoration, but individualistic cultures emphasize effort and pay while collectivistic cultures may emphasize loyalty, support, status, and group harmony. **Goal-setting theory** emphasizes participation, difficulty, specificity, and objective, timely feedback; participation may be routed through unions or groups rather than individuals. **Expectancy theory** treats motivation as the combination of belief that effort leads to performance, performance leads to outcomes, and outcomes are valuable; it works best where people perceive control over outcomes.
- **Job Design, Work Centrality, and Rewards:** Match job content, methods, and interdependence to culture. **Sociotechnical designs** blend people and technology through autonomous teams and appropriate control. Work centrality measures the importance of work relative to family, church, and leisure. Match individual or group rewards to local norms and combine financial with recognition, autonomy, growth, and work-life support.

## Key Concepts (5-10)

- Job categories can predict motivational needs as strongly as countries: professionals often value challenge, autonomy, training, and self-actualization; unskilled workers often prioritize earnings, security, benefits, and conditions.
- Job-content factors are internally mediated; job-context factors are controlled by the organization.
- Individual rewards stress equity; group rewards stress equality and solidarity.
- High work centrality can support commitment but also produce **karoshi**, overwork or job burnout.
- Quality of work life reflects cultural expectations about pace, control, hierarchy, participation, and risk.
- Meaningful work, family relationships, development, and social purpose can matter as much as pay.
- Motivation practices may transfer, as some U.S. rewards worked in Russia, but participation and reward form still require cultural testing.

## Mental Models

- **Universal engine, different fuel:** Needs create effort everywhere, but the valued goal and acceptable route differ.
- **No dissatisfaction is not satisfaction:** Hygiene prevents breakdown; motivators create engagement.
- **Equity is culturally framed:** Ask “fair compared with whom?” before designing a pay system.
- **Person-job-culture fit:** A job becomes motivating when task design, technology, autonomy, work centrality, and reward norms fit the local workforce.

## Anti-patterns

- Assuming Maslow's order, U.S.-style participation, or individual merit pay applies unchanged everywhere.
- Trying to solve disengagement with salary while ignoring recognition, trust, career development, family, or purpose.
- Giving individual awards in a collectivistic culture where public distinction causes embarrassment or conflict.
- Designing autonomy-heavy work for a workforce that expects structure, or rigid work for a workforce that expects participation.
- Treating long hours as commitment and ignoring burnout, family needs, and sustainable performance.

## Worked Example

McDonald's Latin American operations used “freedom within a framework” but faced high manager and crew turnover. The company added country representation to the regional HR board, created a continuous-improvement plan, awarded points for operational surveys and skill development, recognized top performers, offered manager education, and shifted managers toward direct attention to customers and employees. Sales rose and turnover fell. The lesson is not to copy every reward globally; it is to combine global standards with locally meaningful recognition, development, and participation.

## Key Takeaways

1. Start with the need, drive, and valued outcome rather than the reward.
2. Use content theories to diagnose what matters and process theories to design how effort becomes performance.
3. Stabilize hygiene factors, then add meaningful work, recognition, responsibility, growth, and feedback.
4. Design rewards and participation around cultural and occupational comparisons.
5. Motivation is a continuing fit problem; measure satisfaction and revise the package.
