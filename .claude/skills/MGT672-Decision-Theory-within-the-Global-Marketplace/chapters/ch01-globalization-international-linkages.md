# Chapter 1: Globalization and International Linkages

## Core Idea
Globalization is the social, political, economic, cultural, and technological integration of countries; internationalization is the process of a business crossing national and cultural borders. Managers must treat global integration as an opportunity constrained by regional institutions, political backlash, demographics, economic systems, and local conditions.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Globalization vs. internationalization**
  - When to use: Distinguish a change in the world environment from a firm's own cross-border activity.
  - How: Track trade, capital flows, migration, communications, and technology for globalization; track foreign sales, operations, ownership, and management nationality for internationalization.
- **Global and regional integration**
  - When to use: Evaluate a market that sits inside a trade or investment bloc.
  - How: Map tariff and nontariff barriers, investment rules, intellectual-property protection, labor and environmental commitments, and the degree of regional coordination. The WTO is the global rule-setting organization; the EU, USMCA, Mercosur, ASEAN, and CPTPP illustrate regional or bilateral integration.
- **Global economic systems**
  - When to use: Predict how ownership, prices, resource allocation, and managerial discretion will work in a country.
  - How: In a **market economy**, private enterprise owns property and responds to demand while the state supports competition. In a **command economy**, the state controls ownership, prices, and supply. In a **mixed economy**, private markets operate alongside government planning, regulation, and state ownership of critical sectors.
- **Emerging-market groupings: BRICS, E7, N-11, and MIST**
  - When to use: Screen future growth rather than relying only on today's largest established economies.
  - How: Compare population, GDP, GDP per capita, growth, reform, FDI, and resource dependence. Use market exchange rates for current conversion and purchasing power parity (PPP) when comparing what output can buy locally.

## Key Concepts
- **International management**: Applying management concepts in a multinational environment while adapting to economic, political, and cultural contexts.
- **MNC**: A firm with operations in more than one country, international sales, and a mix of nationalities among managers and owners.
- **Offshoring**: Performing activities at offshore locations rather than in the firm's country of origin.
- **Outsourcing**: Contracting activities to external organizations that were previously performed inside the firm.
- **Foreign direct investment (FDI)**: Investment in property, plant, or equipment in another country.
- **Emerging market**: A developing economy showing sustained economic reform and growth.
- **Demographic aging**: Longer lives and lower fertility reduce the working-age population and shift demand toward health care and services.
- **Regionalization**: The reality that North America, Europe, Asia, and developing regions remain distinct economic groupings even as global linkages grow.

## Mental Models
- Use a **global-regional-local lens**: global flows create possibilities, regional agreements set access conditions, and local institutions determine execution.
- Treat demographics as both a labor-supply forecast and a demand forecast.
- Use economic-system analysis to predict incentives: demand and competition dominate market economies; political priorities dominate command systems; mixed systems require both market and government stakeholder management.
- Separate growth potential from investability. A fast-growing market can still carry infrastructure, currency, political, or institutional risk.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming globalization distributes benefits evenly**: Offshoring and trade can lower prices and create jobs while also dislocating workers and depressing wages in exposed communities.
- **Treating the world as one uniform market**: Regional blocs, national policies, and cultural differences still shape products, costs, and operations.
- **Confusing offshoring with outsourcing**: A firm can move work abroad and keep it internal, outsource domestically, or combine both practices.
- **Ignoring emerging-market downside risk**: Commodity dependence, weak infrastructure, demographic imbalance, or protectionism can reverse an attractive growth story.

## Worked Example
Apple's U.S.-China supply chain shows why globalization creates both efficiency and exposure. The iPhone was designed in California, assembled in China, and supported by suppliers in both countries. U.S. tariffs on Chinese goods could raise the cost of the finished phone; Chinese retaliation could restrict Apple sales, imported components, or production facilities. Apple therefore had to manage public policy as well as engineering and procurement. A sound response is to map every cross-border dependency, model tariff and disruption scenarios, maintain government relationships in both markets, and assess the time and cost of relocating suppliers or assembly before a crisis forces the decision.

## Key Takeaways
1. Globalization integrates countries; internationalization describes a firm's cross-border activity.
2. Integration is often regional rather than fully global, so market access depends on the relevant trade bloc.
3. Economic systems change who owns resources, who sets prices, and how much managerial discretion exists.
4. BRICS, E7, N-11, and MIST are signals of shifting economic power, not guarantees of investment success.
5. Global strategy must account for aging workforces, changing demand, FDI exposure, and political backlash.
