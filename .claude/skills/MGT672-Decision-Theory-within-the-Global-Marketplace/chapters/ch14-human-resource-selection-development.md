# Chapter 14: Human Resource Selection and Development Across Cultures

## Core Idea

International HR is a strategic allocation of human capital, not simply overseas hiring. MNCs must choose among global talent sources, assess technical and intercultural fit, support the employee's family, compensate fairly, develop local and global capability, and plan both arrival and return. The expensive failure is often not a weak technical performer; it is a technically strong person unable to adjust, lead, or transfer learning.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Sources of Human Resources:** Use **home-country nationals/expatriates** to launch operations, transfer technical expertise, coordinate control, or develop future leaders. Use **host-country nationals** for local language, culture, legitimacy, and lower cost. Use **third-country nationals (TCNs)** for regional knowledge, language, specialized expertise, or a neutral global image. Use **inpatriates**, host- or third-country nationals assigned to headquarters, to build diversity and a transnational core competency. Use subcontracting and offshore outsourcing for flexibility, scarce skills, and cost, while managing quality, political, and coordination risks.
- **International Selection Criteria and Adjustment Process:** Assess adaptability to cultural change, physical and emotional health, age, experience, education, language, motivation, leadership, and spouse/dependent support. Separate **anticipatory adjustment** before departure (selection, prior experience, cross-cultural training) from **in-country adjustment** involving outlook, host-national interaction, role clarity and authority, organizational culture, nonwork/family conditions, and socialization. Support adjustment through host-country network ties: motivation to seek support, selection of actors, actors' willingness to help, use of support, and addition of useful actors to the network.
- **Expatriate Compensation Package:** Build around five elements: **base salary, benefits, allowances, incentives, and taxes**. Use the **balance-sheet approach** to make the expat whole; **localization** to pay on a host-country basis; the **lump-sum method** to give a fixed amount for personal choices; the **cafeteria approach** to offer benefit options; and a **regional system** to standardize packages within a geographic region. Choose the mix that is fair, cost-effective, legally compliant, and appropriate to family and hardship conditions.
- **Ethnocentric, Polycentric, Regiocentric, and Geocentric MNCs:** An **ethnocentric MNC** puts headquarters nationals in key positions and usually trains at headquarters. A **polycentric MNC** gives local nationals authority to manage and develop local people. A **regiocentric MNC** coordinates managers and operations within a region. A **geocentric MNC** integrates the world and assigns based on qualifications, treating subsidiary and headquarters managers as peers. Each orientation creates different selection and training demands.
- **Standardized vs. Tailor-Made Training:** Standardize portable technical tools; tailor cultural, language, leadership, and work-practice content to the host country and participants. Use predeparture and onsite training, family programs, mentoring, field experience, and reinforcement. A teaching organization expects leaders to pass knowledge to others, not merely attend courses.
- **Cultural Assimilator:** Use this programmed, culture-pair-specific learning technique to present short intercultural incidents and alternative interpretations. A critical incident involves an expatriate and host national, is puzzling or likely to be misinterpreted, can be understood with cultural knowledge, and is relevant to the task. Validate scenarios with target-culture respondents; programs commonly narrow 150-200 incidents to 75-100 validated incidents.
- **Repatriation and Transition Strategies:** Use **repatriation agreements**, sponsor relationships, headquarters contact, maintained housing, reentry counseling, family support, debriefing, and a defined postassignment role. Treat the return as a second adjustment, not the end of the assignment.

## Key Concepts (5-10)

- Expatriate assignments can cost up to US$1 million over three years; failure risk makes selection and training investments material.
- Adaptability includes openness, sociability, problem solving across frames, cultural sensitivity, and flexibility under incomplete information.
- Family dissatisfaction, spouse careers, children's education, and lost benefits are major assignment refusal or early-return risks.
- Expatriate packages must address relocation, housing, education, hardship, home visits, benefits, and tax equalization.
- Offshore outsourcing provides access to skills as well as low wages, but wage differences do not translate directly into savings and quality may require repatriation.
- Training must overcome **ethnocentrism**, the belief that one's own way is superior.
- Positive organizational behavior develops measurable strengths and states such as self-efficacy, hope, optimism, resilience, and psychological capital.
- Repatriates often perceive a demotion, lose autonomy, and find that the home office undervalues their international knowledge.

## Mental Models

- **The assignment is a household decision:** Select and support the employee, spouse, dependents, and the social system that makes performance possible.
- **Global HR is a lifecycle:** Select, prepare, relocate, adjust, perform, learn, repatriate, and redeploy; failure at any transition destroys accumulated value.
- **Make whole, then motivate:** Protect purchasing power and fairness first; use incentives and development to make mobility attractive.
- **Training needs a host feedback loop:** Teach, observe local behavior, reinforce use, and revise content with host-country evidence.

## Anti-patterns

- Promoting the best technical expert without testing adaptability, family support, language, or leadership.
- Assuming English and a short cultural briefing are sufficient preparation.
- Treating expatriate compensation as a universal home-country formula or ignoring local morale effects.
- Sending only headquarters nationals and excluding host-country or inpatriate talent from the global pipeline.
- Ending HR responsibility when the expat returns, leaving no role, sponsor, knowledge-transfer process, or family support.
- Building cultural training from home-country stereotypes rather than validated critical incidents.

## Worked Example

General Electric Medical Systems Group prepared families for assignments in France, Japan, and Singapore by pairing the incoming family with a recently returned family. Teenagers could ask practical questions about schools, friendship, language, and daily life. GEMS also reviewed curricula, tutors, home- and host-country requirements, and created an education plan before departure; it provided comparable information before return. The approach treats family adjustment as an operational variable and turns prior expatriate experience into a reusable support network.

## Key Takeaways

1. Use a portfolio of home-, host-, third-country, inpatriate, and outsourced talent rather than assuming expatriates are the default.
2. Select for technical competence plus adaptability, health, language, motivation, leadership, and family readiness.
3. Design compensation around five elements and choose the method that balances fairness, cost, law, and local expectations.
4. Tailor training to the MNC orientation, host culture, learning style, job, and family; reinforce it after arrival.
5. Repatriation is a retention and knowledge-transfer problem that requires a planned career path.
