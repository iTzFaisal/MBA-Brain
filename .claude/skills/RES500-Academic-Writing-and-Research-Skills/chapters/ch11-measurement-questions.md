# Chapter 11: Measurement Questions

## Core Idea
Measurement questions are the bridge between investigative questions and analyzable observations. Design them backward from a preliminary analysis plan, select a response strategy that fits the needed data level and participant, and then test coverage, wording, frame of reference, response alternatives, and order. A question is not good merely because it sounds natural: it must be purposeful, answerable, precise, unbiased, and practical to code.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Preliminary Analysis Plan and Dummy Tables**: Use before selecting or writing a scale. Specify summaries, cross-tabulations, hypothesis tests, association measures, and displays; then draw dummy tables that show which variables will be compared. The required nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio level in those tables guides scale selection. If the plan requires unnecessary questions or creates excessive analysis cost, reduce the plan and measure only what is needed.
- **Four Response Types: Rating, Ranking, Categorization, and Sorting**:
  - **Rating** asks a participant to score one object or property without directly comparing it with another; it includes category, checklist, Likert, semantic differential, numerical, Stapel, constant-sum, and graphic forms.
  - **Ranking** requires comparisons and an order among objects or properties; paired comparison, forced ranking, and comparative scales are the main forms.
  - **Categorization** places a person or indicant into a group, such as gender, age range, or user type.
  - **Sorting** asks the participant to arrange objects, statements, images, or cards along a criterion, as in a Q-sort.
  Choose among them according to the objective: measure characteristics of participants, or use participants as judges of presented objects or indicants.
- **Scale Construction Decisions**: Before writing a rating question, decide whether the scale is unidimensional or multidimensional, balanced or unbalanced, forced-choice or unforced-choice, and how many points it needs. More points can increase reliability, validity, and sensitivity, but the number should match the complexity and involvement of the attitude object. A simple low-involvement product may need three points; complex, consequential topics may justify five to eleven. An unbalanced scale is defensible only when prior knowledge says one side will rarely be used or when precision is needed to correct leniency or strictness.
- **Attitude Scaling**: Treat an attitude as a learned, relatively stable favorable or unfavorable predisposition, then measure its indicants rather than assuming it is behavior. Its components are **cognitive** beliefs and evaluations, **affective** feelings and emotions, and **conative** or behavioral intentions. Specific, strong, accessible, personally relevant attitudes, direct experience, multiple measures across time, and supportive reference groups improve the attitude-behavior link; attitudes still do not guarantee actual behavior.
- **Likert Item Analysis**: Use this procedure to build a discriminating summated rating scale. Gather relevant favorable and unfavorable statements; obtain agreement ratings from participant stand-ins; reverse-score negative wording so higher values always mean a more favorable attitude; sum each person's responses; retain high and low groups, usually the top and bottom 10-25 percent, and discard the middle group; compare item means with a t value; rank items by discrimination; and select roughly 20-25 items with significant differences. A t value of 1.75 or greater is a suggested cutoff when each extreme group has at least 25 people. Summing items is convenient and often reliable, but the same total can hide different response profiles.
- **Semantic Differential (SD) Scale**: Use bipolar adjectives or phrases to profile the connotative meaning of an object. The classic semantic space has three dimensions: **evaluation**, **potency**, and **activity**. Select a variable, identify candidate nouns or stimuli, choose stable linear bipolar pairs, use a commonly seven-point scoring system, reverse-score about half the pairs to reduce halo, and mix the dimensions rather than grouping all pairs of one type together. Average scores by dimension to compare profiles.
- **Specialized Rating and Ranking Scales**: Use a **constant-sum scale** to allocate 100 points among attributes and reveal proportions; a **graphic rating scale** to capture fine differences along a continuum; a **visual graphic rating scale** when icons or images communicate better than words; a **Stapel scale** when suitable bipolar adjectives are unavailable, with usually +5 to -5 ratings; and **numerical** or **multiple rating list** scales for anchored, repeatable judgments. Simple category, multiple-choice single-response, and checklist questions produce nominal data; Likert and semantic differential questions are generally treated as interval; numerical, multiple-rating, and Stapel data may be ordinal or interval; constant-sum data are ratio; graphic data may be ordinal, interval, or ratio depending on defensible linearity. Use **paired comparison** for unambiguous choices between two stimuli, **forced ranking** for a faster relative order, and a **comparative scale** when a familiar standard is available. Ranking data are ordinal unless stronger interval assumptions are supported. Paired comparisons require `n(n-1)/2` judgments for `n` stimuli and can become tiring.
- **Q-Sort and Scalogram Analysis**: Use a **Q-sort** to place statements, words, or images into 7-11 piles along a continuum, usually with 60-120 cards; structured sorts fix the number per pile, while unstructured sorts fix only the piles. Use **scalogram analysis**, a cumulative-scale procedure, when endorsement of an extreme item should imply endorsement of all less-extreme items and the goal is to test unidimensionality.
- **Question-Quality Audit**: For each question, check coverage, wording, frame of reference, personalization or projection, and the structure, number, and order of alternatives. Pretest individual questions with participant surrogates and research colleagues to expose unanswerable items, inappropriate scales, leading or double-barreled wording, unsupported assumptions, and missing definitions.

## Key Concepts
- **Measurement question**: The specific prompt and response strategy used to collect an observation for a variable.
- **Structured question**: A closed question with supplied response alternatives; it is easier to code and analyze but can omit unanticipated answers.
- **Unstructured question**: An open or free-response question that lets the participant supply words, ideas, dates, or numbers; it is useful for exploration but costs more to administer and interpret.
- **Dummy table**: A mock-up cross-tabulation used to determine the relationships and data levels the study must support.
- **Balanced rating scale**: Equal favorable and unfavorable choices around a midpoint, with or without an indifference option.
- **Forced-choice rating scale**: Requires selection from the offered alternatives; it can reveal a direction when most participants have an opinion but biases undecided participants.
- **Unforced-choice rating scale**: Allows no opinion, don't know, or neutral; it protects participants without a real attitude but can conceal meaningful differences when "neutral" is not genuine.
- **Response errors**: Central tendency, leniency, strictness, and halo affect ratings; acquiescence affects agreement statements; primacy tends to affect visual surveys and recency oral surveys.
- **Disguised or projective question**: Conceals the objective or asks how others would respond to reduce reluctance or social desirability; sentence completion, cartoon or balloon tests, and word association are examples, but interpretation can be difficult. Use the technique only when the information need justifies it, and preserve voluntary participation, confidentiality, and respectful treatment of sensitive topics.
- **Behavior time frame and memory decay**: Behavior questions need a period matched to the behavior cycle and frequency; distant or insignificant events are poorly recalled and should allow "don't know" or "don't remember" where appropriate.
- **Adequate alternatives**: Response options should cover about 90 percent of expected answers, with an "other" option when needed, while remaining exhaustive, mutually appropriate, and on one conceptual dimension.

## Mental Models
- **Start with the table, not the wording**: Imagine the cross-tabulation or test the manager needs, determine the data level, and only then choose the question format.
- **Treat wording as measurement equipment**: Every word establishes a frame, time period, assumed knowledge, and possible bias. A shared vocabulary and operational definition are part of the instrument, not cosmetic editing.
- **Use an answerability gate**: Before asking for an answer, confirm that the participant has the knowledge, memory, thought, communication ability, willingness, and time to provide it. A screen or filter is better than forcing a guess.
- **Match scale resolution to the object**: Give participants enough points to express meaningful differences, but do not add artificial precision to a low-involvement or poorly considered attitude.

## Anti-patterns
- **Collecting interesting rather than decision-useful data**: Every question should serve an investigative question or be necessary to interpret another result; extra questions increase cost and fatigue.
- **Using double-barreled questions**: One answer cannot reveal whether the participant judged sales, profits, comfort, cleanliness, or some other separate component; split the question.
- **Using leading wording or unsupported assumptions**: Suggestive adjectives, prestige names, and assumptions that everyone has an experience or opinion distort both response and willingness to continue.
- **Omitting a frame or behavior time frame**: "Evaluate the store," "last week," "regularly," or "ever" can mean different things to different participants and invite memory error.
- **Assuming knowledge and accepting any answer**: Participants may cooperate by guessing. Use screen questions, definitions, skip directions, or a don't-know alternative rather than manufacture data.
- **Forcing an opinion that does not exist**: A forced choice can create apparent attitudes and shift means toward the midpoint when many respondents are undecided.
- **Borrowing questions without their context**: A source question may have unreported reliability or validity, depend on adjacent items, use outdated language, or no longer fit the new sample; adapt and pretest it.
- **Overloading ranking or sorting tasks**: Too many paired comparisons produce fatigue and ill-considered answers; sample pairs or use standards when the full set is burdensome.
- **Assuming a summated score has one meaning**: Different combinations of favorable and unfavorable item responses can produce the same Likert total.

## Worked Example
For five alternative package designs, 200 heavy users complete paired comparisons. Each cell records how many respondents preferred the column design to the row design. Summing all wins gives A = 378, B = 666, C = 510, D = 178, and E = 268, producing the rank order B, C, A, E, D. If preferences are mostly transitive, the totals provide a useful overall order; if A is preferred to B and B to C but C is repeatedly preferred to A, the order is less trustworthy. The task yields ordinal data, not known preference distances. It also illustrates the burden rule: four stimuli require six pair judgments, while 15 require 105. A researcher can show each participant an equal sample of pairs or compare many stimuli to a smaller set of standards, preserving coverage while reducing fatigue.

## Key Takeaways
1. Build the preliminary analysis plan and dummy tables before choosing a measurement scale.
2. Select rating, ranking, categorization, or sorting according to the research objective and participant capability.
3. Balance the scale, force choices, and choose the number of points only when the topic and expected respondent knowledge justify those decisions.
4. Treat attitudes as cognitive, affective, and conative indicators that predict intentions better than guaranteed behavior.
5. Use Likert item analysis and semantic-differential construction rules when a custom attitude scale needs evidence of discrimination and coverage.
6. Make every question precise about scope, vocabulary, frame, time, alternatives, and willingness to answer.
7. Pretest both custom and borrowed questions; a familiar source does not transfer validity automatically.
