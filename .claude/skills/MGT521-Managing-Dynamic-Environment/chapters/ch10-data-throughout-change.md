# Chapter 10: Get and Use Data Throughout the Change Process

## Core Idea

Measurement and control are not an end-of-project audit. They are navigation systems that should begin when the need for change is framed and continue through implementation, institutionalization, closure, and learning. Measures direct attention and behavior, so the right measures clarify expectations, expose risks, support accountability, enable midcourse correction, and show whether new practices have become part of normal work. The wrong measures produce cynicism, gaming, distorted data, and behavior that optimizes a local target while damaging the change's real purpose.

## Frameworks Introduced

### Measurement Throughout the Change Path

**When to use:** From the first assessment of the need for change through the evaluation of outcomes and preparation for future change.

**How:** Match the information to the question being answered at each phase.

**Design and early stages:** Use mission, vision, and values as belief-system guides; environmental scanning and SWOT-style assessment as interactive controls; and boundary information to identify unacceptable risks or actions. Combine primary and secondary research, exploratory conversations, internal assessments, readiness information, and early experiments. Use the evidence to compare alternatives, define milestones and steering measures, and make an explicit initial go/no-go decision. Build in reality checks that surface information leaders may not want to hear.

**Middle stages:** Keep the direction, boundaries, and values visible, but rely increasingly on diagnostic and steering data. Track budgets and variances, schedules, relevant communication or social-media signals, pilot results, skill acquisition, understanding, acceptance, and service or process failures. Distinguish leading indicators, which show whether the change is moving in the intended direction, from lagging indicators, which may take longer to appear. Use milestones as road markers, celebrate progress, and make conscious go/no-go, timeline, measure, or design adjustments when evidence shows that the next stage is not yet appropriate.

**Toward the end:** Shift from implementation signals to concrete outcome measures. Compare actual accomplishments and impact with the original expectations, assess whether systems, processes, and behaviors are working appropriately, determine what has been internalized and institutionalized, and capture observations for future initiatives. The measures should close the current change while creating organizational learning.

### Simon's Four Management Control Systems

**When to use:** Use the four levers together to keep strategy, freedom, risk, values, and performance aligned. Their relative importance changes as a change project moves from framing to execution and institutionalization.

**How:**

- **Interactive controls:** Sense environmental changes that matter to strategy, such as competitor or market intelligence. Use them most heavily when defining the need and vision, continue scanning during implementation, and use feedback at the end to judge the initiative against the environment.
- **Boundary systems:** Define the limits of authority and action, including prohibited behavior, sanctions, spending limits, and other go/no-go conditions. Set and test boundaries during design, reassess or reestablish them in the middle, and reevaluate them at the close.
- **Belief systems:** Express the values, mission, and purpose that give the change meaning. Test congruence at the start, frame the change in terms of core values, reaffirm or transparently modify those values during implementation, and reassess them in light of learning.
- **Diagnostic and steering controls:** Track critical performance variables, milestones, and corrective actions. Develop them early, use them for ongoing progress monitoring and learning, and use final measures to confirm completion and the proper operation of the new system.

### Six Principles for Selecting and Deploying Measures

**When to use:** Apply these tests before adopting a metric, when revising a measure, and whenever a measure begins producing unexpected behavior.

**How:**

1. **Focus on key factors.** Select the few factors that move the change toward its intended outcomes rather than measuring everything. Use analysis to expose both the harmful effects of the current pattern and the benefits of the desired one. Check for confirmation, recency, loss-aversion, herding, and outcome bias.
2. **Use measures that lead to challenging but achievable goals.** Show attainable steps toward the larger objective and favor measures people can influence. Small visible wins can connect daily action to a difficult long-term goal.
3. **Use measures and controls perceived as fair and appropriate.** Involve the people responsible for the results in developing and legitimizing the measures. Explain the purpose, invite feedback, and do not punish reasonable actions taken in good faith. Excessive rewards or severe sanctions can encourage game playing and falsification and can displace autonomy, mastery, and purpose.
4. **Avoid sending mixed signals.** Align what leaders say, measure, reward, and tolerate. Explicitly resolve trade-offs, such as quality versus delivery speed, rather than letting employees infer that the legacy target still wins.
5. **Ensure accurate data.** People provide timely, honest information when they trust the system and do not expect the data to be used against them. Keep pressure and consequences reasonable so that targets do not induce people to shade the facts.
6. **Match measurement precision to the ability to measure.** Precise, explicit measures fit a relatively clear, structured, predictable change with a short completion horizon. Approximate measures centered on vision, milestones, and learning-as-you-go fit turbulent or ambiguous work with a longer horizon. Balance the speed, accuracy, and cost of information; simple and understandable is better than precisely wrong.

### Strategy Maps

**When to use:** Use a strategy map when the change is complex and people need to see the desired end state, the action paths leading to it, and the assumptions connecting those actions.

**How:** Translate the change vision and strategy into a visible cause-and-effect chain. In a for-profit organization, start with the desired **financial perspective**, then identify the customer value proposition and any internal efficiencies needed to produce it. Specify the **internal business process** changes that create those customer and efficiency outcomes. Finally, define the **learning and growth** capabilities needed to make the processes work: human, information, and organizational capital, including culture, leadership, alignment, and teamwork. For a nonprofit, the customer or stakeholder perspective may sit at the top because it expresses the organization's purpose. Give every initiative objectives, measures, timelines, resource requirements, and an action plan. Use the map to communicate the logic, test assumptions, find missing objectives or measures, and align reporting and effort.

### Balanced Scorecard

**When to use:** Use a balanced scorecard when a change needs a manageable set of integrated measures rather than a single success indicator.

**How:** State the change mission, vision, and strategy and check their consistency with the organization's direction. Populate four linked perspectives: financial results; customer or stakeholder results; internal business processes; and learning and growth. For each perspective, state what success would look like, choose objectives and measures, and identify leading and lagging indicators. Internal customers, such as employees in another department, may be the relevant customers for an internal change. Seek feedback from trusted colleagues to test whether the scorecard makes the change, its progress signals, and the required actions understandable. Use multiple perspectives to make causal assumptions visible and reduce the risk of being misled by one metric.

### Risk Exposure Calculator

**When to use:** Use this tool during planning and deployment to assess internal risk exposure for a proposed change or for maintaining the status quo.

**How:** Rate nine cumulative risk drivers, grouped into three categories. **Change pressure** includes pressure to produce, ambiguity, and inexperience with change. **Change culture** includes rewards or norms that push risk-taking, executives' resistance to bad news, and internal competition. **Information management** includes a complex and fast-changing situation, gaps in diagnostic measures, and decentralized change decisions. The chapter's exercise rates each driver on a 1-to-5 scale and totals the factors: 9-20 is a safety zone, 21-34 a cautionary zone, and 35-45 a danger zone. Treat the result as a diagnostic, not a universal optimum: very low risk can also weaken adaptability, while acceptable risk depends on the environment, consequences, capabilities, and available safeguards. Reduce ambiguity with a clear vision and milestones, add experienced managers where needed, improve diagnostic data, and monitor the score as circumstances change.

### DICE Model

**When to use:** Use DICE for a process-oriented estimate of whether a change project has the management conditions needed to succeed.

**How:** Score four factors from 1, favorable, to 4, unfavorable. **Duration** is the time between formal reviews: less than two months scores 1, two to four months 2, four to eight months 3, and more than eight months 4. **Integrity of performance** assesses the leader's skill and credibility plus the change team's skill, motivation, focus, and available time; a capable, respected, motivated team with sufficient assignment scores 1, while broad weakness scores 4. **Commitment** has two scores: senior management commitment is 1 when words and actions consistently support the change, 2-3 when leadership is neutral, and 4 when it is unsupportive; local commitment is 1 when affected employees are very supportive, 2 when willing but not eager, and 3-4 when reluctant. **Effort** scores incremental employee effort below 10% as 1, 10-20% as 2, 20-40% as 3, and above 40% as 4.

Calculate the total as:

`DICE = Duration + (2 x Integrity) + (2 x Senior Management Commitment) + Local Commitment + Effort`

The chapter reports 7-14 as a high-likelihood-of-success range, 15-17 as a worry zone, and 17+ as extremely risky, with scores above 19 very unlikely to succeed. Because the source's bands overlap at 17, treat 17 as an escalation point. Lower risk by shortening review intervals, staffing the project with credible and capable people, increasing senior and local commitment, and allocating sufficient time for the work.

## Key Concepts

- **Measurement and control system:** A set of measures and processes used to focus attention, monitor conditions, guide decisions, and manage organizational action.
- **Legitimacy of a measure:** The extent to which those using or affected by a measure see it as relevant, fair, understandable, and appropriately developed.
- **Leading indicator:** An earlier signal that the change is producing conditions likely to generate a later result, such as response time preceding customer satisfaction.
- **Lagging indicator:** A result that appears after the actions or conditions that produced it, such as repeat sales or profitability following improved service.
- **First-order effect:** An immediate or direct result of an intervention, such as customer satisfaction with a service.
- **Second-order or lag effect:** A later consequence of the direct result, such as loyalty, repeat purchases, and profitability.
- **Milestone or road marker:** A visible intermediate achievement used to assess progress, reinforce effort, and decide whether to proceed or adjust.
- **Internalization and institutionalization:** The movement from implementing a change to having its practices, systems, and behaviors accepted and operating as part of normal organizational life.
- **Cause-effect alignment:** The explicit connection between capabilities, processes, customer or stakeholder outcomes, and final results.

## Mental Models

- **Measures are behavior architecture.** Ask, "What will people pay attention to, protect, or game if this is measured and rewarded?" A metric is an intervention, not a neutral observation.
- **Use a dashboard as a navigation system, not a finish line.** Read early signals before waiting for final outcomes, and revise the route when evidence changes.
- **Follow the causal chain.** Start with the capability or behavior the change must build, connect it to process performance, then to customer or stakeholder results and financial or mission outcomes.
- **Treat risk as a portfolio of levers.** A high score is useful only if it shows where to reduce ambiguity, improve information, strengthen capability, or increase commitment. A low score is not automatically healthy if it reflects complacency.

## Anti-patterns

- **Measuring everything or measuring only at the end:** This overwhelms attention early and removes the chance to correct course.
- **Rewarding A while hoping for B:** Individual competition, short-term volume, or delivery speed will not reliably produce collaboration, quality, or loyalty when those are not measured and rewarded.
- **Slogan-based alignment:** A vision statement about quality or customers is not credible when supervisors visibly override quality controls or tolerate behavior that contradicts it.
- **Punitive measurement:** Excessive rewards and sanctions encourage data shading, target manipulation, and unethical work rather than learning.
- **Precision theater:** Detailed measures built for a predictable setting create false confidence when the change is ambiguous, turbulent, or moving faster than the data can support.
- **The cursory review:** A periodic "how is it going?" with no systematic review, milestones, or decision criteria leaves a major change effectively out of sight.
- **Ignoring inconvenient evidence:** Enthusiasm, confirmation bias, and a desire to protect the initiative can prevent leaders from hearing bad news until options have narrowed.

## Worked Example (study depth): Control Production Systems Realigns Its Measures

**Situation.** Control Production Systems (CPS), a mid-sized designer and provider of customized production-control systems, was losing market share. Customer loyalty was declining, product and service updates were harder to sell, and price was doing more of the work of winning business. This was happening despite innovative, high-quality offerings. A cross-functional team of sales and customer-support employees was formed after a town hall, with a two-month diagnostic mandate, monthly senior-management reporting, an intranet for communication, and explicit norms of candor and no reprisals.

**The measurement problem.** Sales agents were organized geographically and paid through salary plus commission, then handed customers to support staff. Support employees were rewarded for cost control and throughput. No customer contact was treated as good, short calls were preferred to long troubleshooting calls, standardized responses were favored, and calls were not systematically recorded. These measures reduced immediate service cost but discouraged relationship building and concealed whether customers were getting value from the products.

**Diagnosis and redesign.** The change team found that customers with minimal contact were less likely to form a relationship with CPS, derive value from purchases, or learn about useful innovations. Benchmarking confirmed that CPS was falling behind competitors in service models, satisfaction, and purchase decisions. The team reframed the desired position as a trusted productivity and quality partner. CPS integrated sales and support, formed industry-based account subteams, and shifted the relationship toward customer intimacy, service excellence, solution finding, and one-stop service.

**Measures during the transition.** The team measured employee understanding and commitment, skill acquisition, pilot results, and service failures in delivery, response time, quality, and relationship management. It looked for systemic causes rather than blaming individuals, developed remedies, encouraged experimentation, and used milestones and small wins to reinforce progress.

**Measures after alignment.** The organization tracked satisfaction with the breadth and depth of services, response time, referrals, repeat sales, and margins. Rewards changed to salary plus team-based performance incentives tied to customer satisfaction and retention, share of each customer's business in the relevant product and service area, and customer profitability over time. Over the following three years, the targeted measures improved, and customer-service feedback influenced product refinement and development.

**Lesson.** CPS moved from first-order measures that optimized short-term service cost to measures that connected service quality with second-order outcomes: loyalty, follow-up purchases, and profitability. The change succeeded not by adding data indiscriminately, but by diagnosing what the old measures caused, involving affected people, tracking leading signals during transition, and aligning rewards with the intended relationship.

## Key Takeaways

1. Start measurement at the inception of change and keep it active through institutionalization and review.
2. Choose a small, balanced set of legitimate measures that people can influence and that reflect both immediate signals and later outcomes.
3. Match Simon's four control levers to the phase: scan the environment and clarify values early, steer execution in the middle, and verify outcomes and learning at the end.
4. Use strategy maps to make assumptions and causal links visible, then use a balanced scorecard to monitor the links from multiple perspectives.
5. Treat risk tools as action guides. The Risk Exposure Calculator identifies systemic drivers; DICE identifies project conditions that can be changed.
6. Align statements, measures, rewards, and tolerated behavior. People believe the system leaders actually use.
