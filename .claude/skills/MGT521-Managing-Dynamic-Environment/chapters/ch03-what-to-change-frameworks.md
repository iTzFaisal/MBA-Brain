# Chapter 3: What to Change in an Organization: Frameworks

## Core Idea

Organizational change requires two different judgments:

- **What should change?** This is the content and diagnosis question.
- **How should the change be carried out?** This is the process question addressed by the Change Path Model in Chapter 2.

Good execution cannot rescue a poor diagnosis. The chapter uses the historical example of bloodletting to make this point: practitioners could become skilled at the procedure while acting on a false understanding of the problem. Likewise, a change initiative can generate acceptance, momentum, and implementation discipline while solving the wrong problem. A clear purpose or vision and an accurate understanding of the organization's critical needs are both necessary.

The chapter treats organizations as **open, dynamic, multilevel, time-dependent, and complex systems**. Organizations exchange information, materials, and energy with their environments; their internal parts are interdependent; and people can hold different views of the organization's purpose. A change in one component can alter the behavior of other components, sometimes in unexpected ways. Therefore, diagnosis is not a one-time activity performed at the start of a project. Leaders should revisit assumptions and evidence as the environment, internal conditions, objectives, and outputs change.

The central diagnostic map is the **Nadler and Tushman's Congruence Model**. It links environmental and historical inputs to strategy, then to the transformation process of work, formal organization, informal organization, and people, and finally to outputs and feedback. The other frameworks add distinct lenses:

- **Open Systems Approach** keeps the organization connected to its environment.
- **Sterman's Systems Dynamics Model** exposes feedback, delays, stocks, side effects, and nonlinear responses.
- **Quinn's Competing Values Model** connects individual, team, department, and organizational perspectives and makes value conflicts visible.
- **Greiner's Model of Organizational Growth** adds a growth-stage and crisis perspective.
- **Stacey's Complexity Theory** emphasizes emergence, self-organization, uncertainty, and the limits of long-term control.

Use these models together rather than treating one as a complete description of reality. The practical aim is to identify the real gaps, understand the interdependencies, choose changes that restore useful alignment, and keep learning while the system responds.

## Frameworks Introduced with When/How

### Open Systems Approach

**When to use it:** Start with this lens whenever diagnosing a change, especially when performance has shifted because of competitors, technology, regulation, economic conditions, social expectations, or other environmental forces. It is also useful when an organization appears internally efficient but is becoming irrelevant outside its boundaries.

**Core view:**

- An organization is not isolated. It obtains resources from its environment and transforms them into outputs that the environment accepts or rejects.
- A system is made of interrelated and interdependent parts, not a simple chain of linear causes.
- Systems tend toward equilibrium and usually require some force or energy to move out of an established state.
- Different participants may understand the system's function and purpose differently.
- An event, issue, or force should be analyzed as part of the larger system rather than in isolation.

**How to apply it:**

1. Scan the external environment, including political, economic, social, technological, ecological, and legal factors.
2. Examine the organization's history, because earlier choices shape its mission, culture, strategy, capabilities, constraints, and access to resources.
3. Identify what the organization takes in, how it transforms those inputs, what it produces, and how the results feed back into future decisions.
4. Look for misalignment between the environment, the strategy, and the internal organization. Ask what the environment now rewards, permits, or threatens that it did not previously.
5. Revisit the analysis after significant environmental or internal shifts. A successful past design can become incongruent when conditions change.

The open-systems lens prevents leaders from treating an internal redesign as sufficient when the real issue is a changed marketplace. Disruption can damage established organizations, but it can also create the conditions for renewal and creative destruction.

### Nadler and Tushman's Congruence Model

**When to use it:** Use this as the chapter's primary framework for organizing a comprehensive diagnosis, communicating it clearly, and turning it into a change plan. It is particularly useful when leaders know that performance is weak but do not yet know which organizational components are responsible or how a proposed change will affect the rest of the system.

**Core view:** Organizational performance depends on the fit, or congruence, among four internal components and their alignment with the environment and strategy:

- **Work:** The tasks the organization and its subunits must perform to implement the strategy. This includes key success factors, coordination requirements, skill requirements, decision authority, and the degree of standardization or judgment required.
- **People:** The knowledge, skills, attitudes, roles, responsibilities, and influence of employees and other important individuals. Formal leaders matter, but informal leaders and people with critical technical expertise may be equally important.
- **Formal organization:** The designed architecture of roles, reporting relationships, departments, structures, systems, processes, and controls. Examples include human resource systems, compensation, performance management, training, information systems, budgets, measurement, and production systems.
- **Informal organization:** The relationships, norms, values, beliefs, culture, informal influence patterns, political relationships, decision habits, and managerial styles that shape how work actually gets done.

The broader flow is:

1. **Inputs:** External environment, organizational history, and resources.
2. **Strategy:** The intended or emergent approach for using capabilities to respond to opportunities and threats. Compare what leaders say the strategy is with the strategy visible in actual decisions and resource allocation.
3. **Transformation process:** Work, formal organization, informal organization, and people interact to convert inputs into results.
4. **Outputs:** Products and services, profitability or mission achievement, customer satisfaction, member satisfaction, and development of organizational and individual competencies.
5. **Feedback:** Results become new information that can prompt changes to strategy or internal alignment.

**How to apply it:**

- Begin with the external environment, history, and resources. State what has changed and what constraints or capabilities those inputs create.
- Clarify the strategy and the purpose of the proposed change. Decide whether the initiative is fine-tuning an existing strategy or changing the strategy itself.
- Translate the strategy into key tasks and success factors.
- Test each task against the formal structure and systems, the informal culture and power patterns, and the people required to perform it.
- Examine fit among the four components, not just fit between each component and the strategy. For example, empowerment will be undermined if supervisors, rewards, and culture continue to reinforce control and mistrust.
- Define desired outputs and measures. Include more than financial results: customer and member satisfaction and the development of capabilities also matter.
- Repeat the analysis at the individual, group, unit, and whole-organization levels. A change that looks incremental to a chief executive may be transformational for the department that must absorb it.

Congruence has two related meanings. **Internal fit** supports efficiency: the tasks, people, formal systems, and informal system reinforce one another. **External fit** supports effectiveness: the strategy and internal organization respond to environmental realities. Both matter. The model is dynamic rather than a one-way claim that strategy always dictates structure. Existing systems and capabilities can also create new strategic options, as when delivery expertise or server capacity becomes the basis for a new business.

Use congruence as a practical objective, not as a demand for perfect stability. In a fast-moving environment, an acceptable alignment that can be adjusted may be better than a carefully optimized design that arrives too late.

### Sterman's Systems Dynamics Model

**When to use it:** Use this model when a seemingly rational intervention produces resistance, side effects, competitive responses, delayed results, or outcomes that reverse over time. It is especially important when leaders are tempted to explain a problem with one direct cause and one obvious remedy.

**Core view:** Managers often use a linear model: identify a gap, make a decision, take an action, and expect the intended result. Sterman instead emphasizes that decisions interact with the environment, other actors, and existing organizational dynamics. The result may be nonlinear and counterintuitive.

Important sources of surprise include:

- **Feedback loops:** An action changes the conditions that influence later actions by the organization, employees, customers, or competitors.
- **Side effects:** The intervention can improve the target measure while damaging quality, trust, cost, or another objective.
- **Time lags:** The effect may not appear until long after the decision, leading leaders to abandon a sound change or intensify an inappropriate one.
- **Inventories and buffer stocks:** Stored resources or capacity can absorb or exaggerate changes and obscure the real relationship between action and result.
- **Attribution errors:** People tend to assign outcomes to the most visible recent action even when the causal relationship is weak or delayed.
- **Multiple goals:** Optimizing one measure can create losses elsewhere.

**How to apply it:**

1. State the intended effect and list plausible unintended effects.
2. Identify the other actors who will respond, including employees, customers, competitors, regulators, and internal groups.
3. Map how the action changes incentives, behavior, information, capacity, quality, costs, and later decisions.
4. Identify delays, buffers, and indicators that could be misleading in the short run.
5. Test the intervention over both short and long horizons and establish monitoring points before acting.
6. When results disappoint, question the underlying assumptions rather than simply applying more force to the same intervention.

Sterman also supports progressively deeper learning. **Single-loop learning** adjusts operations without questioning the existing objectives. **Double-loop learning** questions the assumptions, policies, standards, values, and operating methods behind those objectives. **Triple-loop learning** goes further by questioning the underlying rationale for the organization and why it exists. More data is useful only when leaders model the relationships and delays that give the data meaning.

### Quinn's Competing Values Model

**When to use it:** Use this model when people disagree about what the organization should prioritize, when a culture or reward system appears one-sided, or when a diagnosis must connect individual, team, department, and organizational levels. It is also useful when conflict between functions reflects different legitimate assumptions rather than simple incompetence.

**Core view:** Two dimensions create four organizational frames:

- **Internal versus external focus:** Is attention directed primarily toward how the organization operates or toward its environment and external fit?
- **Control versus flexibility:** Is the priority stability, direction, and compliance, or adaptation, change, and responsiveness?

The resulting value orientations are:

- **Human Resources View:** Internal focus with flexibility.
- **Open Systems View:** External focus with flexibility.
- **Rational Economic View:** External focus with control.
- **Internal Process View:** Internal focus with control.

Each frame contains valid concerns and assumptions, but the values compete. Individuals tend to operate more naturally from one quadrant and may find the assumptions of another quadrant difficult to accept. An organization still needs all four orientations. Too much flexibility can become chaos; too much control can become rigidity and paralysis.

**How to apply it:**

- Diagnose which frame dominates the culture, major tasks, decision processes, and reward systems.
- Ask which frame the change requires and which frame is currently missing.
- Check whether the organization has managers with the roles and skills needed for the desired orientation.
- Deliberately include people who see the organization through different frames, especially when marketing, operations, research, or other functions define the environment differently.
- Balance the desired shift with the controls and capabilities that must remain.

For an external and flexible shift, Quinn identifies **innovator** and **broker** roles. Innovation requires understanding change, creative thinking, and risk-taking. Brokering requires influence, negotiation, persuasion, and coalition building. These roles show how a value orientation becomes observable managerial behavior rather than remaining an abstract cultural label.

### Greiner's Model of Organizational Growth

**When to use it:** Use this model when an organization is growing, when a formerly successful design is becoming strained, or when leaders must decide whether an issue calls for incremental adjustment or revolutionary transformation. It helps explain why a crisis may be a predictable consequence of growth rather than an isolated management failure.

**Core view:** Organizations tend to pass through periods of relative stability and evolutionary, incremental change. These periods are periodically interrupted by crises that require revolutionary change. As the organization grows, previous ways of operating become less aligned with its internal and external realities. Pressure accumulates until a breaking point forces a more fundamental redesign.

The chapter presents Greiner's framework as five growth states followed by five crisis stages. The important diagnostic pattern is the transition between them, including pressures associated with:

- A founding leader no longer being sufficient and a crisis of leadership.
- The need for functional specialization.
- The need for greater autonomy and delegation as the organization expands.
- The need to decentralize into divisions that stay closer to customers.
- The need for greater flexibility so the organization can use the potential of its employees.

**How to apply it:**

1. Describe the organization's current size, growth condition, dominant management approach, and accumulated tensions.
2. Identify the crisis that the current design is struggling to resolve.
3. Decide whether incremental tuning can close the gap or whether the crisis signals a need for broad transformation.
4. Anticipate the values that must shift. For example, movement from autonomy problems toward growth through delegation requires a movement from control toward flexibility.
5. Recheck whether the proposed redesign creates a new tension rather than assuming the old tension disappears.

Greiner is a useful heuristic, not a law. The evidence supporting its universal sequence is weak, and not all organizations follow the proposed path. Internet-enabled ventures, global partnerships, and broker organizations can develop differently. Use the model to surface likely growth tensions and timing questions, not to force an organization into a prescribed sequence.

### Stacey's Complexity Theory

**When to use it:** Use this perspective when the future is highly uncertain, relationships are nonlinear, outcomes are emergent, or no leader can reliably specify and control the long-term result. It is particularly useful as a corrective to programmatic change plans that assume the organization will move predictably from a defined current state to a defined future state.

**Core propositions:**

- Organizations are webs of nonlinear feedback loops connected to other people and organizations through additional nonlinear loops.
- Feedback systems can move among stable and unstable states and can approach chaos.
- Organizations are pulled toward stability through integration, control, security, certainty, and adaptation, but also toward instability through division, innovation, and separation from the environment.
- Too much stability produces ossification and resistance; too much instability produces disintegration. Success lies between frozen stability and chaos.
- Short-run dynamics may look irregular and discontinuous even when longer-term trends can be identified.
- A successful organization faces a specifically unknowable future because unpredicted events can change both results and the way results are achieved.
- Managers can act in the short term, but they cannot control the long-term future through analysis and formal controls alone.
- Long-term development can self-organize through political interaction and group learning. Managers reason partly by analogy as they create and learn about their environments.

**How to apply it:**

- Replace detailed predictions of the ultimate state with a directional vision and a small number of useful ground rules.
- Create conditions in which interaction, learning, innovation, and efficiency can emerge through the relationships of participants.
- Involve teams and multiple voices in needs assessment, vision, strategy, and implementation.
- Use linear and nonlinear evidence, scenario thinking, and alternative approaches to make uncertainty more understandable without pretending to remove it.
- Identify likely leverage and resistance points, but treat them as hypotheses rather than certainties.
- Monitor continuously, prepare contingencies, and adjust as unplanned events occur.

Small changes at important points can have very large downstream effects, but the location of those leverage points and the eventual result often cannot be predicted with confidence. Vision and strategy remain valuable as directional beacons; they become counterproductive when treated as detailed instructions for an unknowable future. This view does not eliminate managed change. It changes the leader's role from controlling every outcome to shaping conditions for adaptive, self-organizing responses.

## Key Concepts

- **Diagnosis is the content side of change.** The Change Path Model organizes the process; the frameworks help determine what must be changed and why.
- **Open systems exchange resources with their environments.** Internal efficiency is not enough if outputs no longer fit what the environment values.
- **History is an input.** Early choices leave durable effects on culture, capabilities, strategy, and constraints. Past success can become a source of present rigidity.
- **Strategy may be intended or emergent.** Compare declared strategy with the strategy revealed by decisions, resource allocation, and repeated behavior.
- **Congruence is a fit problem.** The key question is not only whether each component is individually sound, but whether work, people, formal systems, and informal dynamics reinforce one another and the strategy.
- **Change propagates.** A change to strategy or one transformation component can create a domino effect across tasks, structure, systems, culture, and people.
- **Formal and informal organization are both real.** The organization chart and formal systems may prescribe one behavior while norms, relationships, power, and workarounds produce another.
- **Outputs must be broad and measurable.** Profitability or mission achievement should be considered alongside customer satisfaction, member satisfaction, and capability development.
- **Feedback makes the diagnosis dynamic.** Results provide new inputs and may require a revised strategy or realignment.
- **Fit has an efficiency-effectiveness tension.** Tight internal alignment can improve efficiency, but excessive stability can create routines that suppress innovation and adaptability.
- **Learning can deepen.** Single-loop learning fixes operations; double-loop learning questions operating assumptions; triple-loop learning questions the organization's underlying rationale.
- **Different levels reveal different problems.** Individual, group, department, and organizational analyses may produce different definitions of the same change.
- **Value conflict is often structural.** Internal versus external focus and control versus flexibility create legitimate but competing perspectives.
- **Growth creates predictable pressure.** Incremental adaptation can postpone a crisis, but accumulated misfit may eventually require revolutionary change.
- **Complexity limits prediction.** Leaders can improve preparedness and directional judgment without claiming precise control over long-term outcomes.
- **Analysis must be repeated.** Environmental conditions, objectives, assumptions, outputs, and resistance can shift during the initiative.

## Mental Models

1. **The outside-in value flow:** Start with environment, history, and resources; clarify strategy; translate it into work; align formal and informal organization and people; assess outputs; feed the results back into the next diagnosis.
2. **The fit web, not the broken-link chain:** Do not ask only which component is defective. Ask how each component fits the others, where a proposed change creates new gaps, and what must change together.
3. **The two-loop intervention test:** For every action, trace the intended effect and then trace likely reactions, side effects, delays, and later feedback. An intervention changes the system that it is trying to improve.
4. **The four-frame check:** For any major decision, ask what the Human Resources, Open Systems, Rational Economic, and Internal Process Views would notice, value, or warn against. A missing frame is a diagnostic risk.
5. **The two clocks:** View change through both the growth clock and the systems clock. Growth can accumulate pressure toward a crisis, while feedback delays can make today's intervention look ineffective or successful before its real effects appear.
6. **Alignment with slack for adaptation:** Seek enough congruence to perform, but not so much rigid fit that the organization loses the ability to learn, innovate, and respond.
7. **The directional beacon:** In uncertain conditions, use vision and strategy to orient action, not to pretend that the final route and destination can be specified in advance.

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating the organization as a bubble:** Analyze internal operations without examining PESTEL forces, competitors, customers, technology, or resource flows. Correct by beginning with the open-systems context.
- **Confusing implementation quality with diagnosis quality:** Build momentum around a change without clarifying the critical need, purpose, or strategic fit. Correct by answering the what question before optimizing the how.
- **Running a one-shot diagnosis:** Freeze the original assumptions even after the environment, outputs, or resistance changes. Correct by scheduling repeated reviews and updating the analysis.
- **Changing one component in isolation:** Redesign work or structure while leaving skills, rewards, systems, norms, and leadership unchanged. Correct by checking all four Congruence Model components and their interactions.
- **Assuming strategy flows only downward:** Treat strategy as fixed and ignore how capabilities and systems can create new strategic options. Correct by allowing feedback from transformation capabilities to strategy.
- **Using linear cause-and-effect logic:** Assume that one action will produce one predictable result. Correct by mapping other actors, feedback loops, side effects, delays, buffers, and multiple goals.
- **Misreading delayed evidence:** Abandon a change too soon or intensify it because results have not yet appeared. Correct by defining leading and lagging indicators and a time horizon before judging.
- **Optimizing a single measure:** Increase quantity, reduce cost, or improve short-term profit while degrading quality, customer response, capability, or long-term adaptability. Correct by measuring a balanced set of outputs.
- **Allowing one Quinn quadrant to dominate:** Treat control as the only answer, or treat flexibility as automatically healthy. Correct by balancing internal and external needs with control and adaptation.
- **Treating Greiner as destiny:** Force every organization into a universal growth sequence. Correct by using the model as a heuristic for crises and tensions, while checking the organization's actual path.
- **Pursuing perfect congruence in a fast environment:** Delay action while optimizing a design that may be obsolete when implemented. Correct by using workable approximations and adjusting through feedback.
- **Managing complexity through total control:** Assume detailed plans and formal controls can determine the long-term future. Correct by setting direction and conditions that allow learning and self-organization.
- **Collecting data without a model:** Accumulate information or invoke big data without understanding relationships, nonlinear effects, or lags. Correct by modeling the dynamics and including diverse perspectives.
- **Relying only on formal authority:** Ignore informal leaders, political influence, norms, and groups that can facilitate or resist change. Correct by making informal dynamics explicit and engaging key people.
- **Assuming one size fits all:** Apply the same change design across organizational levels or units. Correct by drilling down to the specific tasks, people, systems, and norms that each area needs.

## Worked Example (Study Depth)

### Dell: Restoring Congruence After a Market Shift

**Starting fit.** Dell became successful as a low-cost, efficient producer that sold computers directly to customers. Its environment rewarded reliable equipment, good prices, fast delivery, and service, especially for corporate buyers. The strategy was to make computers efficiently and treat them largely as commodities. The key work was production and distribution. Formal systems supported simplicity and speed: build-to-order manufacturing, minimal inventory, a highly effective supply chain, direct customer relationships, and a cash cycle in which customers paid before Dell incurred production costs. The organization's management expertise and systems fit this strategy well for years.

**Loss of fit.** The environment changed in several connected ways. Consumer demand grew while corporate growth weakened; customers wanted design and attractive machines rather than only processing power; overseas markets were less willing to use Internet ordering and direct delivery; and competition increasingly involved software, Internet capabilities, and retail channels. Dell continued optimizing its old playbook while competitors offered sleeker products and used retail distribution. The original strategy and transformation components were therefore no longer congruent with the market. A clean, efficiency-oriented organization had become too simple for the new demands.

**Realignment.** After returning as CEO in 2007, Michael Dell changed multiple components rather than merely instructing the old organization to sell more:

- **Strategy:** Dell emphasized design, consumer responsiveness, product variety, retail access, and systems solutions in addition to low-cost computer production.
- **Work:** Product design, management of retail distribution, development and timing of new products, and service and systems solutions became critical tasks alongside production.
- **Formal organization:** Dell added retail relationships, created new distribution mechanisms, reorganized around customer segments such as consumers, corporations, small and mid-sized businesses, and government and education, and began overhauling systems and processes for greater complexity.
- **Informal organization:** Managers had to shift their mindset from optimizing a direct-production model to responding flexibly to customers, retailers, design demands, and new market segments. New networks and relationships had to develop.
- **People:** Senior leaders were replaced or added for the new capabilities. Dell recruited executives with consumer-product, retail, restructuring, and systems experience. Group leaders received clear financial targets with discretion over how to achieve them.

New notebooks, mini-notebooks, smartphones, and broader services followed. Some moves were later reversed or refined, including exiting smartphones. Dell also went private in 2013 to pursue longer-term changes away from public-market pressure and acquired EMC in 2016 to strengthen cloud storage, cloud computing, and enterprise services.

**Diagnosis and lesson.** The Congruence Model explains why the response had to be systemic: a strategy shift changed the key success factors, which changed the work, required new skills and relationships, and demanded new structures, systems, culture, and leadership. Dell's later financial results showed that realignment is not a single event or guaranteed solution; margins and revenue initially remained under pressure, and further strategic moves were needed. The example therefore demonstrates three rules: diagnose the environment again when performance changes, change the components that must support the new strategy, and keep monitoring because a new state of fit can itself become misaligned as competitors and technology evolve.

## Key Takeaways

1. Separate the content question, what to change, from the process question, how to change it.
2. Use the Open Systems Approach to keep environmental forces, history, resources, and feedback in view.
3. Make Nadler and Tushman's Congruence Model the first-pass organizational map: environment and history shape strategy, strategy shapes the transformation process, and outputs provide feedback.
4. Check fit among work, people, formal organization, and informal organization, not just the quality of each part by itself.
5. Use Sterman to test interventions for nonlinear responses, side effects, time lags, buffer effects, attribution errors, and competitive or employee reactions.
6. Use Quinn to expose competing values and missing perspectives across individual, team, department, and organizational levels.
7. Use Greiner to ask whether growth has created a crisis that incremental tuning can no longer resolve, while treating the model as a heuristic rather than a fixed sequence.
8. Use Stacey to remain effective when the future is unknowable: set direction, create conditions for learning and self-organization, monitor, and adapt.
9. Balance internal efficiency with external effectiveness and adaptability. Perfect alignment is not always the best target in a rapidly changing environment.
10. Revisit the diagnosis throughout the change initiative. The organization, its environment, its assumptions, and its outputs will continue to move.
