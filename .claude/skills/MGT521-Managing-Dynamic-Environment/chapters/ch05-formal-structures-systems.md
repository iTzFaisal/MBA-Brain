# Chapter 5: Navigating Change through Formal Structures and Systems

## Core Idea

Formal structures and systems are the organization's designed infrastructure for turning strategy into coordinated action. A formal structure divides, groups, and coordinates work through roles, departments, reporting relationships, authority, and spans of control. Formal systems are the recurring processes that make the structure operational: planning, budgeting and accounting, information, performance management, rewards, staffing, and other routines that specify how work and decisions are supposed to occur.

These arrangements do more than describe the organization. They direct attention, distribute information and authority, shape incentives, coordinate work, and influence whether people understand and accept change. Leaders create structures and systems, but those arrangements also shape leaders and members by making some behaviors easy, legitimate, or rewarded and making others difficult.

The central change-management question is not whether an organization should be more formal, flatter, more centralized, or more organic. It is whether its structure and systems fit the environment, the strategy, the information-processing demands, the decision rights, and the behavior required in the desired future state. A sound change diagnosis therefore asks:

- What uncertainty and complexity does the organization face?
- What work must be differentiated, and what must be integrated?
- Who has the information and authority needed for each decision?
- Do planning, information, budget, staffing, performance, and reward systems reinforce the strategy?
- What formal route is needed for approval, and what will create actual acceptance after approval?

Formal approval authorizes a project; it does not guarantee that people will adopt it. Change leaders must use existing systems where they help, modify them where they conflict with the change, and sometimes work around them carefully. The long-term aim is an adaptive organization that can learn, coordinate, and adjust without requiring a crisis for every improvement.

## Frameworks Introduced With When/How

### 1. The structural frame and six design dimensions

**When to use it:** Use this frame when diagnosing the current organization, locating a structural obstacle, or designing a future state. It is especially useful before changing reporting lines, decision authority, departmental boundaries, or work processes.

**How to use it:** Map the formal design, then compare it with how work and decisions actually occur. Examine the six dimensions together rather than optimizing one in isolation:

1. **Differentiation:** How much are tasks divided into specialized jobs, functions, departments, or levels? Differentiation clarifies expertise and can improve efficiency, but it can also create silos and more handoffs.
2. **Integration:** How are specialized tasks coordinated into a process or coherent result? Integration includes shared processes, cross-functional teams, liaison roles, and other mechanisms that reconnect differentiated work.
3. **Chain of command:** Who reports to whom, and where does formal decision responsibility sit? This reveals the official power and escalation path.
4. **Span of control:** How many people report to each manager? A narrow span can create costly layers and micromanagement; a wide span can reduce supervision, feedback, and development. The appropriate span depends on the work, strategy, culture, and capability of the people involved.
5. **Centralization versus decentralization:** Where are decisions made? Centralization can support consistency and control; decentralization can place decisions closer to customers, expertise, and changing conditions.
6. **Formal versus informal:** Which roles, processes, and relationships are codified and expected to be followed? The official chart is only part of the design; leaders should also observe the informal practices that determine how the formal design is used.

The diagnostic output should identify task gaps, duplicated work, slow handoffs, unclear decision rights, overloaded managers, and boundaries that block information. It should also show which existing arrangements are valuable and must be protected during change.

### 2. The mechanistic-organic continuum

**When to use it:** Use this framework when the organization or a particular unit must be matched to its level of environmental uncertainty, dynamism, risk, and need for efficiency or innovation.

**How to use it:** Treat mechanistic and organic forms as ends of a continuum, not as labels for good and bad organizations.

- A **mechanistic** design uses specialized and relatively fixed tasks, formal rules, hierarchy, narrow spans, centralized knowledge and control, and mainly vertical communication. It fits routine work, relatively stable conditions, and situations where reliability, standardization, and the cost of error are central.
- An **organic** design uses flexible roles, fewer rules, broader participation, decentralized expertise, horizontal communication, and more fluid coordination. It fits dynamic and ambiguous conditions where learning, innovation, and adaptation are central.

There is no universal best form. A mature production operation may benefit from standardization while research, new-product development, or market expansion needs more organic arrangements. A change leader can therefore design a differentiated organization with different subunits using different degrees of formality, provided the interfaces and decision rights between them are clear.

### 3. Galbraith's information-processing view

**When to use it:** Use it when uncertainty is increasing, decisions are delayed, information is trapped in departments, or a proposed structure seems unable to coordinate the work required by the strategy. Major change itself increases short-term uncertainty, so this framework applies to the change process as well as to the end state.

**How to use it:** Compare the organization's information-processing requirements with its information-processing capacity. As uncertainty rises, more information must be exchanged among decision makers. The leader has two broad choices:

- **Reduce the need to process information:** Add slack or buffers such as people or inventory; create more self-contained units around products, customers, or geography; outsource or redesign work to reduce interdependence; or use automation, multi-skilled workers, flatter structures, and self-managed teams.
- **Increase the capacity to process information:** Use clear decision rules and criteria, vertical information systems and decision reports, richer horizontal communication, cross-functional roles, and formal or informal networks.

Horizontal mechanisms can be scaled to the problem. They include direct contact between affected people, liaison roles, multi-department task forces, formal teams, integrating roles with cross-functional authority, managerial linking roles with stronger decision authority, and dual-authority arrangements such as a matrix. The choice should be proportionate: a direct designer-manufacturing conversation may solve a small interface problem, while a recurring enterprise-wide conflict may require a formal integrating role or shared governance.

Technology alone does not solve an information problem. Leaders must also settle where information resides, who can access it, what form it takes, how privacy and security are protected, who has the decision right, and how boundaries or intellectual-property concerns will be handled.

### 4. Congruence and strategic alignment

**When to use it:** Use this framework when the change affects the value proposition, strategy, operating model, performance outcomes, or relationships with suppliers and other external partners.

**How to use it:** Test the fit among the external environment, the strategy, and the internal formal organization. Then trace the strategy into the systems that implement it: structure, information, budgeting, staffing, performance measures, rewards, processes, and external relationships. Include the organization's history, culture, and resources in the diagnosis because they affect what can be changed and how quickly.

The sequence matters: strategic choices should guide structural choices, and systems should then be aligned to support the selected structure. A customer-retention strategy will be undermined if measures and rewards emphasize only short-term cost reduction. A global system may also need local adaptation if a uniform rule produces behavior that conflicts with local context. The business case should include the benefits and risks of the proposal, the cost of implementation, and the cost and risk of doing nothing.

### 5. Structural dilemmas

**When to use it:** Use this lens when every available design appears to create a different problem. Structural choices involve enduring trade-offs rather than a final solution that eliminates all tensions.

**How to use it:** Make the trade-off explicit and choose where to manage it. The central dilemmas include:

- **Differentiation versus integration:** Specialization improves focus and expertise but increases the need for coordination, communication, and costly integrating mechanisms.
- **Gaps versus overlaps:** Unassigned work can fall through the cracks; overlapping assignments can create conflict, redundancy, and wasted effort.
- **Control versus flexibility:** Rules and hierarchy improve consistency but can slow initiative and local response; autonomy improves adaptability but can create variation and ambiguity.

Once a structural option is selected, formal policies, processes, roles, and systems should be used to reduce its weaknesses. A leader should also ask whether the problem can be solved by reducing interdependence or differentiation rather than continually adding departments and coordination layers.

### 6. Approval architecture and three routes to change

**When to use it:** Use this framework before spending significant resources, changing work rules, altering strategy, or making a change visible to formal authorities. The required route depends on magnitude, cost, risk, strategic importance, urgency, and the organization's tolerance for ambiguity.

**How to use it:** First determine whether approval is actually required or whether the decision is within the change leader's authority. If approval is required, map the process from idea to decision:

- Identify the formal decision maker, required committees, votes, or go/no-go points.
- Determine the approval threshold for money, staffing, risk, compliance, and changes to work organization.
- Check agendas, meeting cycles, budget timing, cycle time, and emergency or expedited routes.
- Identify who has direct authority and who has indirect influence, including people whose work or rewards will be affected.
- Learn what the decision makers require: data and benchmarks in familiar domains, or confidence in the analysis and advocate when the proposal is ambiguous.
- Secure a credible sponsor, do the detailed homework, consult appropriately, and obtain support before the formal meeting when possible.

Howell and Higgins identify three broad approaches:

1. **Straightforward rational/formal approval:** Develop the proposal, place it on the agenda, present its scope, costs, benefits, risks, and alignment, and accept approval, rejection, or a request for rework. This route is strongest when authority is clear, the proposal needs substantial resources, and due process or risk control is important.
2. **Creeping commitment and coalition building:** Use surveys, benchmarks, task forces, preliminary studies, pilots, and stakeholder involvement to clarify the need, address concerns, create shared ownership, and build support before the formal decision. This can produce a tipping point, but it takes time and can create opposing coalitions or destructive politics.
3. **Renegade action:** Begin a manageable, defensible initiative without first seeking formal approval when it is within the leader's discretion. Keep relevant supervisors informed, avoid safety or compliance violations, gather evidence, refine the idea, and preserve credibility. This is a deliberate low-scale experiment, not chaotic disruption or an excuse to evade legitimate authority.

For large, expensive, strategically important, or disruptive changes, a **staged approval process** is often safest. Move from concept and initial analysis to a pilot, then a larger test and final review, with explicit go/no-go decisions. Each stage increases rigor and resource commitment while preserving opportunities to learn, revise, and stop. A well-designed process can stimulate innovation, reduce surprises, manage the portfolio of simultaneous changes, and make the eventual decision appear legitimate.

### 7. Strategic alignment, starting small, and morphing

**When to use it:** Use these tactics when the desired change is large but immediate approval would appear too risky, disruptive, or incongruent with existing systems.

**How to use it:** Frame the initiative in terms of mission, vision, strategy, value, and limited initial resource requirements. Break the path into exploratory research, a focused pilot, review of learning and system conflicts, expansion to a suitable group, and further stages. Each step should produce evidence and clarify what must change next.

Starting small is not merely a political tactic. It allows the organization to learn and modify structures, systems, and assumptions before committing at scale. When a sequence of modest changes accumulates into a major shift, the organization has effectively **morphed** toward a new state. Leaders should still test whether the incremental path addresses the underlying problem; small steps that avoid the real issue are delay, not change.

### 8. Implementation acceptance

**When to use it:** Use this framework after approval and throughout implementation. Approval is a formal decision; acceptance is the degree to which people understand, support, and use the implemented change.

**How to use it:** Build an acceptance infrastructure around the desired behavior:

- Assign clear authority, responsibility, sponsorship, and escalation paths.
- Provide the training, time, staffing, budget, and management bandwidth required to act.
- Align information systems, job definitions, measures, rewards, and routines with the new behavior.
- Involve affected people or credible representatives so they can ask questions, contribute knowledge, and see that the review was serious.
- Communicate the purpose, direction, progress, and implications for employees and other stakeholders.
- Make the changed output part of normal work rather than allowing it to be ignored as a one-time project.
- Use physical space, team composition, and system cutovers thoughtfully because they can alter communication and behavior.

Behavior can sometimes precede attitude. For example, disabling an old system and carefully supporting a new one can require people to practice the new behavior; positive experience may later change their attitudes. This should never substitute for analysis, training, involvement, and support. Heavy-handed directives can reduce information sharing, risk-taking, and trust even when they produce short-term compliance.

### 9. Adaptive systems and structures

**When to use it:** Use this framework when the environment is turbulent, the organization must continuously improve, or future requirements cannot be specified with confidence in advance.

**How to use it:** Design the organization as a learning system that acquires knowledge, spreads it, and uses it. Formalize the conditions that support learning:

- Scan the external environment and create early-warning and opportunity-finding processes.
- Use strategy reviews, performance measures, and a shared view of the gap between current and desired results.
- Encourage experiments, questioning, open debate, honest information, and continuous education.
- Invite diverse methods and competencies; support multiple advocates for ideas while retaining critical review.
- Keep leaders actively involved and make interdependence visible through a systems perspective.
- Reward innovation, development, and information sharing rather than only local efficiency.
- Use networks, cross-functional teams, virtual collaboration, shared technologies, and external partnerships when they improve learning and flexibility.

Adaptive organizations need not eliminate formal systems. They use formal systems to make scanning, learning, collaboration, and adjustment repeatable. Flexible networks can be held together by contracts, shared information, customer demands, common purpose, and trust rather than only by hierarchy. Any new form, including self-organizing teams, should be judged by whether it improves coordination and results in its context.

### An integrated application sequence

1. Diagnose the environment, uncertainty, strategy, history, resources, and desired outcomes.
2. Map the actual structure and systems, including tasks, handoffs, information, authority, budgets, measures, rewards, and external interfaces.
3. Identify the principal fit problems: a missing integration link, excess differentiation, an information bottleneck, a decision-rights gap, or a system that rewards the wrong behavior.
4. Decide whether to reduce information-processing requirements, increase capacity, or use both approaches.
5. Select the approval route and map the people, timing, evidence, and resources needed.
6. Build acceptance by involving recipients and aligning downstream systems before and during implementation.
7. Start with a defensible pilot or staged commitment when uncertainty or risk is high; use evidence to refine the design.
8. Institutionalize what works and retain scanning, measurement, and learning mechanisms for the next change.

## Key Concepts

- **Formal structure:** The formal division, grouping, and coordination of organizational tasks, including hierarchy and decision authority.
- **Formal systems:** Documented routines for coordination and control, including information, accounting, planning, compensation, human resources, and performance systems.
- **Environmental uncertainty:** The variability created by environmental complexity and dynamism. More uncertainty generally creates greater information-processing demand.
- **Mechanistic organization:** A relatively formal, specialized, hierarchical, standardized, and centralized arrangement.
- **Organic organization:** A relatively flexible, decentralized, less specialized, less rule-bound arrangement with stronger lateral communication.
- **Differentiation:** The separation of work into specialized roles or units.
- **Integration:** The mechanisms that coordinate differentiated work into a whole.
- **Vertical information strategy:** Hierarchy, decision rules, criteria, reports, and vertical information systems used to handle uncertainty.
- **Horizontal information strategy:** Direct contact, networks, teams, task forces, integrating roles, and other lateral mechanisms used to cross boundaries.
- **Information-processing fit:** The match between the information a strategy and environment require and the capacity a structure and systems provide.
- **Decision rights:** The explicit authority to decide, act, allocate resources, and escalate an issue.
- **Congruence:** Alignment among the external environment, strategy, and internal formal arrangements.
- **Formal approval:** Authorization through the organization's recognized decision process.
- **Acceptance:** Actual understanding, support, and adoption by the people expected to change their behavior.
- **Creeping commitment:** Gradually increasing involvement and commitment through small decisions, evidence, and participation.
- **Coalition building:** Forming partnerships that create support and influence for a change.
- **Renegade approach:** Initiating a manageable and defensible change without first obtaining formal approval.
- **Morphing:** A gradual transformation produced by a series of smaller changes that accumulate into a significant shift.
- **Adaptive systems:** Structures and routines that support scanning, experimentation, knowledge sharing, learning, and ongoing adjustment.

## Mental Models

### Fit is the objective, not a preferred structure

Ask, "Fit for what conditions and what strategy?" A hierarchy may be appropriate for reliability and control, while a network or cross-functional team may be appropriate for innovation. The answer can differ across departments and across stages of the same project.

### Uncertainty creates an information bill

Every increase in ambiguity, interdependence, or environmental change generates more information that must be gathered, shared, interpreted, and acted upon. Pay the bill by reducing interdependence or by increasing processing capacity. Adding meetings without clarifying decisions, information, and authority does not pay it.

### The organization chart is a behavioral machine

Reporting lines, systems, rewards, physical spaces, and software channels tell people what matters and whom to consult. If the desired behavior conflicts with the system that measures or rewards performance, the system usually wins unless it is deliberately changed.

### Approval opens the gate; acceptance moves the work

A signed decision, budget, or vote legitimizes a change but does not create capability or commitment. Implementation requires clarity, resources, participation, aligned routines, and visible use of the new practice.

### Small steps can change the trajectory

Starting small lowers perceived risk and produces learning. Morphing is powerful because each step can be accepted within the current system while the cumulative direction changes the system itself. The safeguard is to define the strategic destination and test whether the sequence is genuinely moving toward it.

### Formalization is a trade, not a cure

More rules can improve consistency, scalability, and accountability while reducing speed, discretion, and initiative. Less formality can improve flexibility while increasing ambiguity and coordination cost. Formalize the bottleneck and preserve discretion where local judgment creates value.

### Change temporarily makes the organization less certain

Even a well-managed change disrupts familiar roles, information paths, and expectations. The change plan therefore needs its own temporary information-processing capacity: more communication, lateral links, resources, decision criteria, and feedback than business-as-usual may require.

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating mechanistic or organic design as universally superior:** This ignores contingency and can make a reliable operation too loose or an innovative operation too rigid. Choose the form that fits the work and environment.
- **Changing structure before clarifying strategy:** A reorganization without a strategic purpose often moves boxes on a chart while leaving the underlying problem intact. Define the desired value and outcomes first.
- **Adding differentiation without integration:** New departments, specialists, and partners can create more silos and handoffs than the change can absorb. Budget for the coordination mechanisms or reduce unnecessary interdependence.
- **Using hierarchy as the default answer to uncertainty:** Requiring every issue to move upward slows decisions when the relevant expertise is lower or lateral. Use decision criteria, delegated authority, and lateral links where appropriate.
- **Assuming information is shared because it exists somewhere:** Boundary disputes, security, privacy, incompatible formats, and unclear decision rights can prevent useful information from becoming timely action.
- **Triggering formal approval unnecessarily:** Escalating an initiative within the leader's authority can invite delay, politics, and a veto that the system does not require. Distinguish awareness and consultation from formal authorization.
- **Equating formal approval with acceptance:** A vote or executive decision does not supply skills, time, trust, ownership, or aligned rewards. Plan for recipient behavior and implementation conditions.
- **Presenting a high-risk change as a single irreversible bet:** A large proposal without staged evidence increases perceived risk and makes learning expensive. Use pilots, go/no-go gates, and progressive resource commitments.
- **Building coalitions through manipulation or division:** Coalition tactics can create momentum, but they can also damage integrity and produce an opposing coalition. Involve people authentically and preserve long-term relationships.
- **Using the renegade route naively:** Quiet action is not permission to violate authority, safety, compliance, or trust. Keep relevant supervisors informed and ensure the initial scope is defensible.
- **Calling avoidance "incremental change":** A sequence of harmless adjustments is not morphing if it never addresses the strategic problem. Connect each stage to a defined future state and review cumulative impact.
- **Issuing a heavy-handed directive and expecting commitment:** Coercion may produce compliance while reducing information sharing, risk-taking, and retention. Explain purpose, invite useful input, and demonstrate legitimate authority.
- **Allowing growth to outrun infrastructure:** Informal training, role knowledge, and personal coordination may work at small scale and fail suddenly when volume and complexity rise. Add targeted onboarding, feedback, and integration before the bottleneck becomes a crisis.
- **Leaving sponsorship, resources, or ownership ambiguous:** Approved work with no accountable owner, management bandwidth, or executive support becomes optional work. Assign authority and provide the capacity to use it.

## Worked Example (study depth)

### Beck Consulting Corporation: preserving an organic advantage while adding structure

**Situation.** Beck Consulting grew from a one-person relocation consultancy into a 40-person firm over roughly eleven years. Its work was project-based: small teams coordinated moves and facility-management assignments, then re-formed around new clients. The company was known for hands-on customer service, fast decisions, and a strong shared culture. Beck and Susan Smith were the main formal leaders, while most project managers had no status titles and could alternate between leading a project and working as a colleague on another. Assignments considered availability, expertise, and employee preferences. Project managers had substantial autonomy, and performance was judged mainly through project scope, budget, schedule, client relationships, and situational feedback.

This was a deliberately light structure rather than an absence of organization. The project system, personal relationships, word-of-mouth hiring, hourly or individually negotiated pay, and shared service values provided coordination without a conventional hierarchy. At the firm's earlier scale, the organic arrangement supported the value proposition: employees could adapt to clients and help with whatever a move required.

**The fit problem.** In 2012-2013, client growth and mergers caused the workforce to double from about 20 to 40 people. Hiring accelerated before the firm had enough infrastructure. New employees were not trained consistently, people did not always know whom to report to, and communication became difficult. Because project roles changed from assignment to assignment, someone could be a manager on one project and a peer on the next. The firm's internal role structure also did not always match the leadership role visible to a client. Junior employees lacked regular supervisory feedback, while Beck and Smith's increasingly distinct external and operational responsibilities created further uncertainty about authority.

Through the chapter's lenses, the problem was not simply that Beck lacked a formal organization chart. Growth increased differentiation, project interdependence, and information-processing demand faster than the firm's informal integration mechanisms could handle. The original organic design still fit customer-facing work, but it no longer provided enough onboarding, role clarity, knowledge transfer, or integration for the new scale. A blanket move to a tall hierarchy could have damaged the autonomy and service culture that created the firm's advantage.

**Targeted response.** Beck introduced a more formal mentoring system: new employees were paired with experienced people on projects until they could work independently. The company also created a two-hour staff luncheon every other week, including off-site employees, where project issues could be shared and outside experts or vendors could present. Team assignments were shifted so employees could build relationships beyond their usual collaborators, while the leaders continued to preserve a supportive family atmosphere.

The Federal Courthouse move showed when more structure was justified. It was much larger and more complex than prior assignments. Makayla Jones became project leader, maintained direct client contact, coordinated three project managers assigned to individual courts, and held the schedule, budget, and overall picture together. This temporarily concentrated authority in a team-lead role even though the project managers had previously operated as equals. The change initially created tension, but the team discussed the role difference and accepted that the structure was necessary for that project.

**Study analysis.** The response illustrates fit rather than a wholesale shift from organic to mechanistic design:

- The firm formalized the bottlenecks created by scale: onboarding, cross-project communication, and coordination of complex work.
- It increased information-processing capacity through mentoring, shared meetings, and clearer project integration rather than adding hierarchy everywhere.
- It used a temporary or project-specific concentration of authority when complexity required it, while preserving autonomy and fluid teams elsewhere.
- It protected the strategic and cultural strengths of hands-on service, personal responsibility, and adaptability.
- It surfaced remaining design questions for the next growth stage: whether reporting lines and supervisory feedback must become clearer, whether Beck and Smith need a more explicit division of authority, how compensation should evolve, and whether a new management layer would support or undermine the culture.

The case's lesson is that informal coordination has a scale limit. The right response is not to formalize everything or to defend informality at all costs. It is to identify where uncertainty, volume, and interdependence exceed the current system's capacity, then add the smallest structures and routines that restore clarity and coordination. Those additions should be aligned with the organization's value proposition and revisited as growth changes the work.

## Key Takeaways

1. Formal structures divide and coordinate work; formal systems turn the design into recurring behavior.
2. Structures and systems both enable change and constrain the people trying to make it.
3. There is no best structure independent of environment, strategy, uncertainty, risk, and organizational scale.
4. Diagnose differentiation, integration, authority, information flow, spans, and actual practice before redesigning.
5. Rising uncertainty creates an information-processing requirement; reduce interdependence or increase processing capacity.
6. Align the external environment, strategy, structure, information, budgets, measures, rewards, people systems, and partners.
7. Every structural choice creates trade-offs. Manage gaps, overlaps, coordination cost, control, and flexibility explicitly.
8. Understand the approval system before acting: authority, thresholds, timing, decision makers, evidence, and influence patterns matter.
9. Use formal approval, creeping commitment and coalitions, or a carefully bounded renegade experiment according to context and risk.
10. Staged approval and starting small can reduce risk, generate learning, and allow a large change to morph into the organization over time.
11. Approval is not acceptance. Implementation requires ownership, resources, skills, participation, aligned incentives, and visible use.
12. Adaptive organizations formalize learning, scanning, experimentation, knowledge sharing, collaboration, and systems thinking.

## Connects To

- **Change-management gap analysis:** Formal structures and systems are one diagnostic layer in the broader change process. Map this layer before analyzing informal dynamics, stakeholders and recipients, and the change leader's role.
- **Strategy and vision:** Mission, vision, value proposition, and strategy provide the test for whether a structural or systemic change is worth pursuing.
- **Information systems and data governance:** Information-processing fit depends on timely access, usable formats, security, privacy, ownership, and clear decision rights, not only on technology.
- **Budgeting and resource allocation:** Approval timing, budget cycles, capital thresholds, staffing, and management bandwidth determine whether a formally attractive change can proceed.
- **Human resources and performance management:** Job definitions, training, feedback, evaluation, pay, and rewards either reinforce or contradict the new behavior.
- **Project and change governance:** Sponsors, decision gates, pilots, go/no-go reviews, portfolio capacity, and escalation paths turn a change idea into a controlled implementation.
- **Culture and stakeholder acceptance:** Formal legitimacy can support understanding, but recipient participation, trust, perceived fairness, and lived experience determine whether people accept the change.
- **Operations and supply-chain design:** Differentiation, integration, outsourcing, partner networks, standardized processes, and lateral coordination determine how complex work is delivered.
- **Organizational learning and continuous improvement:** Early-warning systems, metrics, experiments, open debate, and knowledge sharing make future change less dependent on heroic intervention.
- **Adaptive leadership:** Leaders must preserve enough structure for accountability while creating enough flexibility for local judgment, innovation, and response to emerging conditions.
