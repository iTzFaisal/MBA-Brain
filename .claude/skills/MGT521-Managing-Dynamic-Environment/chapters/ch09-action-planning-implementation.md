# Chapter 9: Action Planning and Implementation

## Core Idea

Organizational change creates value only when a sound idea is translated into coordinated action. Change leaders therefore need a "do it" orientation: prepare enough to act intelligently, identify who does what and by when, take informed risks, observe the consequences, and adapt. Planning and implementation are not separate phases in which a finished plan is executed mechanically. They form an action-learning-reaction cycle.

An effective change plan connects the present state to the desired future through owners, sequence, resources, milestones, contingencies, communication, influence, and transition arrangements. It also preserves trust and keeps the organization operating while the change takes place. The practical discipline is to plan the work, work the plan, learn from what happens, and revise the route without losing sight of the destination.

## Frameworks Introduced (With When/How)

### 1. Selecting the change path

Mintzberg and Westley's three paths help match the planning style to the situation:

- **Thinking first:** Use when the issue is clear, data are reliable, the context is structured, and decisions can be specified in advance. Use analysis, explicit criteria, schedules, and disciplined execution. A routine process improvement such as Six Sigma fits this path.
- **Seeing first:** Use when a solution must combine many elements, commitment depends on people seeing the whole, and communication across boundaries is essential. Use prototypes, pilots, demonstrations, and shared experiences so people can understand and commit to the solution. New product development is an example.
- **Doing first:** Use when the situation is novel or confusing, detailed specifications would block movement, and simple operating rules can support experimentation. Start with small actions, observe reactions, and use the resulting information to discover a workable path. Testing customer-service approaches or responding to business-model disruption can fit this path.

As ambiguity and complexity increase, the preferred movement is generally from thinking first toward seeing first and then doing first. The path can change as the initiative produces new information. A pilot may move a leader from doing first to seeing first; clearer evidence may later support a more programmatic plan.

Nohria's three generic change strategies provide a second lens:

- **Programmatic change:** Best for clear, well-defined, incremental change. Implement through missions, objectives, plans, training, timelines, and steering committees. Watch for inflexible, one-size-fits-all solutions and insufficient attention to behavior.
- **Discontinuous change:** Best when existing assumptions no longer work and a major break or reorientation is necessary, such as restructuring after rapid growth or market change. Use decisive, often top-down structural action. Watch for political coalitions, inadequate resources or control, stress, and the loss of talented people.
- **Emergent change:** Best when direction is ambiguous, the workforce is capable, and useful solutions can grow through incremental initiatives and feedback. Use experimentation, metaphors, employee input, and risk-taking. Watch for confusion, uncertainty about effects, and slow progress.

These lenses do not prescribe a single ideology. Unilateral action can be useful for techno-structural changes involving systems, structures, or technology; participation is particularly valuable for behavioral-social changes involving relationships and culture. Concrete structural action can change behavior, after which attitudes may shift. However, unilateral implementation still needs sensitivity to stakeholder concerns, or avoidable resistance and lost insight may result.

### 2. Planning with others and aligning the plan

Beer, Eisenstat, and Spector's six steps are a cooperative planning sequence:

1. Mobilize commitment through joint diagnosis of business problems.
2. Develop a shared vision of how the organization should organize and manage for competitiveness.
3. Build consensus for the vision, competence to enact it, and cohesion to sustain it.
4. Spread revitalization across departments rather than relying only on top-down pressure.
5. Institutionalize the change through formal policies, systems, and structures.
6. Monitor and adjust strategies as problems emerge.

This sequence is useful as a planning scaffold, not as a universal recipe. It assumes enough commonality of purpose for a shared vision and a relatively cooperative change process. Where goals conflict or the change is imposed from above, the plan needs political, structural, or directive elements as well.

The chapter compares this sequence with Jick's ten commandments, Kotter's eight-stage process, and Lueck's seven steps. Across the models, recurring work includes diagnosing the need, creating urgency and a direction, building leadership or sponsorship, designing implementation and enabling structures, communicating and involving people, producing results, institutionalizing the change, and monitoring or adjusting. The difference is emphasis and sequencing, not the need to address these work areas.

**Alignment review.** Before implementation, test the plan against its objective and deadline, available support and influence, team competence and credibility, logical sequence, owners and dates, milestones, probabilities of success, secondary or lagging effects, bad and good contingencies, dependencies, stakeholder coalitions, and serious obstructors. If the answers reveal a gap, change the plan or increase the capacity to deliver it. A plan is not aligned merely because its activities are individually reasonable; the activities must fit the diagnosis, authority, resources, timing, and human system.

### 3. The 11 action-planning tools

Use the smallest tool that provides adequate control, and combine tools when the change requires it.

1. **To-do list:** Use for a simple initiative or the first outline of a more complex one. List the initial sequence of actions and check them off. It becomes inadequate when ownership, dependencies, or contingencies matter.
2. **Responsibility charting:** Use when multiple people or units must coordinate. List decisions or actions, then assign who is responsible and the relevant dates. The compact coding is **R** for responsibility, **A** for approval or veto, **S** for support and resources, and **I** for informing or consulting before action. Too many approvers make implementation difficult; too few responsible or supportive parties expose the plan.
3. **Contingency planning:** Use for critical actions whose consequences could materially change the initiative. Decision trees map choices, possible consequences, probabilities, and subsequent branches. Scenario planning develops a limited set of plausible futures, works backward from each one, and identifies what would make it real and how the organization should respond. Scenario planning is not the same as forecasting: it starts with possible futures rather than projecting a single trend line from the present.
4. **Flow charting:** Use to understand a current process and design a future one. Diagram task sequences, decision points, participants, and handoffs. The current-state map supports diagnosis; the future-state map keeps the vision concrete and helps recipients understand how work will change.
5. **Design thinking:** Use early for difficult problems that have resisted conventional solutions. Engage users in context assessment, problem framing, ideation, prototyping, pilot testing, evaluation, and translation into action. Its human-centered, iterative character uses abductive reasoning: explore plausible solutions, test them, refine them, and repeat until a viable path earns commitment.
6. **Surveys, survey feedback, and appreciative inquiry:** Use surveys to sample attitudes, experiences, readiness, culture, satisfaction, commitment, or emerging acceptance over time. Use survey feedback when participants need to review and interpret their own results with a skilled facilitator and identify actions. Combine it with appreciative inquiry's active listening, mutual development, feedback, and renewal to build understanding and energy. Protect anonymity, explain the purpose, and attend to validity, reliability, privacy, transparency, and legal or ethical limits.
7. **Project planning and critical path methods:** Use for major changes with deadlines, task durations, resource needs, and dependencies. Work backward from completion, sequence tasks, identify simultaneous work, and plot the timeline. The critical path has the least slack and deserves special attention. Parallel work can shorten cycle time, but it requires coordination because it can also create confusion and duplicated effort. If timing or cost is unacceptable, add resources, revisit specifications or the route, or extend the project.
8. **Force and stakeholder assessment tools:** Use when outcomes depend on support, opposition, culture, or political relationships. Force-field and stakeholder analysis identify forces, key players, and relationships. Add three planning views:
   - **Commitment charts:** Map each major player's position from opposed, through neutral or "let it happen," to "help it happen" and "make it happen." Record the person's level of understanding and distinguish current from desired commitment.
   - **Adoption continuum or AIDA:** Move stakeholders from awareness to interest to desire and then action or adoption. General communication can raise awareness; personal impact, benchmarks, simulations, and test runs can build interest; individual conversations, influential supporters, resource changes, and rewards can support desire and action.
   - **Cultural mapping:** Examine dominant and subcultures, artifacts, norms, beliefs, observed behaviors, outcomes, and the enablers or blockers behind them. Use the map to define the desired culture and the shifts required at team, department, division, or organization level.
9. **Leverage analysis:** Use when a small number of relationships or actions may shift a larger system. Identify opinion leaders, connectors, sponsors, and moments where a modest intervention can create momentum. A sticky vision and the support of a critical few can help a change reach a tipping point, but the plan must then scale without implementation failure or stalled progress.
10. **Training and development tools:** Use when new reporting relationships, jobs, workflows, teams, technologies, or accountabilities require new skills, abilities, or attitudes. Good training builds competence and confidence, surfaces concerns, and makes supportive people capable of performing the new work.
11. **Diverse change approaches:** Use the growing repertoire of management and change techniques as a menu rather than a formula. Select approaches in light of the diagnosed need, the organization's context, and the likely implementation risks. Popularity or a high satisfaction rating does not by itself make a tool appropriate.

### 4. Working the plan ethically and adaptively

Execution requires focus, delivery capacity, support, testing of assumptions, attention to opportunities, appropriate risk-taking, and adaptation. The action-learning-reaction cycle turns implementation into a source of knowledge. Leaders should actively reflect during the work rather than relying on selective memories after success or failure.

Ethics is an implementation capability, not an optional constraint. Honest mistakes can be used for learning, but scapegoating drives risk underground and encourages concealment. Accountability still matters; the challenge is to hold people responsible for their choices without destroying the experimentation needed for change. Decision makers should remain realistic. Implementers may project enough confidence to give others courage, but confidence must not become self-deception or a reason to ignore evidence.

### 5. Communication planning

The communication plan has four purposes: make the need for change widespread, help people understand the personal impact, explain structural and job changes, and keep people informed about progress. Its emphasis changes over four phases:

- **Pre-change approval:** Persuade influential decision makers to authorize the change. Break a proposal into manageable steps when useful, persist, use timing and allies, and connect the proposal to organizational goals, plans, and priorities.
- **Developing the need for change:** Explain the problem, rationale, urgency, vision, and plan. Use credible comparative or benchmark data, clarify how people will be treated, and provide a memorable elevator pitch. A sticky message is simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional, and carried by a story that connects the change to the individual, team, customer, and society.
- **Midstream and milestone communication:** Explain new roles, structures, systems, procedures, and training. Report progress, solicit feedback, correct misconceptions, recognize milestones, and sustain enthusiasm as the novelty fades.
- **Confirming and celebrating:** Communicate results, celebrate progress and completion, capture learning, identify unfinished work, and prepare the organization for the next change. Completion of one phase is not the end of change.

Match channel richness to the message. Routine information can travel through reports or general e-mail. Complex, personal, or emotionally loaded information needs richer channels such as personalized communication, telephone, video, or face-to-face discussion. Line managers and opinion leaders are important links, but they must be briefed and engaged. Communication is two-way: collecting concerns and interpretations is as important as sending the official message.

Six practical principles are emphasized: repeat important messages through multiple media; prefer face-to-face communication when the issue warrants its cost; use line authority; make the immediate supervisor a trusted messenger; identify opinion leaders; and translate general information into personally relevant terms. Clear, timely, candid communication reduces the space in which rumors grow. Social and digital channels can inform, solicit feedback, and build ownership, while direct conversation remains especially valuable when uncertainty is high.

### 6. Influence strategies

Kotter and Schlesinger's seven strategies provide a graduated menu:

1. **Education and communication:** Explain the logic, need, requirements, and importance of the change when understanding is the barrier.
2. **Participation and involvement:** Include people in design and action when time is available and voluntary compliance or active support is needed.
3. **Facilitation and support:** Provide guidance, resources, and help when anxiety, fear, or capability gaps are the barrier.
4. **Negotiation and agreement:** Make explicit deals when resistance is organized, power is salient, or the benefits are unclear. It may secure compliance without wholehearted support.
5. **Manipulation and co-optation:** Attempt covert influence or enlist opponents. It may shift positions temporarily, but perceived manipulation damages trust and can increase resistance.
6. **Explicit or implicit coercion:** Require compliance when urgency is high and other options have been exhausted. Use it cautiously and address the residual negative feelings it creates.
7. **Systemic adjustments:** Change structures, assignments, systems, or processes to reduce resistance and support the desired behavior. Mishandling can mobilize resistance elsewhere.

Push tactics use rational persuasion or pressure and can trigger defensiveness. Pull tactics use inspiration, consultation, shared values, and participation to draw people toward the change. In the evidence summarized in the chapter, inspirational appeals and consultation were the strongest of Falbe and Yukl's nine tactics. Rational persuasion, ingratiation, personal appeals, and exchange were intermediate; direct pressure, legitimating tactics, and coalition pressure were least effective. Credibility and trustworthiness of the change leader condition all of them.

Nutt's four implementation tactics add another lens: **intervention** by respected executives who justify the change and set new performance norms; **participation** by engaging stakeholders; **persuasion** through experts; and **edict** through directives. Intervention combines strong sponsorship with a relatively fast and durable adoption pattern in the chapter's evidence. Participation takes longer but has a stronger record than persuasion or edict. Persuasion is frequently attempted but less successful, while edict is the least attractive default because adoption is weaker and slower. A blended approach, often intervention plus participation, is usually more sensible than treating one tactic as universally correct. Edict belongs near the last resort, not the starting point.

### 7. Transition management

Transition management keeps ongoing operations effective while the organization moves from its present state to the desired one. A transition manager should have:

- **Authority and sponsorship:** a link to the CEO or another senior executive and enough power to make necessary decisions.
- **Resources:** people, money, training, and consulting or specialist expertise.
- **Transition structures:** temporary teams or arrangements that allow normal operations and change work to coexist.
- **A transition plan:** operating arrangements, benchmarks, standards, responsibilities, questions, and decisions for the period between states.

The visible change leader articulates the need and vision. The transition manager and implementation team handle structural and system changes, as well as the emotional and behavioral issues that could otherwise endanger either the work or the operation. Midpoint goals and milestones are especially important for long changes: they are close enough to show progress and permit correction, but far enough away to provide direction.

Transition systems should answer employee questions about new bosses, roles, locations, work terms, customers, and colleagues. They should also reduce anxiety, cultivate a problem-solving climate, communicate with the right individuals and groups, measure and celebrate progress, and capture learning. Closure includes an after-action review (AAR): ask what results were intended, what actually happened, why the difference occurred, and what should be done better next time. Use multiple perspectives and codify the learning so it is available beyond the project team.

## Key Concepts

- **Action is disciplined, not impulsive.** A do-it orientation means acting on a reasoned understanding, not pursuing novelty or bypassing all governance.
- **Authority is distributed.** Senior sponsorship helps, but people in the middle or lower in the hierarchy can test the limits of their empowerment, read the political context, and build support.
- **Planning is contingent.** A plan is a means to improve action, not a reason to ignore emerging information. The appropriate balance of analysis, participation, experimentation, and direction depends on context.
- **Design and adoption are linked.** A technically sound solution can fail when acceptance, understanding, capability, or implementation has not been addressed.
- **Alignment is a system property.** Objectives, sequence, roles, power, resources, skills, stakeholder interests, communication, and contingencies must reinforce one another.
- **Responsibility is not approval.** In responsibility charting, the person doing the work need not be the person with veto power. Excessive approval rights create bottlenecks.
- **A critical path is a management priority.** It is the chain with least slack, not a label for every important activity. Parallel work is valuable only when dependencies and coordination are clear.
- **Data collection has obligations.** Surveys can reveal views that people will not state publicly, but poor questions, weak instruments, unclear purpose, or privacy violations make the information dangerous rather than useful.
- **Adoption is staged.** Stakeholders do not move directly from hearing about a change to acting on it. Awareness, interest, desire, and action require different messages and interventions.
- **Influence is relational.** The same tactic can produce different results depending on credibility, timing, power, readiness, and whether the recipient experiences it as support or threat.
- **Transition is dual work.** The organization must deliver today's products and services while building tomorrow's systems, roles, and behaviors.
- **Learning must be designed.** Feedback, milestone reviews, celebrations, and the AAR prevent experience from disappearing when the project ends.

## Mental Models

- **Ready-fire-aim-refire-re-aim:** In ambiguous or fast-moving situations, begin with a bounded action, learn from the reaction, and correct course. This is not permission to act without thought; it is recognition that action supplies information unavailable in advance.
- **The plan as a hypothesis:** Treat assumptions about sequence, stakeholder response, resources, and timing as testable. Monitor what happens and revise the path rather than defending the document.
- **From here to there while keeping the plane flying:** Change is a concurrent operating problem. Preserve service delivery while temporary structures, new systems, and new behaviors are built.
- **The adoption staircase:** Move people from awareness to interest to desire to action. Diagnose the current step before selecting the next message or intervention.
- **The critical few and the chasm:** A small set of credible sponsors, opinion leaders, and connectors may create momentum, but early enthusiasm must be converted into scalable implementation for the broader majority.
- **Channel richness follows human stakes:** The more complex, personal, or emotionally loaded the message, the richer and more interactive the channel should be.
- **Accountability with psychological safety:** Hold people answerable for choices while treating intelligent failure as information. Blame suppresses the very risk-taking and reporting needed for adaptation.

## Anti-patterns

- Waiting for a perfect plan, complete certainty, or formal permission when a safe, informative first step is available.
- Taking action for excitement or speed without a diagnosis, a feedback mechanism, or a way to limit downside.
- Applying programmatic tools or a participation ritual as a one-size-fits-all answer to every change context.
- Treating a plan as an endpoint and ignoring evidence because it conflicts with the approved sequence.
- Leaving owners, deadlines, decision rights, or support roles implicit after a meeting.
- Giving approval or veto rights to everyone who has an opinion, creating an implementation bottleneck.
- Running every task sequentially when parallel work is feasible, or running parallel work without coordination and creating duplicated effort.
- Using surveys as listening theater, asking weak questions, hiding the purpose, or compromising privacy and anonymity.
- Communicating once through a lean channel and assuming that sending a message means people understood or believed it.
- Letting rumors fill an information vacuum, especially when leaders know the change is personally consequential.
- Starting with manipulation, threats, coalition pressure, or edict when education, participation, support, or negotiation could address the barrier.
- Confusing compliance with commitment and failing to provide the training, resources, or authority required for the new work.
- Removing resistance through structural action without considering dignity, fairness, downstream coalitions, or residual resentment.
- Launching a change without a transition manager, temporary structure, operating safeguards, question channel, or midpoint milestones.
- Declaring victory without an after-action review and losing the knowledge of what actually produced the result.
- Scapegoating honest mistakes, which encourages concealment and makes adaptive execution less likely.

## Worked Example (Study Depth)

### HP Labs: turning diagnostic data into shared understanding

**Situation.** The director of HP Labs asked Barbara Waugh why people did not regard the lab as the world's best industrial research laboratory. Waugh and the director did not begin by prescribing an answer. They used a survey to ask the organization. The feedback filled roughly 800 single-spaced pages and surfaced problems in programs, people, and processes: too many projects with too few priorities, concerns about poor performance and researchers' freedom, and inadequate information infrastructure.

**Implementation move.** The challenge was no longer only to collect data. The change leaders needed senior managers to understand and experience what the data meant. Waugh converted survey material into a play, recruited executives to perform as staff members, recruited junior people to perform as executives, and presented it to 30 senior managers. The managers were initially quiet and then applauded. The chapter presents this as an engaging way to communicate otherwise boring data and make the diagnosis difficult to dismiss.

**Framework analysis.**

1. **Path selection:** The work used a seeing-first and emergent sensibility. The problem crossed programs, people, and processes; commitment required shared understanding; and a creative representation helped people see the whole rather than receive another report.
2. **Planning with others:** The survey and the presentation involved affected people in diagnosing the problem. The role reversal also created a boundary-crossing conversation between hierarchy levels.
3. **Tools:** The primary tools were a survey and survey feedback, supported by a creative design-thinking-like communication device. The survey generated raw input; the performance made it interpretable and actionable for a key stakeholder group.
4. **Influence:** The approach relied on participation, consultation, and an emotionally memorable message rather than pressure. It used the credibility of the experience and the participants' own words, not merely an executive assertion.
5. **Communication:** The play was a rich, personally relevant channel for a complex organizational message. It translated a large volume of information into a shared story and invited a response.
6. **Ethical and adaptive lesson:** The diagnostic material had to represent employees' frustrations and insights faithfully. The broader lesson is to use action to create learning and commitment, then continue the planning cycle rather than assume that one compelling presentation completes implementation.

The example demonstrates a change-planning principle: engagement does not mean asking people for input and then burying it in a report. It means designing a process through which participants and decision makers can understand the evidence, see its implications, and become capable of choosing useful next actions.

## Key Takeaways

1. Choose thinking first, seeing first, doing first, programmatic, discontinuous, emergent, unilateral, or participative elements according to the situation rather than personal preference.
2. Start before certainty is complete, but make the first step informed, bounded, and measurable.
3. Engage others in diagnosis and planning unless confidentiality is genuinely necessary; involvement improves information quality, understanding, and commitment.
4. Translate the vision into owners, dates, dependencies, milestones, contingencies, and decision rights.
5. Use the 11 tools selectively: simple lists for simple work, structured charts for coordination, experiments and design methods for ambiguity, stakeholder and leverage tools for influence, and training for capability.
6. Build feedback into execution. Reassess assumptions, adapt the route, and balance accountability with the willingness to learn from failure.
7. Communicate repeatedly, candidly, in personally relevant language, through channels rich enough for the stakes involved.
8. Prefer credible pull strategies and participation where feasible; reserve coercion or edict for carefully considered cases where urgency and responsibility justify them.
9. Treat transition management as a core implementation function: protect current operations, provide authority and resources, answer questions, mark progress, and manage closure.
10. Finish with an after-action review and codify the learning so the next change starts with more organizational capability.
