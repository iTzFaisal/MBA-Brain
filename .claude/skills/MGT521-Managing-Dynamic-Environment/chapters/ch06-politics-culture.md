# Chapter 6: Navigating Organizational Politics and Culture

## Core Idea

Organizational change is shaped not only by formal structures and systems but also by the informal organization: power relationships, influence networks, norms, history, and culture. These forces determine whose ideas receive attention, who can authorize or block action, how people interpret a proposal, and whether they will actually change their behavior.

The practical task for a change agent is therefore political and cultural diagnosis. Treat politics as the normal process of negotiating scarce resources and divergent interests, not as an automatically unethical activity. Assess both the power available to you and the power perceived by others. Read culture at the level of visible artifacts, stated values, and unspoken assumptions. Then connect those observations to the people who are affected, the people who must behave differently, and the people who can help or obstruct the change.

A change agent is also part of the system being analyzed. Personal reputation, networks, motives, comfort with influence, and assumptions about the organization's culture affect what the agent sees and what tactics will work. Change is more likely when the intervention changes the balance of forces and gives important individuals a credible reason to support it, rather than merely announcing an organizational benefit.

## Frameworks Introduced (When/How)

### 1. Power and politics diagnosis

**When to use it:** Use it before seeking approval, allocating resources, redesigning roles, changing a department's work, or introducing a proposal that threatens established interests. It is especially important when the formal organization chart does not explain who actually makes things happen.

**How to use it:**

1. Establish the limits of formal authority. Identify who can authorize or deny the initiative, who controls spending and staffing, and what decisions require another approval.
2. Assess individual power through four overlapping sources:
   - **Positional power:** legitimate authority attached to a title, including control of resources and formal decisions.
   - **Network power:** access to information and support through formal and informal relationships across levels and boundaries.
   - **Knowledge power:** scarce expertise, credentials, information, and practical knowledge of how formal and informal processes really work.
   - **Personality and reputation power:** the ability to generate trust and enthusiasm, reinforced by a history of success, integrity, or credibility.
3. Separate actual resources from perceived influence. A person with formal authority may be ignored if others do not believe that person can or will act. Conversely, the perceived backing of a trusted senior leader may influence behavior even before any resource is used.
4. Assess departmental power. A department becomes more powerful when it absorbs environmental uncertainty, performs work that is central to organizational survival or strategy, or provides an essential capability that is difficult to substitute. Marketing and sales may reduce uncertainty by bringing in orders; finance may become more central during turbulence; HR or technology may hold power because others depend on their authorization or expertise.
5. Identify the kind of influence involved. **Yea-saying power** can make something happen; **nay-saying power** can prevent it without necessarily having authority to choose the alternative.
6. Look for Hardy's three dimensions: **resource power** over valued resources and sanctions; **process power** over agendas, decision arenas, and appointments; and **meaning power** to define what events, symbols, language, and rituals signify.
7. Choose tactics deliberately. Rational reasons, coalitions, friendliness, negotiation, assertiveness, and escalation all have different effects depending on the target, the relationship, and the culture. Savvy change agents build alliances and obtain information without treating every disagreement as a contest to be won.

### 2. Schein's culture model

**When to use it:** Use it when a proposed change appears rational but behavior does not follow, when a merger combines different work norms, or when visible practices and official values do not explain employee reactions.

**How to use it:** Analyze culture at three levels, moving from what is easiest to see to what is hardest to challenge:

1. **Artifacts:** Observe dress, office layout, status differences, parking, meals, interaction patterns, structures, and processes. Artifacts are visible but ambiguous; do not infer an entire culture from one observation.
2. **Espoused beliefs and values:** Read mission, values, strategy, website, social-media, and public statements. Ask several members what the organization claims to value and compare those claims with behavior and with changes over time.
3. **Basic underlying assumptions:** Investigate the taken-for-granted beliefs that guide perception and behavior. Useful questions concern the organization's orientation to time, what counts as an important time unit, and whether people are assumed to be trustworthy, neutral, or in need of tight control. These assumptions are usually unarticulated, treated as obvious, and therefore difficult to debate.

Schein's broader definition is useful as a test: culture is a pattern of assumptions learned by a group as it solves external-adaptation and internal-integration problems, judged valid because it has worked, and taught to newcomers as the right way to perceive, think, and feel. A change agent should distinguish a surface artifact that must adapt to growth from an underlying value that the organization wants to preserve.

### 3. Individual change equation

**When to use it:** Use it to predict support or resistance at the individual level, especially when leaders argue that a change is obviously good for the organization.

**How to use it:** Estimate each affected person's perceptions, not just the objective business case:

`Dissatisfaction * Benefits * Probability of success > Perceived cost`

Here, dissatisfaction is dissatisfaction with the status quo; benefits are the expected personal and organizational benefits; probability of success is the person's belief that the change will work; and cost includes effort, risk, lost status, worse work conditions, uncertainty, or job threat. If any of the first three factors is near zero, a large promised benefit may not produce action.

Build the case using both "head" and "heart" considerations. Data about cost, market position, profitability, or competitive advantage may establish the need, but people also assess effects on themselves, teammates, customers, and the community. Show why the status quo is inadequate, make benefits credible, demonstrate a path to success, discuss personal costs honestly, and create early evidence that the initiative can work. Do not confuse a benefit to the organization with a benefit to the individual who must do the work.

### 4. Homeostasis and force field analysis

**When to use it:** Use it when an organization appears stable, when support and resistance are being treated as personality problems, or when a change produces unexpected reactions. The organization is in a form of homeostasis: interdependent forces have settled into a relatively stable balance.

**How to use it:**

1. List driving forces and restraining forces. Include people, departments, norms, power patterns, rewards, skills, resources, formal systems, habits, customers, suppliers, competitors, standards, and regulations.
2. Estimate the strength and timing of each force. Distinguish immediate pressures, such as a quarterly target, from slower forces, such as morale or customer satisfaction.
3. Seek leverage rather than simply adding pressure. Strengthen a high-leverage driver, reduce a key restraint, remove an incompatible system, or convert a restraint into support. A small change in a central relationship or reward system may matter more than a large general communication campaign.
4. Test second-order effects. A short-term incentive may reduce visible resistance while encouraging unethical behavior, weakening commitment, or damaging the compensation system.

Strebel's change-arena view separates forces for and against change rather than assuming that every force is a direct opposite. Strong drivers with weak resistance support **continuous change**. Strong drivers and strong resistance create **breakpoint change**, in which the system remains stuck until pressures shift and then moves abruptly, often with upheaval. Weak drivers and weak resistance create **flip-flop or sporadic change**, where ambivalent participants can reverse a low-stakes change easily. Strong resistance with weak drivers creates a high-resistance arena requiring diagnosis before escalation.

### 5. Stakeholder analysis and stakeholder mapping

**When to use it:** Use it whenever the initiative depends on people outside the change team, crosses departments, affects customers or suppliers, or requires behavior that formal authority alone cannot produce.

**How to use it:**

1. Identify who can say yes or no, who is affected, who must change behavior, who influences those people, and who has resources or power to enable or block the initiative. Include customers, suppliers, communities, and government bodies when relevant.
2. Record each stakeholder's position, motives, power sources, likely response, current status-quo benefits, possible change benefits, and possible losses.
3. Draw the relationships. Use groupings, relationship strength, support or resistance, and arrows showing influence. Look for **central connectors**, **boundary spanners**, **information brokers**, and **peripheral specialists**. These network roles often reveal leverage that the formal chart hides.
4. Use Savage's threat/cooperation assessment. High threat and high cooperation call for collaboration; low threat and high cooperation call for involvement; high threat and low cooperation call for protection or defense; low threat and low cooperation call for monitoring.
5. Reassess the map after conversations and early actions. A map is a set of hypotheses, not a permanent verdict. Solicit feedback and check whether the planned tactics are likely to produce the desired stakeholder response.

### 6. Readiness, commitment, and the adoption continuum

**When to use it:** Use it to tailor engagement rather than treating all stakeholders as equally ready, equally informed, or equally committed.

**How to use it:** Track three separate dimensions:

- **Predisposition:** innovators or early adopters, early majority, late majority, laggards, or non-adopters.
- **Commitment to this change:** resistant, ambivalent, neutral, supportive, or committed. Another practical wording is "keep it from happening," "let it happen," "help it happen," and "make it happen."
- **Readiness to act:** awareness, interest, desire for action, and taking action.

Also distinguish understanding from commitment. A stakeholder can be a strong-consensus supporter, a blind devotee who supports without understanding, an informed skeptic who understands but is not yet committed, a weak-consensus participant, an informed opponent, or a poorly informed but highly hostile opponent. There is no single best cell at every moment: informed skeptics can improve design, while a confidential initiative may temporarily require limited questioning and disciplined execution.

Start with innovators and early adopters, while keeping later adopters informed to prevent unnecessary backlash. Match communication intensity to readiness: broad, impersonal education can create awareness; forums, benchmark data, pilots, and budget allocations can build interest; direct one-on-one persuasion is more appropriate as people consider action. The change agent's reputation, relationships, organizational history, and culture determine which tactic will be credible.

## Key Concepts

- **Informal organization:** Relationships and spontaneous processes layered onto formal structures, including informal leadership, communication, influence, norms, roles, and organizational culture.
- **Power:** The capacity to influence others to accept ideas or plans. Power itself is not inherently good or bad; its purpose and consequences matter.
- **Organizational politics:** Negotiation and the push-pull through which individuals and departments make decisions and allocate scarce resources amid divergent interests.
- **Power perception:** Others' belief that a person can influence events. Perceived power can determine behavior even when formal authority is unchanged.
- **Process and meaning power:** Influence over what gets discussed and decided, and over how people interpret events, language, symbols, and status signals.
- **Culture:** Learned, shared assumptions that have proved workable and are passed to newcomers as the correct way to perceive and act.
- **Resistance:** A preference not to pursue the proposed change. It may arise from information gaps, different perceptions, needs, beliefs, habits, power shifts, or incompatible formal and informal systems.
- **Homeostasis:** The relatively stable equilibrium produced by interdependent organizational forces.
- **Stakeholder management:** The deliberate influencing of critical participants after assessing their positions, motives, relationships, and power.
- **Readiness versus commitment:** General willingness to change is not the same as a person's position on this particular initiative. A late adopter may still become a strong supporter after evidence accumulates.

## Mental Models

- **The real organization has two overlays:** The formal chart shows authority and accountability; the informal network shows trust, information flow, sponsorship, vetoes, and meaning. Diagnose both before acting.
- **Power is authority multiplied by credibility and connection:** A title creates potential, but reputation, expertise, relationships, and others' perceptions determine whether that potential becomes influence.
- **Culture is past success made invisible:** Repeated solutions become assumptions, assumptions become norms, and norms are taught to newcomers. A change that challenges a once-effective solution may feel irrational to people who inherited it.
- **Every change has two business cases:** One explains why the organization should change; the other explains why each affected person should support the change. The second case cannot be skipped.
- **Resistance is a force and a source of information:** Resistance may signal a real cost, an unaddressed cultural assumption, an overlooked stakeholder, or an incompatible system. Reduce it, learn from it, or convert it instead of merely overpowering it.
- **Influence travels through people, not announcements:** Moving a stakeholder from awareness to action usually requires a path through trusted influencers, meaningful conversations, and evidence appropriate to that stakeholder's readiness.
- **Maps are hypotheses:** Force fields and stakeholder maps become useful when they are tested against conversations and revised as relationships, incentives, and perceptions change.

## Anti-patterns

- Treating organizational politics as inherently dirty and refusing to negotiate, build coalitions, or learn how decisions actually occur.
- Assuming that a formal title, budget, or reporting line automatically produces influence.
- Reading only the organization chart and missing central connectors, information brokers, informal leaders, or departments with veto power.
- Inferring culture from one visible artifact or changing artifacts while leaving the underlying assumption untouched.
- Starting with mission statements and ignoring the gap between espoused values and lived behavior.
- Announcing positive organizational outcomes while ignoring individual workload, status, job security, autonomy, or team consequences.
- Treating all resistance as ignorance or bad attitude instead of examining costs, history, habits, power loss, and system incompatibility.
- Increasing pressure on driving forces without reducing a central restraint, thereby producing avoidable backlash or breakpoint upheaval.
- Buying compliance with incentives without considering unethical behavior, damaged commitment, or unintended effects on the reward system.
- Mapping only internal stakeholders and overlooking customers, suppliers, communities, regulators, and other external sources of threat or cooperation.
- Using the same communication tactic for an unaware stakeholder, an informed skeptic, an early adopter, and a late adopter.
- Attempting to convert laggards before building credible support among innovators and early adopters.
- Confusing a formal inclusion program or stated value with a changed culture when informal decisions still reward the old assumptions.

## Worked Example (Study Depth)

### Patrick's promotion-review problem

At Millenial, ten executive directors on a marketing leadership council are reviewing candidates for two director promotions. Learning and Development administers the logistics, but the council's closed discussion strongly shapes the outcome. Alan Witherspoon receives enthusiastic sponsorship from his executive director and other leaders. Patrick, who has worked with Alan on a cross-functional team, has seen self-serving behavior, limited participation during pressure periods, and little openness to feedback. Alicia Conrad is also successful: she meets goals, manages a difficult client portfolio, supports her team, earns strong satisfaction scores, and generates repeat business. Yet the council raises doubts about her need for more coworker support and more experience, concerns Patrick has noticed being applied to female candidates. Millenial has a Male Ally Program intended to increase women's senior leadership representation, but Patrick is the only ally in the room who appears ready to advocate for his partner, Alicia. He expects social and professional consequences if he challenges the group.

### Diagnosis using the chapter's tools

- **Power:** The leadership council has process power because its private agenda and discussion shape promotion decisions. Its members also have positional power and network power through sponsorship relationships. Alan benefits from advocacy and shared affiliations. Learning and Development has formal involvement but less informal influence over the judgment that matters. Patrick has knowledge power from direct cross-functional experience and potential meaning power if he challenges the group's definition of leadership.
- **Culture:** The espoused value is inclusion through the Male Ally Program. The lived culture contains an older leadership image: the leader is expected to arrive as a heroic rescuer. That underlying assumption makes Alicia's collaborative, goal-achieving leadership easier to discount and turns an ambiguous concern such as "needs support" into a gendered evaluation standard. The closed-room practice is an artifact and process that can preserve the assumption by limiting challenge.
- **Stakeholders:** The council members can approve or block the immediate outcome; Alicia and other candidates are directly affected; Patrick is an informed, potentially supportive stakeholder with a personal risk; Learning and Development and Talent Management can influence the process; women in the organization and Male Ally participants have a stake in whether the initiative is credible. Influencers and allies should be mapped rather than assumed from program membership.
- **Force field:** Drivers include Alicia's documented results, the stated goal of increasing women in senior leadership, and the potential credibility of the ally program. Restraining forces include sponsorship bias, heroic leadership norms, limited meeting time, the privacy of the council, fear of peer displeasure, and the possibility that silence will normalize the current standard. Patrick's personal risk is itself part of the force field.

### A defensible change-agent response

Patrick should advocate using evidence and consistent criteria rather than making the discussion a personal accusation. He can ask the council to apply the same promotion standards to every candidate, distinguish observed performance from vague impressions, and document how Alicia's results map to the director role. He can use his direct experience to add information the network is missing, ask what evidence supports the concern about "support," and make the business and fairness implications explicit. Before or after the meeting, he can build a coalition with credible allies and engage Learning and Development or Talent Management around a more transparent review process.

This response uses knowledge power, coalition power, and meaning power while recognizing the council's positional and process power. It also moves the issue beyond whether Patrick is personally loyal to Alicia: the question is whether Millenial's formal inclusion commitment is supported by the informal decisions that control advancement. Speaking up may create peer friction or reduce Patrick's standing in the council. Saying nothing, however, leaves the informal standard unchallenged, weakens the ally program's credibility, and permits a potentially biased assessment to spread through the network. The case illustrates why change agents must evaluate their own incentives, risks, relationships, and willingness to use power rather than treating themselves as neutral observers.

## Key Takeaways

- Diagnose informal power before designing a change; identify both formal authority and perceived influence.
- Analyze individual power, departmental power, and resource, process, and meaning power separately.
- Read culture through artifacts, espoused values, and underlying assumptions; never assume that a mission statement describes lived culture.
- Build the change equation at the individual level. Organizational benefit does not guarantee individual support.
- Use force field analysis to identify leverage, not just to count supporters and opponents. Consider immediate, long-term, and unintended effects.
- Use stakeholder analysis to find decision makers, affected people, required behavior changes, influencers, coalitions, and external participants.
- Segment stakeholders by predisposition, commitment, understanding, and readiness. Move them from awareness to interest, desire, and action with appropriately intensive tactics.
- Treat resistance as diagnostic information and respect the history, norms, and power consequences that make it reasonable from the stakeholder's perspective.
- Remember that the change agent is a stakeholder. Personal motives, reputation, networks, and comfort with political action shape the intervention.
