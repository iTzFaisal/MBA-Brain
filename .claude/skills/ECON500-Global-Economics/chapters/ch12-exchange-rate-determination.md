# Chapter 12: Exchange Rate Determination

## Core Idea

In a market-determined system, currency values are set by supply and demand, but the causes of those shifts depend on the horizon. Long-run structural forces provide an equilibrium path, medium-run business cycles move a currency around it, and short-run asset flows and expectations can drive rapid movements and overshooting.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Market fundamentals and market expectations**: Use this as the master distinction. Fundamentals include productivity, inflation, real interest rates, preferences, investment profitability, trade policy, and policy settings; expectations incorporate news and traders' beliefs about future fundamentals and exchange rates.
  - **Long run**: Track relative price levels, productivity, preferences for domestic versus foreign goods, and trade barriers. Higher relative domestic prices or stronger import demand depreciate the domestic currency; higher relative productivity, export demand, or trade barriers appreciate it.
  - **Medium run**: Look for cyclical changes in economic activity, trade flows, and capital movements.
  - **Short run**: Focus on financial-asset positions, relative real returns, and expected exchange-rate changes. The exchange rate can move opposite to long-run fundamentals without being inconsistent with short-run determinants.

- **Law of one price**: For an identical tradable good, with negligible transport costs, no trade barriers, and competitive markets, converted prices should converge. Arbitrage buys where the good is cheap and sells where it is expensive. Use it as the microfoundation for PPP, not as a universal claim about nontradables such as housing or haircuts.

- **Purchasing-Power-Parity (PPP) theory**: Apply the law of one price to national price indexes. With S quoted as domestic currency per unit of foreign currency:
  `S1 = S0 * (Pdom,1/Pdom,0) / (Pfor,1/Pfor,0)`.
  A currency tends to depreciate by the excess of domestic inflation over foreign inflation. PPP is principally a long-run target, not a reliable day-to-day forecast.

- **Asset market approach**: Use for short-run exchange-rate movements. Investors compare domestic and foreign assets using relative real interest rates plus expected currency gains or losses. Higher domestic real returns or expected domestic appreciation attract capital, increase demand for the domestic currency, and appreciate it today.

- **Exchange rate overshooting**: A short-run appreciation or depreciation is larger than the eventual long-run response. The short-run supply and demand for currency are less elastic, while exchange rates adjust faster than sticky wages and other prices; later trade responses and price adjustment pull the rate toward its long-run path.

- **Forecasting exchange rates**: Combine three approaches according to the horizon. Judgmental forecasts interpret broad economic, political, technical, and psychological information; technical analysis extrapolates price patterns for short horizons; fundamental analysis uses economic relationships and econometric models for longer-run trends. No method eliminates policy shocks or market psychology.

## Key Concepts

- **Market fundamentals**: Economic variables that shift currency supply and demand.
- **Market expectations**: Beliefs about future fundamentals or the future exchange rate that can move today's rate.
- **Nominal interest rate**: The stated money return on an asset.
- **Real interest rate**: `nominal interest rate - inflation rate`; the more relevant cross-border return measure.
- **Law of one price**: The arbitrage-based prediction that identical tradable goods have one common-currency price.
- **Purchasing-power parity**: A relative-price theory linking inflation differentials to long-run currency changes.
- **Asset market approach**: A short-run model centered on international portfolio allocation.
- **Overvalued/undervalued currency**: A market rate above/below an estimated equilibrium benchmark such as PPP.
- **Safe-haven effect**: Capital demand for a currency because its assets are perceived as low risk, even at a lower return.
- **Diversification effect**: Portfolio rebalancing away from an already large currency exposure.

## Mental Models

- **Horizon filter**: Before explaining a currency movement, classify it as short, medium, or long run; then select asset, cyclical, or structural variables accordingly.
- **Return-plus-currency-gain test**: Compare foreign assets using interest income plus the expected exchange-rate change. A high nominal yield is unattractive if expected depreciation cancels it.
- **Long-run magnet**: Treat the structural equilibrium path as an anchor. Short-run news can push the currency away, but trade flows and slower price adjustment tend to pull it back.
- **Currency-demand translation**: Convert each event into a demand/supply shift. More imports raise demand for foreign currency; more exports supply foreign currency and support the domestic currency.

## Anti-patterns

- **Using PPP as a short-run trading signal**: Investment flows, expectations, nontradables, transaction costs, and government restrictions can dominate for months or years.
- **Reading nominal rates mechanically**: A higher nominal rate caused by higher inflation may leave the real return unchanged or lower and can accompany depreciation.
- **Explaining every movement with one determinant**: Interest rates, fundamentals, sentiment, safe-haven demand, and policy can push in opposite directions; state the horizon and hold other factors constant only as an analytical device.
- **Assuming a chart pattern persists**: Technical patterns may last only days or weeks and require rapid execution.
- **Taking leveraged currency bets without stress testing**: The Comercial Mexicana case shows how threshold-triggered contracts and a sudden peso depreciation can turn an expected gain into bankruptcy-scale losses.

## Worked Example

Suppose the current rate is `$1.50 per pound`, and UK investors expect the dollar to appreciate to `$1.45 per pound` in three months. An investor converts `£100,000` into `$150,000` and buys U.S. Treasury bills (ignoring interest for clarity). At maturity, converting at `$1.45 per pound` yields about `£103,448`, an expected foreign-exchange gain of `£3,448` before interest. That expectation increases UK demand for dollar assets, shifts the supply of pounds toward dollars, and causes the dollar to appreciate today. The expectation is therefore partly self-fulfilling. The expectation itself may originate in long-run forces such as higher U.S. productivity, lower U.S. prices, stronger U.S. exports, or higher U.S. trade barriers.

## Key Takeaways

1. Match the determinant to the horizon: assets and expectations short term, cycles medium term, trade and structural fundamentals long term.
2. Long-run appreciation follows relatively low inflation, high productivity, strong export demand, or higher trade barriers, other things equal.
3. PPP predicts adjustment to relative inflation, but is a long-run benchmark with important real-world exceptions.
4. International investors care about real returns and expected currency movements, not nominal interest rates alone.
5. Expectations can move today's exchange rate; flexible exchange rates may overshoot while other prices remain sticky.
6. Forecasting is strongest when fundamental, technical, and judgmental evidence is combined and its limits are made explicit.

## Connects To

- **Balance-of-payments approach**: Trade in goods, services, and investment funds explains how sustained external spending pressures a currency toward depreciation or appreciation.
- **Monetary and fiscal policy**: Policy announcements change interest rates, inflation expectations, money growth, and expected future exchange rates immediately.
- **International GDP comparisons**: PPP conversion controls for cross-country price-level differences that market exchange rates alone can misrepresent.
- **Foreign-exchange risk management**: Exchange-rate forecasts and the Comercial Mexicana example connect determination theory to hedging, contract design, and exposure limits.
