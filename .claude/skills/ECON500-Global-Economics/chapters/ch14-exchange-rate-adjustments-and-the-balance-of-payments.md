# Chapter 14: Exchange Rate Adjustments and the Balance-of-Payments

## Core Idea
Exchange-rate changes can correct external imbalance by changing relative costs and prices, but depreciation is not an automatic cure for a trade deficit. Its result depends on firms' currency exposure, demand elasticities, adjustment lags, price pass-through, domestic spending, productive capacity, and money-market effects.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Effects of Exchange Rate Changes on Costs and Prices**: Use this framework to assess firm-level competitiveness. An appreciation raises the foreign-currency cost of inputs priced in the home currency; a depreciation lowers it. For an appreciation, foreign sourcing dampens the change in foreign-currency total cost and lowers the home-currency cost of foreign-denominated inputs. The larger the foreign-input share, the more the firm is naturally hedged against that appreciation.
- **Elasticity Approach**: Use it to predict the price-and-quantity effect on the trade balance. Depreciation raises the home-currency price of imports and lowers export prices in foreign currency; quantities respond according to import and export demand elasticities.
  - **Marshall-Lerner condition**: Depreciation improves the trade balance when `|eta_M| + |eta_X| > 1`, worsens it when the sum is below 1, and leaves it unchanged at 1. The rule works best as a long-run, ceteris paribus guide: it assumes sellers' own-currency prices do not offset the exchange-rate movement and is clearest when the initial trade balance is near equilibrium.
- **J-curve effect**: Use it to separate short-run from long-run results. A depreciation can first worsen the balance because import prices rise while contractual quantities are fixed; as buyers and producers adjust, import volume falls and export volume rises, producing later improvement if elasticities are favorable.
- **Exchange Rate Pass-Through**: Use it to ask how much of an exchange-rate movement reaches import and export prices. Complete pass-through is proportional; partial pass-through is less than proportional. Invoicing currency, firms' willingness to protect market share through profit margins, and nontraded distribution costs all weaken transmission.
- **Absorption Approach**: Use it to connect the trade balance to domestic production and spending. With `Y = C + I + G + (X - M)`, define absorption as `A = C + I + G` and the trade balance as `B = X - M`; then `B = Y - A`. Depreciation improves `B` only if output rises relative to absorption: unused capacity permits expansion, while full employment requires reduced domestic absorption.
- **Monetary Approach**: Use it to include money balances, reserves, and capital movements. Depreciation raises domestic prices and transaction-money demand. If domestic money supply does not immediately meet that demand, funds flow in, creating a temporary payments surplus and reserve accumulation; the resulting money-supply increase eventually raises spending and removes the surplus. Long run, the main effect is a higher domestic price level, not a permanent real improvement.

## Key Concepts
- **Appreciation**: A currency gains exchange value; domestic firms' home-currency costs are unchanged, but their foreign-currency prices tend to rise.
- **Depreciation**: A currency loses exchange value through market forces; **devaluation** is the comparable policy action under a fixed-rate system.
- **Foreign sourcing**: Purchasing inputs priced in foreign currency; it cushions an appreciation's foreign-currency cost shock but makes home-currency input costs more sensitive in the opposite direction during depreciation.
- **Trade balance**: Export receipts minus import expenditures, `X - M`, measured in a common currency.
- **Demand elasticity**: The percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price; values above 1 are elastic.
- **Exchange-rate pass-through**: The percentage change in trade prices caused by a percentage change in the exchange rate.
- **Absorption**: Domestic consumption, investment, and government spending, `C + I + G`.
- **Adjustment lags**: Recognition, decision, delivery, replacement, and production delays between a currency movement and changed trade quantities.

## Mental Models
- **Currency shock as a cost-mix shock**: Do not ask only whether the currency appreciated. Map every input and revenue stream to its invoicing currency, then compare the firm's exposure on both sides.
- **Elasticity gate**: A cheaper export price matters only if foreign demand responds enough, and an expensive import matters only if domestic demand contracts enough. Check the elasticity sum before predicting a better trade balance.
- **J-curve clock**: Price changes happen first; contracts, inventories, orders, delivery, and production adjust later. A worsening early balance does not disprove a later improvement.
- **Three-lens diagnosis**: Evaluate depreciation through prices (elasticity), spending versus output (absorption), and money balances/reserves (monetary approach). The lenses are complementary, not competing explanations.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating depreciation as a guaranteed deficit reducer**: Low elasticities, partial pass-through, or a large initial deficit can make import spending rise more than export receipts.
- **Assuming complete pass-through**: Exporters may invoice in the buyer's currency, accept lower margins, defend market share, or face large domestic distribution costs.
- **Ignoring capacity and absorption**: A fully employed economy cannot expand output without freeing resources; depreciation may require politically costly restrictive fiscal or monetary policy.
- **Reading the first trade response as the final one**: Prior commitments and adjustment lags can create the J-curve pattern.

## Worked Example
Suppose the UK pound depreciates 10 percent. Let UK import-demand elasticity be 2.5 and foreign demand elasticity for UK exports be 1.5. The Marshall-Lerner sum is `2.5 + 1.5 = 4.0`, so the long-run prediction is improvement. Holding foreign-currency import prices fixed, the pound price of imports rises 10 percent and import quantity falls about 25 percent. Import outpayments therefore fall approximately 15 percent (`+10 - 25`). The pound price received by UK exporters is initially unchanged, but their dollar price falls 10 percent; foreign quantity demanded rises about 15 percent, so export receipts in pounds rise about 15 percent. Both effects strengthen the UK trade balance. If elasticities instead were 0.2 and 0.1, import spending would rise about 8 percent while export receipts rose only about 1 percent, worsening the deficit.

## Key Takeaways
1. Exchange-rate effects depend on the currency denomination of both inputs and outputs.
2. Depreciation improves trade only when quantity responses are sufficiently elastic: `|eta_M| + |eta_X| > 1`.
3. The J-curve makes timing essential: short-run deterioration can precede long-run improvement.
4. Partial pass-through limits the relative-price incentive behind depreciation.
5. A trade improvement requires output to rise relative to absorption, or absorption to fall.
6. Monetary effects can make the real gains from depreciation temporary and leave mainly higher prices over time.
