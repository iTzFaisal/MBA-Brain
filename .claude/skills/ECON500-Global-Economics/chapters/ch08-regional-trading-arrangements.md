# Chapter 8: Regional Trading Arrangements

## Core Idea
Regional integration lowers trade barriers inside a selected group, but usually discriminates against outsiders. Its welfare effect is not automatically beneficial: evaluate the static balance of trade creation versus trade diversion, add dynamic gains from larger and more competitive markets, and account for the policy sovereignty surrendered as integration deepens.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Regional integration versus multilateralism (building blocks or stumbling blocks)**: Use this when judging whether a regional bloc supports the WTO's nondiscriminatory liberalization. A bloc can be a **building block** when it is open and inclusive: deeper common interests, likely enlargement, and worker movement toward comparative-advantage industries can create self-reinforcing support for wider liberalization. It becomes a **stumbling block** when members protect special preferences, divert trade, or lose incentives to negotiate globally.
- **Stages of economic integration**: Use the ladder to identify what is liberalized and what sovereignty is transferred. (1) A **free trade area** removes internal tariff and nontariff barriers while each member keeps its own external policy. (2) A **customs union** adds an identical common external trade policy. (3) A **common market** adds free movement of labor and capital. (4) An **economic union** harmonizes social, tax, and fiscal policies through a supranational institution. (5) A **monetary union** adds unified monetary policy and a common currency.
- **Static welfare effects of economic integration**: Apply to a customs union by comparing the efficiency gained when members replace costly domestic production with partner imports against the efficiency lost when partner imports replace cheaper outsider imports.
- **Dynamic effects of economic integration**: Look beyond one-time price and quantity changes. Market enlargement can deliver economies of scale, specialization, stronger competition, investment, innovation, and technology transfer, raising the long-run growth rate.
- **Optimum currency area theory**: Use before recommending a common currency. Compare lower transaction costs, price transparency, investor certainty, and less exchange-rate risk with the loss of independent monetary and exchange-rate policy. Success is more likely with similar cycles and structures, labor mobility, flexible prices and wages, and stabilizing fiscal transfers.
- **Maastricht convergence criteria**: Treat monetary union as requiring policy convergence, not merely a fixed exchange rate. The chapter's criteria are inflation no more than 1.5 percentage points above the three lowest-inflation members; long-term interest rates no more than 2 points above the relevant average; at least two years of exchange-rate stability without devaluation; budget deficit no more than 3% of GDP; and public debt no more than 60% of GDP.

## Key Concepts
- **Economic integration**: Eliminating restrictions on international trade, payments, and factor mobility so national economies operate more as a regional unit.
- **Free trade area**: Internal barriers are removed, but members retain independent policies toward nonmembers.
- **Customs union**: Internal barriers are removed and members impose the same external restrictions.
- **Common market**: A customs union that also permits cross-border movement of production factors.
- **Economic union / monetary union**: An economic union harmonizes major economic policies; a monetary union unifies currency and monetary authority.
- **Trade creation effect**: Lower-cost partner imports replace inefficient domestic production, producing consumption and production gains.
- **Trade diversion effect**: Higher-cost partner imports replace lower-cost nonmember imports, reducing world efficiency.
- **Static and dynamic effects**: Static effects are immediate resource and consumer-welfare changes; dynamic effects alter scale, competition, investment, productivity, and growth.
- **Common agricultural policy (CAP) and variable levies**: EU farm support uses price supports, payments, output controls, import levies, and export subsidies; a variable levy rises when the world price falls to preserve the internal support price.
- **Optimum currency area**: A region for which one currency is economically preferable to separate currencies because adjustment mechanisms can handle asymmetric shocks.

## Mental Models
- **Net integration test**: `net static gain = trade creation - trade diversion`; for the customs-union diagram, gains occur when `a + b > c`. Never infer welfare from higher internal trade alone.
- **Integration ladder**: Every step removes another border friction, but also narrows national policy discretion. Ask what new efficiency is purchased and which instrument is surrendered.
- **Asymmetric-shock test**: If a member cannot devalue or change interest rates, ask whether labor can move, wages/prices can adjust, or fiscal transfers can cushion the shock. If none works, monetary integration is fragile.
- **Unit-cost relocation test**: Compare productivity-adjusted labor cost, not wage levels alone. A low-wage location need not be the low-cost location if its productivity, infrastructure, legal stability, or technology is weaker.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating regional free trade as automatically global-welfare improving**: A bloc may substitute a high-cost member for a low-cost outsider.
- **Calling a market common because tariffs disappeared**: National standards, procurement rules, border procedures, and business laws can continue to block scale and investment.
- **Adopting a currency before building adjustment capacity**: A shared monetary policy can fit one economy and destabilize another; fiscal discipline and structural flexibility matter.
- **Protecting high-cost agriculture without counting the spillovers**: Support prices and variable levies encourage inefficient output, restrict efficient imports, create surpluses, and can require export subsidies.
- **Measuring an agreement only by aggregate gains or job losses**: Regional trade redistributes across sectors. Include consumer savings, exporting industries, import-using firms, displaced workers, and adjustment costs.

## Worked Example
Assume small Luxembourg can buy grain from the United States for $3.00 per bushel and Germany for $3.25. Before a customs union, a $0.50 tariff makes the U.S. landed price $3.50; Luxembourg consumes 23 bushels, produces 1, and imports 22 from the United States. After Luxembourg removes its tariff on Germany but keeps it against the United States, Germany becomes the supplier at $3.25. Consumption rises to 26 (the chapter's figure shows a three-bushel consumption gain), while domestic output falls by three; these are the consumption and production parts of **trade creation**, represented by areas `b` and `a`. Ten imported bushels also switch from the more efficient United States to the higher-cost Germany: that is **trade diversion**, represented by `c`. The union's static result is positive only if `a + b > c`; its final assessment should then add dynamic scale, competition, and investment effects.

## Key Takeaways
1. Regional arrangements are discriminatory exceptions to WTO nondiscrimination; openness determines whether they reinforce or obstruct multilateralism.
2. The integration sequence runs from free trade area to customs union, common market, economic union, and monetary union; each deeper stage transfers more policy authority.
3. Customs-union welfare depends on trade creation versus trade diversion, not on the volume of intra-bloc trade.
4. Larger markets can produce persistent gains through scale economies, competition, innovation, investment, and technology diffusion.
5. The EU illustrates both deep integration and its limits: CAP protection can divert trade, while regulatory differences can keep a common market incomplete.
6. The euro removes conversion and exchange-rate costs but removes national monetary and exchange-rate adjustment; NAFTA shows that aggregate gains can coexist with concentrated sectoral losses and modest economy-wide effects for a large member.

## Connects To
- **WTO and multilateralism**: Regional preferences must be assessed against nondiscriminatory global liberalization.
- **Comparative advantage and tariff analysis**: Trade creation/diversion extends the small-country tariff model to preferential blocs.
- **Exchange rates and macroeconomic policy**: Optimum currency area theory explains the tradeoff between monetary integration and shock-adjustment tools.
- **Trade adjustment and political economy**: Aggregate efficiency gains do not eliminate displacement, lobbying, or the need for worker assistance.
