# Chapter 9: International Factor Movements and Multinational Enterprises

## Core Idea
International factor movements are driven by the same force as trade: resources move from places where their productivity and returns are low to places where they are high, provided the gain exceeds the cost and risk of moving. Multinational enterprises (MNEs) reallocate capital, technology, and management through foreign direct investment (FDI); migration reallocates labor. These movements can raise world output while redistributing income and creating conflicts over employment, sovereignty, taxation, and adjustment.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Comparative-advantage principle applied to the MNE**
  - When to use: Decide where a good or production stage should be located.
  - How: Compare factor costs, productivity, technology, managerial know-how, market access, and risk. Production tends to locate in the lower-cost country. The MNE explanation emphasizes movement of factor inputs; conventional trade emphasizes movement of finished goods.
- **Motives for Foreign Direct Investment**
  - When to use: Explain or screen a proposed overseas investment.
  - How: Start with expected profit net of risk, then test demand and competitive conditions, raw-material access, labor productivity and cost, transportation, tariffs, investment rules, and host-country incentives. “Cheap labor” lowers cost only when its lower productivity does not offset the wage difference.
- **Direct Exporting versus Foreign Direct Investment/Licensing**
  - When to use: Supply a foreign market after demand is established.
  - How: Export when foreign demand is too small for efficient local scale and transport/distribution plus trade barriers cost less than duplicating production. Choose local production or licensing when demand is large enough to realize local scale or barriers make exporting unattractive.
- **Foreign Direct Investment versus Licensing**
  - When to use: Foreign production is preferred, but ownership form is undecided.
  - How: License when a capable local firm can adapt the technology and the market is too small to cover the parent’s coordination and sunk costs. Invest in a subsidiary when its lower variable costs, capital advantage, control, or larger market outweigh fixed costs. Greater capital intensity, cheaper parent capital, or lower fixed costs reduce the output threshold for FDI.
- **Country Risk Analysis**
  - When to use: Decide whether an otherwise profitable foreign project is tolerable.
  - How: Evaluate political risk (stability, corruption, conflict), financial risk (debt, default, exchange-rate stability), and economic risk (growth, income, inflation). The chapter’s composite weighting is 50% political, 25% financial, and 25% economic; higher scores mean lower risk. Use the composite as a screen, then tailor the decision to the firm’s exposure. The cited guide treats 80-100 as low, 50-79 as moderate, and 0-49 as high risk.
- **International Joint-Venture Welfare Effects**
  - When to use: Assess a joint venture between potential competitors or across borders.
  - How: Weigh the market-power effect against the cost-reduction effect. A monopoly-like JV raises price and reduces output, creating deadweight loss. New capacity, access to markets, or genuine productivity and technology gains can offset it. Wage concessions or other monetary transfers redistribute income but do not by themselves increase national welfare.
- **The Effects of Migration**
  - When to use: Analyze open labor mobility between countries.
  - How: Treat the value of marginal product (VMP) of labor as the labor-demand curve: VMP_L = product price x marginal product of labor, and competitive hiring continues until wage = VMP_L. A wage gap draws workers toward the higher-VMP country, lowering its wage and raising the origin country’s wage until movement costs or restrictions stop the process. World output rises when labor moves to more productive uses, but factor incomes change unevenly.

## Key Concepts
- **Multinational enterprise (MNE)**: A firm with cross-border operations, often multinational ownership and management, a distant planning center, and substantial foreign sales.
- **Foreign direct investment (FDI)**: Overseas investment that creates or acquires controlling productive capacity, including a controlling stock interest, new facilities, expansion funding, or reinvested affiliate earnings.
- **Vertical integration**: Foreign subsidiaries produce inputs (backward) or distribute finished goods (forward) within an internationally specialized chain.
- **Horizontal integration**: A parent duplicates production in the host country to serve its market, adapt to local preferences, or bypass tariffs.
- **Conglomerate integration**: An MNE enters unrelated foreign businesses to diversify opportunities and risk.
- **International joint venture**: A new firm combining the assets and skills of two or more companies, sometimes with a local or government partner.
- **Technology transfer**: Movement of production knowledge and skills through investment, personal contact, demonstration, and competitive pressure.
- **Transfer pricing**: Internal prices on transactions between a parent and affiliates; they allocate reported profit and can shift tax burdens.
- **Labor mobility and migration**: Cross-border movement of workers in response to economic returns and noneconomic forces such as war, politics, or religion.
- **Brain drain**: Emigration of highly educated or skilled workers from developing economies, potentially limiting their growth capacity.

## Mental Models
- **Factor arbitrage**: A return gap is a signal, not a guarantee. Move capital or labor only when the expected productivity gain exceeds transport, relocation, political, psychological, and coordination costs.
- **Scale-versus-distance test**: Compare the cost of one centralized plant plus shipping with the cost of duplicating production abroad. Small markets favor exports; sufficiently large markets favor local production or licensing.
- **Aggregate gain is not universal gain**: FDI and migration can expand total output while hurting a specific factor, region, or industry. Separate the size of the pie from its distribution.
- **Risk is exposure-specific**: A country’s composite rating can hide the risk relevant to a particular business. Examine the component risk that threatens the project rather than accepting a single national score.

## Anti-patterns
- **Equating low wages with low costs**: Ignore productivity and the apparent labor advantage may disappear.
- **Treating every capital outflow as a balance-of-payments loss**: FDI may later generate exports, affiliate purchases, royalties, dividends, and other income inflows.
- **Assuming a JV’s lower price or cost automatically raises welfare**: Separate real resource savings from transfers and subtract the monopoly deadweight loss.
- **Using a country risk score as a universal verdict**: Risk is defined by the user’s exposure; a tourist, lender, and factory owner face different threats.
- **Using arbitrary affiliate prices**: Transfer prices should follow an arm’s-length standard tied to actual costs and activities.

## Worked Example
Anheuser-Busch must supply Canadian Budweiser demand. Its Wisconsin brewery already makes 200 cases for the U.S. market. If Canada demands 100 cases, combined output of 300 cases lowers average production cost to $6 per case. A small Canadian brewery or subsidiary would produce at $11, so Wisconsin saves $5 per case. Export if transport and distribution cost less than $5. If Canadian demand rises to 400 cases, local production can reach the efficient $6 cost and avoids shipping, so licensing or an A-B subsidiary is preferred. Once local production is selected, licensing is favored below the output at which the subsidiary’s fixed coordination and market-entry costs are overcome (illustrated at 400 cases); above that threshold, FDI’s variable-cost advantage justifies ownership.

## Key Takeaways
1. MNEs operationalize comparative advantage by moving capital, technology, and management rather than only shipping goods.
2. FDI decisions are profit calculations shaped by demand, scale, productivity, transport, policy barriers, and risk.
3. Exporting, licensing, and ownership are alternative ways to serve the same foreign demand; market size and fixed costs determine the break point.
4. Joint ventures and MNEs can create capacity, technology, jobs, and exports, but can also create market power, tax conflict, political influence, and short-run employment losses.
5. Transfer pricing and national sovereignty concerns arise because an integrated MNE can coordinate transactions and resources across jurisdictions.
6. Migration increases world output when workers move to higher-productivity uses, but host-country labor, origin-country capital, and other groups experience different gains and losses.

## Connects To
- **Comparative advantage and trade substitution**: Factor movement can substitute for trade in capital-intensive or labor-intensive goods.
- **Balance of payments**: FDI has short-run capital-flow effects and longer-run effects through exports and investment income.
- **Labor-market incidence**: Use VMP and factor complementarity to distinguish workers who compete with migrants from those who work alongside them.
- **Welfare and market power**: Evaluate consumer surplus, producer surplus, productivity, and deadweight loss together.
- **International taxation and governance**: Transfer pricing, tax shifting, and MNE political influence expose the limits of purely national policy.
