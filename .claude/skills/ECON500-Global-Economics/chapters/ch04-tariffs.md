# Chapter 4: Tariffs

## Core Idea
Tariffs protect selected domestic producers by taxing imports, but they also raise consumer and input prices, distort production and consumption, and can damage exporters. The correct analysis separates transfers (to producers and government) from genuine efficiency losses, then asks whether a large importing country can obtain a terms-of-trade gain and whether that gain survives retaliation.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Effective Rate of Protection**: Use when a domestic industry imports components or raw materials; the posted tariff on the finished good is not enough. Calculate the percentage increase in domestic value added made possible by tariffs on both output and inputs:
  `e = (n - a*b)/(1-a)`
  where `n` is the final-good nominal tariff, `a` is imported input value/final-good value, and `b` is the input tariff. A low input tariff relative to the final-good tariff can make effective protection several times the nominal rate; a higher input tariff reduces it and can make it negative.
- **Tariff Escalation**: Use to diagnose a tariff structure that taxes processed goods more heavily than primary inputs. Low or zero duties on logs or raw materials plus higher duties on plywood, food, or manufactured goods protects domestic processing while creating an entry barrier for developing-country value-added exports.
- **Small-Nation Model**: Use when the importing country is a price taker. The world price stays fixed, so the tariff raises the domestic price by the full duty. Production rises, consumption falls, imports contract, and national welfare falls by the protective effect plus the consumption effect.
- **Large-Nation Model and the Optimum Tariff**: Use when the importer is large enough to reduce the foreign export price. Part of the tariff is then borne by foreign suppliers as a terms-of-trade gain. An optimum tariff maximizes `terms-of-trade gain - protective loss - consumption loss`, but it is a beggar-thy-neighbor policy vulnerable to retaliation.
- **Supply and Demand View of Protectionism**: Use to explain policy choice rather than just market effects. The supply of protection rises with social adjustment costs, producer political importance, and public sympathy; industry demand rises with comparative disadvantage, import penetration, production concentration, and low export dependence.

## Key Concepts
- **Tariff**: A tax levied when a product crosses a national boundary; an import tariff is most common.
- **Specific tariff**: Fixed money amount per physical unit; easy to administer but its proportional protection falls as import prices rise.
- **Ad valorem tariff**: Fixed percentage of import value; preserves proportional protection but requires contentious customs valuation.
- **Compound tariff**: Specific duty plus ad valorem duty, often used when both raw-material and finished-product protection are intended.
- **Nominal tariff rate**: Published rate applied to the imported finished product.
- **Consumer surplus / producer surplus**: Gains respectively above what buyers pay and above the minimum variable cost producers require.
- **Deadweight loss**: The protective and consumption effects that are not transferred to another domestic claimant.
- **Revenue, redistributive, protective, consumption, and terms-of-trade effects**: The five lenses for decomposing a tariff's welfare impact.
- **Tariff avoidance vs. tariff evasion**: Legal use of classifications, processing rules, or locations to reduce duties versus illegal smuggling or falsification.

## Mental Models
- **Follow the value added, not the headline rate.** Trace every imported input before judging who is protected or taxed.
- **Small country: tariff burden stays home. Large country: exporter may share it.** Market power determines whether terms of trade can offset efficiency losses.
- **Protection is a tax wedge with organized beneficiaries and dispersed payers.** Producers receive concentrated gains; consumers and downstream firms absorb less visible costs.
- **Trade policy is general equilibrium.** A protected input industry can undermine downstream producers, exports, employment elsewhere, and real incomes.

## Anti-patterns
- **Equating a tariff with net job creation**: protected jobs may be offset by losses in importing, downstream, and export industries.
- **Calling low foreign wages unfair by themselves**: compare unit labor cost, `wage / productivity`, not wage rates alone.
- **Treating retaliation as an afterthought**: an optimum tariff's foreign loss creates an incentive for counter-tariffs and a trade war.
- **Confusing legal avoidance with evasion**: exploiting a lawful tariff category is not equivalent to smuggling or falsifying origin documents.
- **Assuming infant-industry protection self-terminates**: political beneficiaries can make temporary tariffs permanent; a subsidy may achieve learning goals with less consumption distortion.

## Worked Example
Suppose a foreign desktop costs $500, of which imported components account for $400 (80% of the final value). Under free trade, domestic assembly value added is $100. A 10% tariff on finished desktops raises the import price to $550, while components enter duty free. Dell can now spend up to $150 on assembly and remain competitive, a 50% increase in value added: `(150 - 100)/100 = 50%`. The formula confirms `e = (.10 - .80*0)/(.20) = .50`. If components also face a 5% tariff, `e = (.10 - .80*.05)/(.20) = .30`: the same 10% output tariff gives only 30% effective protection. The lesson is that tariff policy can protect final assembly while taxing its input supply chain.

## Key Takeaways
1. Choose the tariff measure deliberately: specific, ad valorem, and compound duties trade administrative simplicity against stable proportional protection and valuation accuracy.
2. Compute effective protection whenever imported inputs exist; nominal rates can badly overstate or understate the incentive to produce domestically.
3. In a small nation, tariff gains to producers and government are transfers; protective and consumption effects are real national losses.
4. A large nation may extract a terms-of-trade gain, but only if it exceeds the two deadweight losses, and retaliation can erase gains for everyone.
5. Tariffs burden exporters through imported inputs, higher wages and living costs, and reduced foreign purchasing power.
6. Protectionist arguments about jobs, cheap labor, fairness, living standards, infant industries, and security require a national-welfare test, not only an industry-welfare test.

## Connects To
- **Comparative advantage and specialization**: tariffs move production away from lower-cost world suppliers and toward protected domestic uses.
- **Factor-endowment theory**: trade changes demand for factors; wage differences must be interpreted alongside productivity and unit labor costs.
- **Trade liberalization and retaliation**: negotiated tariff reduction addresses the beggar-thy-neighbor cycle and restores gains from expanded trade.
- **Nontariff barriers and political economy**: the same concentrated-benefit, dispersed-cost logic helps explain quotas, safeguards, and other protectionist instruments.
