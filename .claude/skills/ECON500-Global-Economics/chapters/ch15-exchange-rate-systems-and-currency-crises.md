# Chapter 15: Exchange Rate Systems and Currency Crises

## Core Idea
An exchange-rate regime is a choice about where adjustment occurs: through the currency price, domestic monetary policy, capital restrictions, or painful internal changes. No regime is universally best; the appropriate choice depends on openness, inflation differences, labor-market flexibility, financial development, policy credibility, capital mobility, and access to fiscal stabilization. A credible regime still needs sound fiscal and banking institutions.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Impossible trinity**: A country can sustainably combine only two of three goals: free capital flows, a fixed exchange rate, and an independent monetary policy. Use it before choosing a regime. With free capital and a peg, interest-rate differences trigger flows that the central bank must offset, surrendering monetary autonomy; with free capital and monetary independence, let the currency float; with a peg and monetary independence, restrict capital movement.
- **Fixed exchange rate system**: Use a par value when price certainty, an external nominal anchor, or confidence is more valuable than monetary flexibility. Define an official rate and defend it through an exchange stabilization fund. A temporary excess demand for the home currency is met by supplying it and buying foreign currency; excess supply is bought with foreign reserves. Persistent disequilibrium eventually requires **devaluation** (legal depreciation to correct a deficit) or **revaluation** (legal appreciation to correct a surplus).
- **Bretton Woods system of adjustable pegged exchange rates**: Combine near-fixed rates for trade with an escape valve for fundamental disequilibrium. Defend the band first with fiscal and monetary adjustment, then re-peg when internal correction would cause severe inflation or unemployment. Delayed, politically difficult, and oversized re-peggings created one-way speculative bets and helped end the system.
- **Managed floating system**: Let supply and demand determine long-run currency movements while intervening against disruptive short-run swings. **Leaning against the wind** means buying or selling currency to damp volatility, not defending a permanent target. A target exchange rate may be revised using inflation, reserve, and balance-of-payments indicators, but it is difficult to estimate reliably.
- **Crawling peg**: Use small, frequent par-value changes, often tied to price or inflation indicators, when high inflation makes a rigid peg unsustainable. It spreads adjustment over time and avoids the large, discontinuous corrections of an adjustable peg, but can still preserve expectations of further depreciation.
- **Currency board and dollarization**: Use a currency board when credibility and monetary discipline outweigh discretionary policy. It issues domestic currency only against at least 100% foreign-reserve backing and cannot finance government or banks. Full dollarization goes further by replacing the national currency, removing currency risk and inflationary money creation but also surrendering monetary policy, lender-of-last-resort capacity, and seigniorage.

## Key Concepts
- **Par value / official exchange rate**: The legally defined value of a currency under a fixed regime; the market rate is defended around it.
- **Key currency**: A widely traded, stable, internationally accepted currency used as an anchor.
- **Currency basket / SDR**: A weighted group of currencies used to average partner-country shocks rather than importing the volatility of one currency.
- **Appreciation and depreciation**: Market-driven increases or decreases in currency value; **revaluation** and **devaluation** are official changes to a fixed par value.
- **Clean float / dirty float**: A clean float leaves adjustment to markets; a dirty float uses intervention that prevents full market adjustment.
- **Fundamental disequilibrium**: A persistent mismatch between the official rate and underlying income, price, productivity, or trade conditions that makes defense prohibitively costly.
- **Currency crisis / speculative attack**: Heavy selling pressure against a weak currency, visible in reserve losses, forward-market depreciation, or flight into foreign currency and real assets.
- **Capital controls**: Government barriers to cross-border asset transactions; they buy policy room but invite evasion, distort allocation, and may weaken confidence.
- **Seigniorage**: Income from issuing money; dollarization removes the issuing country's monetary-source income.

## Mental Models
- **Regime as a constraint triangle**: Before recommending policy, identify which two corners of the impossible trinity the country is protecting and which corner it must forgo.
- **Short run versus long run**: Intervention can smooth a temporary shock; it cannot permanently offset a structural shift in demand, inflation, or productivity without accumulating imbalances.
- **Reserve-loss feedback loop**: Doubts about a peg cause currency sales, which drain reserves, making devaluation more likely and validating further sales.
- **Credibility-flexibility trade-off**: A currency board or dollarization buys credibility by removing discretion; a float buys flexibility by accepting exchange-rate volatility.

## Anti-patterns
- **Defending an overvalued peg indefinitely**: Reserve depletion and high interest rates eventually impose greater costs than an orderly adjustment.
- **Treating a peg as a substitute for fiscal discipline**: Borrowing or money creation can undermine the anchor and make the promised rate noncredible.
- **Using monetary intervention to cure a structural trade deficit**: Expansion may limit appreciation temporarily, but eventual domestic price increases erase competitiveness gains.
- **Assuming capital controls end a crisis**: Controls can be evaded, create corruption and arbitrage, and postpone needed banking or fiscal reform.
- **Calling every weak currency manipulation**: Distinguish a currency effect that is a byproduct of domestic stabilization from deliberate intervention aimed at exporting demand.

## Worked Example
Suppose the dollar-pound market initially clears at $2.80 per pound. A temporary rise in UK interest rates increases U.S. demand for pounds; at the peg, demand exceeds supply by $40 billion. To preserve $2.80, the U.S. stabilization fund supplies pounds and receives the excess dollars, shifting pound supply outward until the market clears. When UK rates normalize, intervention stops. If instead the demand pressure reflects a chronic deficit, repeated support drains reserves. The country must then choose costly internal contraction, restrict capital, or reset the par value through devaluation. If investors anticipate that choice, their sales can accelerate the reserve drain into a speculative attack.

## Key Takeaways
1. Exchange-rate choice is circumstance-dependent; small, open economies may value stability, while economies with inflation or rigid wages need flexibility.
2. Free capital flows make the impossible trinity binding: a peg removes independent monetary policy.
3. Fixed regimes exchange volatility for reserve requirements and crisis vulnerability; floats exchange reserve defense for exchange-rate uncertainty and possible inflationary bias.
4. Managed floats work best when intervention smooths temporary disturbances while allowing fundamental long-run adjustment.
5. Devaluation changes relative prices, but its timing and size are judgment calls that can invite speculation.
6. Currency crises usually expose underlying fiscal, financial, political, or external weaknesses rather than being created by speculators alone.
7. Currency boards and dollarization strengthen credibility by constraining money creation, but they remove policy tools needed to absorb asymmetric shocks.
