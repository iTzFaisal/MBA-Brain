# Chapter 3: Understanding Financial Statements and Cash Flows

## Core Idea
Financial statements are the public language used to monitor a firm's operations, resources, financing, and cash. The income statement measures accrual profits over a period, the balance sheet shows a point-in-time accounting position, and the statement of cash flows explains how cash changed; none should be read in isolation.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Income-statement waterfall**
  - When to use: Compute operating performance and earnings available to owners.
  - How: `Sales - COGS = gross profit`; `gross profit - operating expenses = operating income (EBIT)`; `EBIT - interest expense = earnings before taxes`; `earnings before taxes - income tax = net income`. Then compute `EPS = net income / shares outstanding` and `DPS = dividends / shares outstanding`.
- **Common-sized income statement**
  - When to use: Compare a firm's cost and profit structure across time or against firms of different size.
  - How: Express each income-statement item as a percentage of sales. Track gross profit margin, operating profit margin, and net profit margin.
- **Balance-sheet equation and financing view**
  - When to use: Determine what a firm owns, what it owes, and what owners have supplied on a particular date.
  - How: Enforce `total assets = total liabilities + total shareholder equity`. Separate current assets, fixed and other long-term assets, short- and long-term debt, common equity, and retained earnings. Remember that book values are historical-cost accounting values, not market values.
- **Working-capital and source/use logic**
  - When to use: Identify how changes in operating accounts affect cash.
  - How: A decrease in an asset or an increase in a liability/equity is a source of cash. An increase in an asset or a decrease in a liability/equity is a use. `Net working capital = current assets - current liabilities`.
- **Three-part statement of cash flows**
  - When to use: Reconcile beginning cash to ending cash and distinguish operating health from investment and financing decisions.
  - How: Convert accrual net income to operating cash by adding depreciation, subtracting increases in receivables, inventory, and other current assets, and adding increases in payables and accrued operating liabilities. A useful sub-rule is `cash collections from sales = sales - change in accounts receivable`. Separately report investing in long-term assets and financing through debt, equity, and dividends.

## Key Concepts
- **Accrual basis accounting**: Revenue is recorded when earned and expenses when incurred, regardless of cash timing.
- **Income statement**: Period report of sales, expenses, and net income.
- **Cost of goods sold**: Cost of producing or acquiring goods or services sold in ordinary operations.
- **Operating income (EBIT)**: Sales less COGS and operating expenses, before interest and taxes.
- **Net income**: Earnings available to common and preferred stockholders after interest and taxes.
- **Balance sheet**: Point-in-time statement of assets, liabilities, and shareholders' equity.
- **Book value**: Historical-cost value reported on the balance sheet, not current market value.
- **Liquidity/current assets**: Ability to turn assets into cash quickly without a significant loss; current assets normally convert within 12 months.
- **Retained earnings**: Cumulative profits kept in the business: `beginning RE + net income - dividends = ending RE`.
- **Statement of cash flows**: Reconciliation of cash from operating, investing, and financing activities.

## Mental Models
- **Profit is a claim; cash is fuel**: Accrual profit can exist without collections, while depreciation can reduce profit without using cash.
- **Read the balance sheet as a funding map**: Every asset is financed by a liability or equity claim; changes reveal sources and uses of cash.
- **Work from the three cash-flow buckets**: Solve operating, investing, and financing activities separately, then add them to the beginning cash balance.
- **Treat retained earnings as an accounting accumulation, not a cash account**: Cash may have been invested in inventory, equipment, or receivables.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assume positive net income means positive cash flow**: Credit sales, unpaid expenses, inventory purchases, and depreciation break that equivalence.
- **Treat depreciation as a cash outflow in the cash-flow reconciliation**: Add it back because it is noncash.
- **Treat book value as firm market value**: Historical cost and accumulated depreciation do not capture current economic value.
- **Use net fixed assets when tracing investment cash**: Use the change in gross fixed assets and account separately for depreciation.
- **Treat retained earnings as cash**: Retained earnings are cumulative accounting profits less dividends, not an available bank balance.
- **Ignore accounting discretion or fraud**: GAAP allows choices that affect comparability, and managers can manipulate or misstate reports.

## Worked Example
Menielle, Inc. reports sales of $400,000, COGS of $150,000, selling and marketing expense of $40,000, administrative expense of $30,000, depreciation of $20,000, interest of $35,000, taxes of $40,000, 20,000 shares, and $15,000 of dividends.

```text
Sales                                      $400,000
- COGS                                      150,000
= Gross profit                              250,000
- Operating expenses (40,000 + 30,000 + 20,000) 90,000
= Operating income (EBIT)                  160,000
- Interest                                  35,000
= Earnings before tax                      125,000
- Taxes                                     40,000
= Net income                                $85,000
EPS = 85,000 / 20,000                       $4.25
DPS = 15,000 / 20,000                       $0.75
```

The gross, operating, and net profit margins are 62.5%, 40.0%, and 21.3%. Dividends are 17.6% of net income, so most earnings are retained for growth.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start with the income-statement waterfall, but do not confuse its accrual results with cash.
2. Use common-sized statements to compare cost structures and profit margins across firms or periods.
3. Check that assets equal liabilities plus equity and interpret the balance sheet as a financing map.
4. More liquidity reduces distress risk but may lower profitability because liquid assets generally earn less.
5. For cash from operations, add noncash depreciation, adjust for changes in operating assets, and adjust oppositely for operating liabilities.
6. Separate operating, investing, and financing cash flows; a falling cash balance is not automatically bad if the firm is investing productively.
7. Treat financial statements as useful but imperfect monitoring evidence because accounting choices and misconduct can distort comparisons.
