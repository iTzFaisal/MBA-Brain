# Chapter 4: Evaluating a Firm's Financial Performance

## Core Idea
Financial analysis turns accounting statements into comparable relationships that reveal liquidity, operating effectiveness, financing risk, shareholder returns, and market expectations. Ratios are decision aids, not verdicts: interpret them over time, against appropriate peers, and in the context of accounting choices and business seasonality.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Five-question approach to ratio analysis**
  - When to use: Build a complete performance diagnosis rather than cherry-picking a single ratio.
  - How: Ask: (1) How liquid is the firm? (2) Are managers generating adequate operating profits on assets? (3) How is the firm financing its assets? (4) Are managers providing a good return on shareholders' capital? (5) Are managers creating shareholder value?
- **Two comparison perspectives**
  - When to use: Decide what a ratio means relative to a benchmark.
  - How: Compare the firm's ratios across time and with a properly selected peer group or industry norms. Use common-sized statements and investigate differences before drawing conclusions.
- **Liquidity block**
  - When to use: Test whether short-term obligations can be paid on time and whether current assets are genuinely liquid.
  - How: Use `current ratio = current assets / current liabilities`; `acid-test ratio = (cash + accounts receivable) / current liabilities`; `days in receivables = accounts receivable / (annual credit sales / 365)` or its reciprocal `AR turnover = credit sales / accounts receivable`; and `days in inventory = inventory / (COGS / 365)` or `inventory turnover = COGS / inventory`.
- **Operating-return decomposition**
  - When to use: Explain why a firm's operating return is high or low.
  - How: `OROA = operating income / total assets = operating profit margin * total asset turnover`, where `operating profit margin = operating income / sales` and `total asset turnover = sales / total assets`. Drill into AR, inventory, and fixed-asset turnover.
- **Financing and leverage block**
  - When to use: Assess the amount of debt and the firm's capacity to cover fixed interest.
  - How: `debt ratio = total debt / total assets`; `times interest earned = EBIT / interest expense`. More debt generally increases financial risk, while higher operating profitability improves interest coverage.
- **ROE and favorable financial leverage**
  - When to use: Explain shareholders' accounting return and the effect of funding choices.
  - How: `ROE = net income / total common equity`. More debt raises ROE only when `OROA > interest rate`; if `OROA < interest rate`, additional debt lowers ROE and increases losses for equity holders.
- **Market-value ratios**
  - When to use: See how investors assess reported earnings and invested book capital.
  - How: `P/E = market price per share / EPS`; `P/B = market price per share / equity book value per share`. A high ratio relative to peers implies stronger expected growth and/or lower perceived risk, not proof of superior operations.

## Key Concepts
- **Financial ratio**: Accounting data restated in relative terms to expose strengths, weaknesses, and trends.
- **Liquidity**: Ability to pay bills on time and convert noncash assets to cash without a significant loss.
- **Current and acid-test ratios**: Broad and stringent comparisons of current resources to current liabilities.
- **Days/turnover measures**: Alternative ways to measure the speed and quality of receivable and inventory conversion.
- **Operating return on assets (OROA)**: EBIT generated per dollar of total assets.
- **Operations management**: Control of revenues, COGS, and operating expenses, reflected in operating profit margin.
- **Asset management**: Efficiency with which assets generate sales, reflected in turnover ratios.
- **Debt ratio and times interest earned**: Measures of debt reliance and EBIT coverage of interest.
- **Return on equity (ROE)**: Net income earned per dollar of common book equity.
- **P/E and P/B ratios**: Market prices placed on one dollar of earnings and one dollar of book equity, respectively.

## Mental Models
- **Diagnose before prescribing**: Use the five questions to locate the problem, then decompose the relevant ratio into operational drivers.
- **Margin versus turnover**: A firm can earn returns through high profit per sale, fast asset use, or both; a strong turnover cannot fully offset a weak margin.
- **Leverage is a two-edged sword**: Debt magnifies the spread between OROA and the interest rate, improving ROE in good conditions and worsening it in bad conditions.
- **Ratios generate questions**: A ratio above or below a norm signals where to investigate, not what the final answer must be.

## Anti-patterns
- **Judge a firm from one ratio**: Liquidity, profitability, leverage, ROE, and market expectations interact.
- **Use net income to measure operating returns on assets**: Use EBIT to isolate operations from financing.
- **Treat an industry average as an ideal target**: A norm describes an average firm and may not suit a specific strategy.
- **Compare mismatched businesses or accounting policies**: Peer selection, depreciation methods, and other accounting choices affect ratios.
- **Use year-end balances for seasonal firms without caution**: Average balances across months or quarters can be more representative.
- **Assume higher is always better**: Excess liquidity can depress returns; lower liquidity may reflect efficient working-capital management or distress.
- **Call higher leverage automatically beneficial**: It raises ROE only when the asset return exceeds the debt interest rate and it always raises financial risk.

## Worked Example
Walmart's 2018 ratios compared with Target show how to move from measurement to diagnosis:

```text
Liquidity: current 0.74 vs 0.95; quick 0.15 vs 0.20
Collections: AR days 10.24 vs 9.56; AR turnover 35.65X vs 38X
Inventory: 42.80 days and 8.53X vs 61.81 days and 5.91X
Fixed assets: turnover 4.36X vs 2.87X
Operating return: OROA 10.0% vs 11.1%
  OPM 4.1% vs 6.0%; total asset turnover 2.45X vs 1.84X
Financing: debt ratio 61% vs 70%; times interest earned 9.38X vs 6.47X
Owners/market: ROE 12.5% vs 25%; P/E 26.22X vs 14.07X; P/B 3.27X vs 3.53X
```

Walmart is slightly weaker on broad liquidity and receivable collection, but it turns inventory and fixed assets faster. Its lower OROA is driven by a much lower operating margin despite better total asset turnover. Lower debt gives Walmart stronger interest coverage but also contributes to its lower ROE. Investors nevertheless price Walmart's earnings more richly, suggesting better expected growth and/or lower perceived risk; Target commands the higher price-to-book multiple.

## Key Takeaways
1. Begin ratio analysis with the five questions and use both time-series and peer comparisons.
2. Evaluate liquidity with both asset-to-liability ratios and conversion speed; quality matters, not just quantity.
3. Decompose OROA into operating margin and asset turnover before recommending corrective action.
4. Use debt ratio with times interest earned; debt size alone does not show current servicing capacity.
5. Favorable leverage requires `OROA > interest rate`; otherwise debt destroys ROE while increasing risk.
6. Use ROE to assess shareholders' accounting return and P/E/P/B to incorporate market expectations.
7. Treat unusual ratios as prompts for additional questions about strategy, accounting, seasonality, and asset quality.
