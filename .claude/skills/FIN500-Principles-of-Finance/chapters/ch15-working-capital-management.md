# Chapter 15: Working-Capital Management

## Core Idea
Working-capital management balances liquidity against return. Hold enough current assets and use financing that will be available when obligations mature, but avoid idle assets and unnecessarily expensive long-term funding. The cash conversion cycle measures how long operating cash is tied up; the effective cost of credit determines which short-term source is actually economical.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Risk-return trade-off in liquidity**
  - When to use: When choosing the size of current-asset balances or the mix of short- and long-term liabilities.
  - How: More cash and marketable securities, or more long-term debt, lowers illiquidity risk but generally lowers return. More current liabilities can be cheaper and flexible, but increases refinancing, availability, and variable-interest-rate risk.
- **Hedging principle (principle of self-liquidating debt)**
  - When to use: When matching an asset need with a debt maturity.
  - How: Match the cash-flow-generating characteristics of the asset with the maturity of its financing. Finance temporary assets with temporary sources; finance permanent investments with permanent sources; use spontaneous trade credit and payables where operations create them. A seasonal inventory build should be paid down from the inventory's sale proceeds.
- **Cash conversion cycle (CCC)**
  - When to use: When comparing operating working-capital efficiency or diagnosing why cash is tied up.
  - How: `CCC = DSO + DSI - DPO`, where `DSO = accounts receivable / (sales / 365)`, `DSI = inventories / (COGS / 365)`, and `DPO = accounts payable / (COGS / 365)`. Reduce CCC by collecting sooner, turning inventory faster, or obtaining longer supplier terms without damaging operations or supplier relationships.
- **Effective cost-of-credit analysis**
  - When to use: When comparing short-term credit whose term, usable proceeds, fees, discounts, or compensating balances differ.
  - How: Start with `Interest = principal * rate * time`; compute simple `APR = interest / (principal * time)` and, when compounding matters, `APY = (1 + i/m)^m - 1`. Use usable proceeds as principal when a compensating balance or discount reduces funds available. For trade terms `a/b, net c`, the annualized cost of passing up the discount is `(a / (1 - a)) * (360 / (c - b))`.
- **Source-of-credit evaluation**
  - When to use: After the required amount and duration are known.
  - How: Classify the source as unsecured or secured, then compare effective cost, availability for the needed period, and the effect on other financing sources. Consider trade credit, bank lines or transaction loans, commercial paper, receivables pledges/factoring, and inventory collateral.

## Key Concepts
- **Gross working capital**: Total investment in current assets.
- **Net working capital**: `current assets - current liabilities`.
- **Operating net working capital**: `current assets - cash and marketable securities - (current liabilities - short-term debt)`.
- **Permanent investment**: An asset investment expected to be held longer than one year, including minimum current assets and fixed assets.
- **Temporary investment**: A current-asset investment that will be liquidated and not replaced within one year.
- **Spontaneous financing**: Trade credit, wages payable, accrued interest, and accrued taxes arising from operations.
- **APR/APY**: Annualized simple cost versus annualized compounded yield.
- **Compensating balance**: Required idle deposit that raises the effective cost of bank credit.
- **Commercial paper**: Short-term unsecured promissory notes issued by large, creditworthy firms.
- **Factoring**: Outright sale of receivables to a factor that bears collection risk, generally without recourse.

## Mental Models
- **Self-liquidating debt**: Ask, "What cash flow will repay this borrowing?" If the asset will last 20 years, a one-year note is a maturity mismatch.
- **CCC as a cash clock**: DSO and DSI move the clock forward; DPO moves it backward. A negative CCC means suppliers finance operations longer than cash is tied up.
- **Usable dollars, not face rate**: Compare total cost with the funds the firm can actually use after discounts, fees, and required deposits.
- **Liquidity has two dimensions**: The firm needs both enough cash and financing that remains available at the time it is needed.

## Anti-patterns
- **Finance permanent assets with repeated short-term rollovers**: A refinancing failure or rate increase can create insolvency even when the project is profitable.
- **Compare quoted rates only**: Ignoring discounted interest, compensating balances, processing fees, reserves, or service savings understates effective cost.
- **Stretch trade credit as a routine strategy**: Delayed payment can be an emergency source, but repeated violations can destroy supplier credit.
- **Optimize CCC mechanically**: Lower DSO or inventory may sacrifice sales or production reliability; higher DPO may trigger higher prices or lost supplier support.
- **Use commercial paper without a liquidity backstop**: Its impersonal market offers little flexibility if an issue cannot be rolled over.

## Worked Example
M&M Beverage needs `$200,000` for six months. Its line requires a 20% compensating balance and charges 10% annual interest.

1. Since only 80% of borrowing is usable, total borrowing must be `$200,000 / 0.80 = $250,000`.
2. Six-month interest is `$250,000 * 0.10 * (180/360) = $12,500`.
3. Effective APR uses usable principal: `($12,500 / $200,000) * (360/180) = 12.5%`.
4. If interest is deducted in advance, usable proceeds fall to `$187,500`, and the effective APR becomes `($12,500 / $187,500) * 2 = 13.33%`.

## Key Takeaways
1. Liquidity policies always trade lower default risk for lower expected return, or lower cost for higher refinancing risk.
2. Match temporary asset needs with temporary debt and permanent needs with permanent financing.
3. Reduce CCC by accelerating collections, increasing inventory turns, and delaying disbursement appropriately.
4. Annualize the cost of every short-term source using usable proceeds and all fees or required balances.
5. Select credit on effective cost, availability, and financing-system effects, not on the nominal interest rate alone.
