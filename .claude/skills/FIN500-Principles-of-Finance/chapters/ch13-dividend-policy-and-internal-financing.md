# Chapter 13: Dividend Policy and Internal Financing

## Core Idea
Paying dividends distributes cash that could otherwise finance the firm's investments; retaining earnings reduces the need for external equity. Dividend policy is therefore a financing choice as well as a communication and distribution choice. In practice, pay dividends after funding acceptable investments, preserve a credible long-term pattern, and consider taxes, agency costs, investor clientele, liquidity, legal limits, and repurchases.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Dividend-retention trade-off**
  - **When to use:** Use after investment and target debt-equity decisions are known.
  - **How:** Measure `Dividend payout ratio = DPS / EPS`. A larger payout leaves less internal equity, increasing reliance on external equity; a smaller payout retains more funds for growth.
- **Three basic dividend views**
  - **When to use:** Use to diagnose why investors might value dividends differently from retained earnings.
  - **How:** Under **dividend irrelevance**, hold investment and financing decisions fixed and assume perfect capital markets; investors are indifferent between dividend income and capital gains. Under the **bird-in-the-hand dividend theory**, dividends are more certain than capital gains and therefore valued more highly. Under the low-dividend or tax-preference view, retention is favored because capital gains can be taxed later and may receive favorable treatment, provided retained funds earn attractive returns.
- **Residual dividend theory**
  - **When to use:** Use when flotation costs make retained earnings cheaper than issuing new common stock.
  - **How:** Accept every positive-NPV investment; finance the equity portion first with internal funds; issue new equity only after internal funds are exhausted; pay dividends only from the remaining internal capital. Use a multi-year investment forecast rather than reacting to one year's residual.
- **Clientele, information, agency, and expectations effects**
  - **When to use:** Use when a dividend policy change could affect investor demand or the stock price even though the firm's investment cash flows are unchanged.
  - **How:** Match policies to investor income and tax preferences (clientele effect), recognize that managers know more than investors (information asymmetry), use payouts to reduce monitoring or free-cash-flow agency problems, and compare the announced dividend with what investors expected. An unexpected cut can signal weaker future earnings; avoid unnecessary surprises.
- **Practical policy and payment procedure**
  - **When to use:** Use when converting a long-run payout objective into an annual policy.
  - **How:** Check legal and contract restrictions, liquidity, earnings predictability, ownership-control goals, and access to capital markets. Choose a constant payout ratio, stable dollar dividend per share, or a small regular dividend plus a year-end extra. Track declaration, ex-dividend, record, and payment dates in that order except that ex-dividend occurs before record.
- **Stock dividends, splits, and repurchases**
  - **When to use:** Use when distributing value without a conventional cash dividend or when changing shares outstanding and the capital structure.
  - **How:** A stock dividend distributes additional shares up to 25% of shares outstanding; a larger distribution is conventionally a stock split. A repurchase reduces shares outstanding and can substitute for a dividend, alter leverage, or signal undervaluation. For a cash-equivalent repurchase, use the post-dividend share value plus the dividend as the repurchase price, then calculate shares bought as cash paid divided by that price.

## Key Concepts
- **Dividend payout ratio:** Dividends per share divided by earnings per share.
- **Dividend irrelevance:** In perfect capital markets with fixed investment and financing decisions, payout form does not change firm value.
- **Bird-in-the-hand dividend theory:** Dividends are more certain than capital gains and therefore more valuable.
- **Residual dividend theory:** Dividends equal cash left after financing all positive-NPV investments.
- **Clientele effect:** Investors select firms whose payout mix matches their current-income and future-growth preferences.
- **Information asymmetry and expectations theory:** Management knows more about operations and prospects than outside investors, so market reaction depends on how the actual dividend differs from what investors expected.
- **Agency costs:** Value lost from conflicts between managers and security holders; dividends can increase outside monitoring.
- **Dividend payment dates:** Declaration is board approval; ex-dividend is conventionally two working days before record; record identifies the owners entitled to payment; payment is when cash is sent.
- **Stock dividend and stock split:** Pro rata share distributions; a distribution above 25% is conventionally treated as a split.
- **Stock repurchase and tender offer:** A repurchase reduces shares outstanding; a tender offer is a formal offer to buy a specified number at a stated price, normally above market.

## Mental Models
- **Dividends are residual financing.** First fund positive-NPV investments and the target financing mix; only then decide what cash can safely leave the firm.
- **Actions speak louder than labels.** Investors interpret an unexpected dividend change as information about future earning power, not merely as a current cash transfer.
- **Retention is valuable only when productive.** Low payouts help only if retained cash is invested in positive-NPV opportunities; otherwise returning cash can prevent managerial waste.
- **The ownership pie is not larger after a split.** A stock dividend or split changes the number and price of pieces, not the firm's investments or aggregate earnings claim.

## Anti-patterns
- **Equating retained earnings with available cash:** Retained earnings may already be invested or committed to debt; dividends require liquidity.
- **Paying a dividend that crowds out a positive-NPV investment:** A payout can force costly external equity and reduce current-owner wealth.
- **Changing the dividend capriciously:** Unanticipated cuts or extras can reset investor expectations and damage credibility.
- **Treating a stock split as value creation:** Unless demand, information, or trading-range effects change, the split is a proportional redivision of ownership.
- **Assuming a repurchase is automatically fair:** Buying above intrinsic value harms remaining holders; disclose purpose and method and use a fair price.
- **Using EPS alone to compare dividends and repurchases:** Consider taxes, ownership concentration, future investment needs, risk, and the treatment of holders who do not sell.

## Worked Example
Telink has net income of `$7.5 million`, 1,000,000 shares, EPS of `$7.50`, a P/E ratio of `8`, and a post-dividend share price of `$60`. Management can pay `$4 million` in dividends (`$4` per share) or repurchase shares at `$64`, the post-dividend price plus the dividend.

With a repurchase, the firm buys:

`$4,000,000 / $64 = 62,500 shares`

Shares outstanding fall to `937,500`, so:

`EPS after repurchase = $7,500,000 / 937,500 = $8.00`

Applying the P/E ratio gives an implied price of `$8.00 * 8 = $64.00`. A holder who receives the dividend has `$60` of stock plus `$4` cash, also `$64`; absent taxes, commissions, and information effects, the two distributions are economically equivalent. In reality, a seller may defer or pay a different tax on appreciation, while a non-selling holder can defer the tax entirely.

## Key Takeaways
1. A higher payout means less internal financing and potentially more costly external equity.
2. Use the residual dividend rule: fund positive-NPV investments first, then distribute the remaining internal cash.
3. Treat liquidity, legal and debt covenants, earnings stability, and control as binding payout constraints.
4. Maintain a stable long-run dividend pattern and avoid surprises relative to investor expectations.
5. Retention is not automatically good; it creates value only when management can invest the cash productively.
6. Dividends and repurchases can be equivalent in perfect markets, but taxes, transaction costs, information, valuation, and investor preferences make the choice consequential.
