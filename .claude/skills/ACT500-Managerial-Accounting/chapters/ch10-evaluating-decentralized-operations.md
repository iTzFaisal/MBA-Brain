# Chapter 10: Evaluating Decentralized Operations

## Core Idea
Decentralization delegates decisions to managers close to products, customers, and regions. It improves speed, expertise, training, and responsiveness only when decision rights, performance measures, and incentives are aligned with company-wide goals. Managerial accounting supplies responsibility reports, ROI/residual-income measures, and transfer-pricing rules to make that alignment visible.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Organizational architecture stool**: Balance decision rights, performance measures, and incentives; changing one leg requires checking the other two.
- **Responsibility accounting**: Report performance by responsibility center and match accountability to authority.
- **Responsibility-center ladder**: Use cost centers for cost control, profit centers for revenues and costs, and investment centers for revenues, costs, and assets.
- **DuPont ROI**: `ROI = profit margin x investment turnover`; improve ROI through margin, asset use, or both.
- **Residual income**: `Operating income - (minimum acceptable return x invested assets)`; use to encourage projects above the corporate hurdle even when they reduce average ROI.
- **Transfer-pricing corridor**: With excess capacity, a negotiated price should generally exceed variable cost and remain below market price; at full capacity, opportunity cost changes the result.

## Key Concepts
- **Decision rights**: Authority delegated to a manager or unit.
- **Controllable cost**: Cost the manager can influence.
- **Cost center**: Responsible mainly for costs.
- **Profit center**: Responsible for revenues and costs.
- **Investment center**: Responsible for revenues, costs, and invested assets.
- **ROI**: Operating income divided by invested assets.
- **Residual income**: Income above the required return on assets.
- **Transfer price**: Internal price charged by one division to another.

## Mental Models
- Decentralization is not delegation alone; it is a matched authority-measure-incentive system.
- Use a controllability filter before charging a manager with an allocated cost.
- Raw operating income is a scale measure; ROI and residual income add asset efficiency and hurdle-rate context.
- Internal transfer decisions must pass both the divisional-incentive test and the total-company-income test.

## Anti-patterns
- Delegating decisions while retaining incentives that reward only local savings.
- Evaluating investment managers with operating income alone.
- Using ROI alone and causing rejection of projects above the corporate minimum return.
- Charging noncontrollable corporate costs as if the manager chose them.
- Allocating support costs equally or by revenue when usage differs.
- Using actual transfer costs to pass supplier inefficiency to the buyer.
- Using market price for an excess-capacity transfer without considering idle capacity.

## Worked Example
Wilson's Eastern Division has excess capacity, variable cost of `$10`, and market price of `$20`. It transfers 20,000 units to Western at a negotiated `$15` price.

- Eastern gain = `($15 - $10) x 20,000 = $100,000`.
- Western gain = `($20 - $15) x 20,000 = $100,000`.
- Company gain = `($20 - $10) x 20,000 = $200,000`.

The transfer price aligns both divisions because it lies between variable cost and market price. If Eastern were at full capacity, the opportunity cost of displaced external sales would change the minimum acceptable price.

## Key Takeaways
1. Document decision rights before choosing measures.
2. Match cost, profit, or investment-center reports to managerial authority.
3. Decompose ROI into margin and turnover.
4. Use residual income when investment growth and company alignment matter.
5. Evaluate transfer pricing under capacity constraints and from the company's perspective.
