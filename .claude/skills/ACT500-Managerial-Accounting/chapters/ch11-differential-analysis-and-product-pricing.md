# Chapter 11: Differential Analysis and Product Pricing

## Core Idea
Differential analysis compares the future revenues and costs that change between alternatives. It excludes sunk costs and unchanged fixed costs, includes opportunity costs when capacity or resources have another use, and separates short-run special pricing from long-run normal pricing.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Differential/incremental analysis**: Compare Alternative 1 with Alternative 2 and compute the change in profit.
- **Relevant-cost filter**: Include future amounts that differ; exclude past, unrecoverable, or unchanged amounts.
- **Decision templates**: Apply the same counterfactual logic to lease/sell, continue/discontinue, make/buy, replace, process/sell, and special-order decisions.
- **Normal product pricing**: Use product cost, total cost, or variable cost plus markup, while covering omitted costs and desired profit.
- **Target costing**: `Expected market price - desired profit = target cost`; close any cost drift through design, material, labor, or waste changes.
- **Theory of constraints**: Rank constrained products by contribution margin per bottleneck hour, not contribution margin per unit.
- **Yield pricing**: Discount unused capacity in high-fixed-cost service businesses when incremental revenue exceeds incremental cost.

## Key Concepts
- **Relevant cost**: Future cost that differs between alternatives.
- **Differential/incremental cost**: Change in cost between alternatives.
- **Opportunity cost**: Contribution forgone by choosing one use of a scarce resource.
- **Sunk cost**: Past, unrecoverable cost unaffected by the decision.
- **Target cost**: Maximum allowable cost consistent with expected price and desired profit.
- **Bottleneck/constraint**: Resource whose capacity limits output.

## Mental Models
- Ask what changes under the counterfactual, not what total accounting cost exists.
- Use a sunk-cost firewall: book value is not a future cash flow.
- Separate fixed from avoidable; fixed does not automatically mean irrelevant.
- Capacity changes the answer: idle capacity permits special prices, while constrained capacity creates opportunity cost.
- Normal pricing starts with the market; special pricing starts with incremental economics.

## Anti-patterns
- Discontinuing a product because it reports a loss without testing avoidable fixed costs.
- Comparing a supplier price with unavoidable allocated overhead.
- Treating book value as relevant in a replace/sell decision.
- Accepting a special order without checking lost sales, shipping, tariffs, or quality effects.
- Ranking bottleneck products by unit margin.
- Treating cost-plus pricing as a substitute for demand and competition analysis.

## Worked Example
B-Ball has monthly capacity of 12,500 units, current sales of 10,000, and receives a one-time offer for 5,000 units at `$18`. Variable manufacturing cost is `$12.50`; fixed costs do not change and production can be spread across three months.

| Differential item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Special-order revenue: `5,000 x $18` | `$90,000` |
| Variable cost: `5,000 x $12.50` | `($62,500)` |
| Fixed cost change | `$0` |
| Differential profit | **`$27,500`** |

Accept because the price is above incremental cost and capacity is available, even though it is below the `$20` full manufacturing cost. For a long-run normal price, use a cost base that covers fixed costs, selling and administrative costs, and desired profit.

## Key Takeaways
1. Differential analysis is an alternative-based profit comparison.
2. Exclude sunk and unchanged costs; include opportunity costs.
3. A reported segment loss does not prove that discontinuation improves company income.
4. Short-run special orders and long-run normal prices require different rules.
5. Bottleneck decisions require contribution margin per constrained resource.
