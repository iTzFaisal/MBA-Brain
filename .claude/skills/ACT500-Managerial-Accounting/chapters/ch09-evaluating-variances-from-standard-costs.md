# Chapter 9: Evaluating Variances from Standard Costs

## Core Idea
A standard cost system sets attainable goals for price, rate, quantity, and time. Management compares actual costs with the standard cost allowed for actual output, decomposes differences into useful variances, investigates significant exceptions, and assigns responsibility only for controllable causes.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Normal/attainable standards**: Use standards that allow reasonable operating difficulties; reserve ideal standards for reference, not routine evaluation.
- **Principle of exceptions**: Focus management attention on significant or suspicious variances rather than investigating everything.
- **Actual-output budget performance report**: Compare actual cost with standard cost allowed for actual production, not the original planned volume.
- **Variance bridge**: Separate price/rate effects from quantity/time effects.
- **Flexible factory-overhead analysis**: Separate variable controllable spending effects from fixed-capacity volume effects.
- **Controllability framework**: Hold managers responsible only for factors they can influence.

## Key Concepts
- **Standard**: Attainable performance goal for cost, quantity, rate, or time.
- **Favorable variance**: Actual cost is less than standard cost.
- **Unfavorable variance**: Actual cost exceeds standard cost.
- **Materials price variance**: Difference caused by actual versus standard input price.
- **Materials quantity variance**: Difference caused by actual versus standard input usage.
- **Labor rate/time variances**: Differences caused by wage rate and hours used.
- **Fixed overhead volume variance**: Capacity-use effect relative to normal capacity.

## Mental Models
- Hold output constant before interpreting cost performance.
- A variance is a signal for investigation, not a final judgment.
- Responsibility follows influence, not merely the department named in a report.
- Favorable inputs can create unfavorable quality, rework, turnover, or inventory outcomes.

## Anti-patterns
- Using ideal standards as ordinary performance targets.
- Blaming Purchasing for market-wide price or exchange-rate changes.
- Treating every favorable variance as beneficial.
- Producing excess inventory to obtain a favorable volume variance.
- Changing operations while leaving standards stale.
- Optimizing one department at the expense of downstream quality or total cost.

## Worked Example
Western Rider produces 5,000 pairs. Standards are 1.5 square yards of materials at `$5`, 0.8 labor hours at `$9`, and `$3.60` variable plus `$2.40` fixed overhead per labor hour. Actuals are 7,300 yards at `$5.50`, 3,850 hours at `$10`, variable overhead `$10,400`, and fixed overhead `$12,000`.

| Variance | Calculation | Result |
|---|---:|---:|
| Materials price | `($5.50 - $5.00) x 7,300` | `$3,650 U` |
| Materials quantity | `(7,300 - 7,500) x $5` | `$1,000 F` |
| Labor rate | `($10 - $9) x 3,850` | `$3,850 U` |
| Labor time | `(3,850 - 4,000) x $9` | `$1,350 F` |
| Variable overhead controllable | `$10,400 - (4,000 x $3.60)` | `$4,000 F` |
| Fixed overhead volume | `(5,000 - 4,000) x $2.40` | `$2,400 U` |

Total manufacturing variance is `$3,550 U`: material `$2,650 U` plus labor `$2,500 U` minus overhead `$1,600 F`. Investigate price, labor mix, capacity, and quality causes rather than accepting the totals at face value.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use standard cost allowed for actual output.
2. Separate price/rate from quantity/time effects.
3. Investigate significant unfavorable and suspiciously favorable variances.
4. Use nonfinancial quality and operational measures alongside standards.
5. Assign accountability only after checking controllability and cross-department effects.
