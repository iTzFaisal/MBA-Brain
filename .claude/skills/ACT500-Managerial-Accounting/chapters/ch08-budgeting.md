# Chapter 8: Budgeting Systems

## Core Idea
Budgeting integrates planning, directing, controlling, and feedback. The master budget links sales expectations to production, materials, labor, overhead, selling and administrative expenses, income, cash, capital expenditures, financing, and accountability. A sound budget is both a technical plan and a behavioral contract.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Planning-directing-controlling cycle**: Set goals, execute through responsibility centers, compare actual results, and revise using feedback.
- **Master budget**: Coordinate operating budgets and financial budgets into one plan.
- **Budget sequence**: Sales -> production -> materials, labor, and overhead -> COGS -> selling and administrative -> income statement -> cash/capital/financing -> balance sheet.
- **Static budget**: Use one activity level when volume is stable or costs are not volume-driven.
- **Flexible budget**: Separate fixed and variable costs and compare actual spending with the budget for actual activity.
- **Continuous budgeting**: Maintain a rolling 12-month projection.
- **Zero-based budgeting**: Reassess operations as if starting from zero rather than accepting prior allocations.
- **Responsibility accounting**: Assign a manager authority and accountability for a responsibility center.

## Key Concepts
- **Budgetary period**: Usually a fiscal year subdivided into shorter control periods.
- **Budget committee**: Cross-functional group coordinating the annual budget.
- **Budgetary slack**: Cushion created by understating sales or overstating resources.
- **Goal conflict**: A local target is achieved while company performance suffers.
- **Operating budget**: Sales, production, resource, expense, and operating-income plan.
- **Financial budget**: Cash, capital expenditure, financing, and investing plan.
- **Pro forma statement**: Budgeted income statement or balance sheet.

## Mental Models
- The master budget is a dependency graph: sales drives production, which drives resource needs.
- A flexible budget is a fair benchmark when output differs from plan.
- The cash budget is a timing lens; budgeted profit does not guarantee liquidity.
- The budget is a behavioral contract that can create commitment, slack, gaming, or goal conflict.

## Anti-patterns
- Comparing actual costs with a static budget when activity changed.
- Setting impossible or trivially easy targets.
- Rewarding local results that harm the company.
- Treating budgets as entitlement pools or using "spend it or lose it" rules.
- Ignoring collection and payment timing.
- Treating depreciation as a cash payment.
- Coordinating subbudgets poorly or publishing unsupported pro forma information.

## Worked Example
Elite Accessories expects to sell 528,000 wallets at `$12` and 280,000 handbags at `$25`, for budgeted revenue of `$13,336,000`. Desired ending inventories produce planned output of 520,000 wallets and 292,000 handbags. Materials purchases total `$2,587,500`; direct labor is `$4,851,600`; factory overhead is `$2,089,080`.

1. Direct materials used = `$99,000 + $2,587,500 - $104,400 = $2,582,100`.
2. Manufacturing costs = `$2,582,100 + $4,851,600 + $2,089,080 = $9,522,780`.
3. COGM = `$214,400 + $9,522,780 - $220,000 = $9,517,180`.
4. COGS = `$1,095,600 + $9,517,180 - $1,565,000 = $9,047,780`.
5. With selling and administrative expenses of `$1,885,000`, operating income is `$2,403,220`.

The chapter's cash illustration shows why cash must be budgeted separately: a profitable plan can still fall below its minimum cash balance in a month with unfavorable collection and payment timing.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start with sales, then coordinate every dependent budget.
2. Use flexible budgets to distinguish volume effects from spending control.
3. Set challenging but attainable goals and monitor ethical behavior.
4. Keep operating income, cash, and capital expenditures as distinct planning questions.
5. Separate resource planning from incentive evaluation when gaming risk is high.
