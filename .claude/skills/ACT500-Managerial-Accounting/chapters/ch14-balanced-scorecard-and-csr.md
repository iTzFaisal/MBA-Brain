# Chapter 14: The Balanced Scorecard and Corporate Social Responsibility

## Core Idea
A strategic performance measurement system links organizational objectives to metrics. The balanced scorecard (BSC) balances financial and nonfinancial measures across financial, customer, internal-process, and learning-and-growth perspectives. Strategy maps and measure maps make causal assumptions visible, while strategic learning tests them. Corporate social responsibility (CSR) is strongest when integrated into the scorecard rather than treated as an add-on.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Balanced scorecard**: Organize performance through financial, customer, internal-process, and learning-and-growth perspectives; adapt perspectives when the organization's strategy requires it.
- **Scorecard elements**: Separate strategic objectives (purpose), metrics (representation), initiatives (action), and targets (desired level).
- **Strategy map**: Show expected cause-and-effect links among objectives, such as training -> process quality -> customer loyalty -> profit.
- **Measure map**: Link the actual variables used to represent objectives and distinguish leading from lagging indicators.
- **Strategic learning**: Test measure relationships, revise weak metrics, and question causal assumptions when evidence disagrees.
- **Scorecard cascading**: Translate corporate strategy into divisional, departmental, and personal scorecards while preserving local relevance.
- **Sustainability balanced scorecard/triple bottom line**: Integrate financial, social, and environmental outcomes; use NPV for measurable CSR cash flows and qualitative/legal judgment for nonfinancial benefits.

## Key Concepts
- **Leading indicator**: Metric that predicts another outcome.
- **Lagging indicator**: Outcome predicted by another measure.
- **Strategic objective**: Purpose or desired condition.
- **Performance metric**: Imperfect representation of an objective.
- **Strategic initiative**: Action intended to achieve an objective.
- **Performance target**: Desired metric level or improvement.
- **Surrogation**: Acting as if a measure is the objective itself.
- **Motivated reasoning/common-measures bias**: Cognitive distortions in interpreting data or comparing divisions.
- **CSR**: Responsibility for organizational impacts on society and social well-being.

## Mental Models
- A metric is a shadow of strategy, not strategy itself.
- Strategy maps are hypotheses; measure maps make hypotheses testable.
- Cascading aligns work but can create common-measures bias if unique divisional outcomes are ignored.
- Incentives focus effort but can intensify surrogation, gaming, and crowding out of intrinsic motivation.

## Anti-patterns
- Measuring only financial outcomes and missing leading indicators.
- Confusing initiatives with objectives or metrics.
- Treating strategy-map links as proven causality.
- Optimizing one salient measure while harming the underlying strategy.
- Comparing divisions only on common measures.
- Setting 100% targets that encourage harmful behavior.
- Treating CSR as a disconnected program or evaluating every social benefit through NPV alone.

## Worked Example
Cordier Toys' scorecard links employee training and lower turnover to faster delivery and fewer shipping errors, then to customer delight, repeat customers, market share, and operating profit. A compact measure map is:

`Training hours + employee tenure -> delivery hours + shipping errors -> repeat customers + satisfaction -> market share + operating profit`.

If employees obtain positive online ratings by issuing refunds, the rating improves while sales and profit decline. This is surrogation: the proxy improved while customer and financial outcomes deteriorated. The response is to use multiple measures, make strategy accessible, and test the assumed links over time. CSR measures such as utility efficiency, employee well-being, and customer CSR ratings can be integrated into the same perspectives.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start with strategy, then define objectives, measures, initiatives, and targets.
2. Balance financial outcomes with customer, process, and capability measures.
3. Treat causal links as hypotheses and use strategic learning to revise them.
4. Use multiple, balanced measures to reduce surrogation and gaming.
5. Integrate CSR when social and environmental outcomes are part of the core strategy.
