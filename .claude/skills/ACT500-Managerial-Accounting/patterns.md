# Patterns and Techniques

## Define the Cost Object First
**When to use**: Any cost-classification or allocation problem.
**How**: Name the product, job, department, customer, or decision; trace economically direct costs; allocate the rest with a defensible driver.
**Trade-offs**: More tracing improves information but can cost more than the decision benefit.

## Manufacturing Cost Flow
**When to use**: Building COGM, COGS, or inventory schedules.
**How**: Materials used -> manufacturing costs -> COGM -> COGS; reconcile beginning and ending materials, WIP, and finished goods.
**Trade-offs**: Detailed schedules improve control but require reliable inventory counts.

## Job Costing
**When to use**: Custom jobs or separately identifiable batches.
**How**: Collect requisitions and time tickets on a job sheet; apply overhead at a predetermined rate; transfer completed and sold jobs through inventory.
**Trade-offs**: Timely estimates versus actual-overhead true-up.

## Process-Cost Report
**When to use**: Continuous homogeneous production.
**How**: Reconcile physical units; calculate materials and conversion equivalent units; divide current or blended costs by equivalent units; assign transfers and ending WIP.
**Trade-offs**: FIFO isolates current performance; weighted average is simpler but blends periods.

## Activity-Based Costing
**When to use**: Products or customers consume support activities differently.
**How**: Identify activities, create pools, select drivers, compute rates, multiply rate by usage, and compare with broad-rate results.
**Trade-offs**: More accuracy and insight versus data collection and maintenance cost.

## Support Allocation
**When to use**: Distributing service-department costs.
**How**: Choose direct for simplicity, sequential for partial interaction, or reciprocal for full mutual-service recognition.
**Trade-offs**: Accuracy increases with complexity; total cost is unchanged but responsibility and product distribution change.

## CVP Decision
**When to use**: Break-even, target-profit, pricing, or volume questions.
**How**: Classify cost behavior; compute unit contribution margin or ratio; apply fixed-cost and target-profit formulas within the relevant range.
**Trade-offs**: Fast sensitivity insight depends on linearity, stable mix, and unchanged inventory assumptions.

## Variable-Costing Reconciliation
**When to use**: Absorption and variable operating income differ.
**How**: Compare production with sales; calculate the change in fixed manufacturing overhead carried in inventory.
**Trade-offs**: Absorption supports GAAP; variable costing better exposes short-run economics and inventory incentives.

## Budget-to-Actual Control
**When to use**: Planning and performance review.
**How**: Build sales-led master budgets; use flexible budgets when activity changes; investigate significant deviations and behavioral gaming.
**Trade-offs**: Participation can improve commitment but can also create slack.

## Standard Variance Bridge
**When to use**: Explaining direct material, labor, and overhead differences.
**How**: Compare actual input at actual price with actual input at standard price, then compare standard input with standard input allowed for actual output.
**Trade-offs**: Variance detail aids diagnosis but can encourage local optimization unless quality and controllability are included.

## Responsibility-Center Evaluation
**When to use**: Decentralized divisions or departments.
**How**: Match authority to cost, profit, or investment-center measures; use controllable income, ROI/turnover, and residual income as appropriate.
**Trade-offs**: Local measures can conflict with company goals without aligned incentives.

## Differential Analysis
**When to use**: Make/buy, special order, discontinue, replace, process further, or transfer decisions.
**How**: Compare future revenues and costs that change; add opportunity costs; exclude sunk and unchanged costs; check qualitative effects.
**Trade-offs**: Short-run incremental logic may not support long-run pricing or capacity recovery.

## Capital Budgeting
**When to use**: Long-lived investments.
**How**: Estimate incremental cash flows; screen with ARR/payback; discount with NPV/IRR; rank with PVI when capital is scarce; test sensitivity.
**Trade-offs**: Financial models provide discipline but depend on uncertain forecasts and may omit strategic or social benefits.

## Lean Activity Analysis
**When to use**: Flow, quality, waiting, and process-cost improvement.
**How**: Map processing, movement, waiting, setup, inspection, and failure; reduce setup and batch waiting; use pull and quality-cost analysis.
**Trade-offs**: Lower inventory exposes problems but does not solve them automatically.

## Balanced Scorecard Design
**When to use**: Strategy execution and broad performance evaluation.
**How**: Define objectives, metrics, initiatives, targets, strategy maps, and measure maps; test leading/lagging relationships and guard against surrogation.
**Trade-offs**: More balanced measures improve coverage but increase measurement and interpretation complexity.

## Cash-Flow Reconciliation
**When to use**: Explaining change in cash or earnings quality.
**How**: Classify operating, investing, and financing; under indirect method add noncash expenses, reverse gains/losses, apply working-capital signs, and reconcile ending cash.
**Trade-offs**: Indirect method is efficient from accrual data; direct method is more transparent about operating receipts and payments.

## Financial-Statement Analysis
**When to use**: Lending, investing, or management assessment.
**How**: Identify the decision; use horizontal, vertical, and common-size analysis; calculate related ratios with average balances; compare time, industry, and disclosures.
**Trade-offs**: Ratios compress information and require context about industry, seasonality, leverage, accounting policy, and unusual items.
