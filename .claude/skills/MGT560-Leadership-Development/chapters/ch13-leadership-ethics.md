# Chapter 13: Leadership Ethics
## Core Idea
Leadership ethics is not one unified theory; it is a set of lenses for judging what leaders do and who they are. Because leaders influence followers, usually hold more power, pursue shared goals, and shape organizational values, leadership carries a special moral burden. Ethical leadership treats followers as dignified human beings, seeks goals compatible with their purposes and the common good, and builds an ethical climate. The chapter's strength is its practical, timely integration of ethics into leadership; its limitation is that the field remains young, descriptive, anecdotal, and thinly supported by direct empirical research.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Kohlberg's Stages of Moral Development (Kohlberg, 1984):** Use to diagnose the reasoning behind an ethical choice, not merely the choice itself. Level 1, preconventional morality, includes Stage 1 obedience and punishment and Stage 2 individualism and exchange. Level 2, conventional morality, includes Stage 3 interpersonal accord and conformity and Stage 4 maintaining the social order. Level 3, postconventional morality, includes Stage 5 social contract and individual rights and Stage 6 universal principles. The model is seminal but criticized for its justice-only focus, all-male and individualist-cultural sample, sex and cultural bias, and preference for principles above law or society.
- **Conduct and character ethics:** Use multiple lenses before deciding. Teleological theories judge conduct by consequences: ethical egoism maximizes the leader's good, utilitarianism seeks the greatest good for the greatest number, and altruism prioritizes others' interests. Deontological theories judge duty, rights, promises, truth, fairness, and respect independent of results. Virtue-based (aretaic) theories judge character and the learned habits of courage, temperance, honesty, justice, integrity, fidelity, benevolence, and humility. The first two conduct lenses can conflict; character asks what a good person would become through repeated action.
- **Heifetz's ethical perspective and Burns's moral leadership:** Use Heifetz's approach when followers face conflicting values or adaptive change: use authority to focus attention, reality-test information, frame issues, orchestrate perspectives, and make a trusting, empathic holding environment. Burns's transformational view asks leaders to engage followers' needs and values and raise moral functioning toward liberty, justice, and equality. Its unresolved criticism is who gets to define the "higher" values.
- **Toxic triangle (Padilla, Hogan, & Kaiser, 2007):** Diagnose destructive leadership through the interaction of destructive leaders, susceptible followers, and conducive environments. Leaders may be charismatic, narcissistic, coercive, power-seeking, and driven by hateful or negative life themes. Conformers bring unmet needs, low self-evaluations, or low maturity; colluders bring ambition or compatible bad values. Instability, perceived threat, compatible cultural values, weak checks and balances, and ineffective institutions enable the triangle.
- **Five principles of ethical leadership:** Use as a behavioral audit: respect others as ends, listen and tolerate dissent; serve others through altruism, mentoring, empowerment, and stewardship; practice justice by applying clear, reasonable distributive rules; be honest without deceptive spin while judging what is appropriate to disclose; and build community by aligning leader, follower, organizational, civic, and societal purposes. The principles are a foundation, not an exhaustive decision algorithm.
- **Perceived Leader Integrity Scale (PLIS; Craig & Gustafson, 1998):** Use observer ratings to assess perceived integrity, training needs, or ethical climate. It is utilitarian in orientation; reverse-score items 9 and 30, sum 30 items, and interpret lower totals as higher perceived ethics. Ratings have been associated with job satisfaction, lower desire to quit, transformational leadership, and effectiveness; it measures perceptions rather than moral truth.

## Key Concepts
- **Ethos:** The customs, conduct, character, values, and morals that shape judgments of right and wrong.
- **Moral development:** The reasoning capacity guiding ethical choices; it can mature, but a stage label does not guarantee ethical behavior.
- **Preconventional morality:** Self-interest, punishment avoidance, rewards, and exchange.
- **Conventional morality:** Internalized group expectations, conformity, law, authority, and social order.
- **Postconventional morality:** Conscience, social contract, individual rights, justice, and universal principles.
- **Teleology:** Outcome-centered conduct ethics.
- **Deontology:** Duty-, rule-, and rights-centered conduct ethics.
- **Virtue ethics:** Character-centered ethics in which moral habits are learned through practice.
- **Pseudotransformational leadership:** Influence used for the leader's personal ends rather than mutual moral uplift.
- **Distributive justice:** Allocating benefits and burdens by equal opportunity, need, rights, effort, societal contribution, or merit/performance.

## Mental Models
- **Use** consequences, duties, and character together; a favorable outcome does not excuse rights violations, and a rule does not eliminate responsibility for foreseeable harm.
- **Think** influence creates responsibility: before acting, ask whether followers are respected, served, treated fairly, told the truth, and included in a genuine community.
- **Use** the toxic triangle to inspect the system, not just the villain; remove enabling instability, collusion, weak oversight, and unmet follower needs.
- **Think** ethically about both the immediate individual and the wider common good; fairness may require a transparent, justified distinction rather than identical treatment.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating leadership as morally neutral or confusing coercion and personal charisma with ethical influence.
- Using utilitarian benefit as permission to sacrifice rights, or using rules mechanically without considering people and context.
- Favoritism, hidden criteria, deceptive promises, evasive spin, suppressed accountability, or selective disclosure that destroys trust.
- Pseudotransformational or toxic leadership that leaves followers worse off, exploits fear, and uses power for personal gain.
- Assuming Kohlberg's postconventional level is universally superior without confronting its cultural, gender, and justice-centered limitations.

## Worked Example
Perfect Plastics Incorporated (PPI) is a 50-person injection-molding company with about $4 million in annual sales and no major injury since its founding. Owner Tom Griffin maintains a highly orderly plant and voluntarily brings in insurance and OSHA consultants each year. Audits continue to identify hazards, leading to new equipment, repairs, and workflow changes; employees respond positively and safety improves. PPI's attorneys oppose the audits because records could prove prior knowledge of unsafe conditions in a lawsuit. Management accepts that legal exposure exists but regards repeated review as a responsibility to employees and as the mechanism of continual improvement. The case is a useful ethical comparison: management's concern reflects duty to protect workers, utilitarian reduction of harm, and a virtue-like safety culture; the attorneys emphasize duty to protect the firm. A leader should make the safety philosophy explicit, preserve credible audits and corrective action, and address legal risk without treating liability avoidance as the only moral objective.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ethical leadership concerns both conduct and character and is central to influence, follower relationships, and organizational climate.
2. Kohlberg's three levels and six stages explain moral reasoning but require cultural and gender-sensitive interpretation.
3. Teleological, deontological, and virtue lenses expose different parts of a dilemma; none should be used as a shortcut.
4. Respect, service, justice, honesty, and community provide practical benchmarks for ethical action.
5. Destructive leadership is a system involving leader, follower, and environment; diagnosis must include checks and balances.
6. Ethical leadership education and research remain necessary because the empirical base is limited.
