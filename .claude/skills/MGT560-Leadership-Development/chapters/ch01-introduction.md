# Chapter 1: Introduction
## Core Idea
Leadership is a complex, relational process rather than a possession, title, or synonym for force. The text defines it as a process in which an individual influences a group of individuals to achieve a common goal. The definition requires four components: process, influence, group context, and common goals. Leaders and followers affect one another; leadership is therefore interactive, available to people in both formal and informal roles, and carries an ethical obligation to respect followers and pursue mutual purposes.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Leadership process definition:** Use the four criteria - process, influence, group, and common goal - to decide whether an activity is leadership. Without influence or a group pursuing something together, the text's definition is not met.
- **Trait versus process leadership (Jago, 1982):** Use the trait lens when examining relatively stable qualities; use the process lens when diagnosing interaction and behavior that can be learned. The process lens avoids restricting leadership to people with presumed innate gifts.
- **Assigned versus emergent leadership:** Assigned leadership follows a formal position. Emergent leadership develops when group members accept someone as influential through communication, initiative, informed participation, opinion seeking, and firm but nonrigid behavior. Hogg's social identity view adds that people emerge when they fit the group's prototype.
- **French and Raven's six bases of social power (1959; Raven, 1965):** Diagnose influence as referent, expert, legitimate, reward, coercive, or information power. Legitimate, reward, coercive, and information power are position power; referent and expert power are personal power.
- **Kotter's management-leadership distinction (1990):** Use management for order and consistency through planning, organizing, staffing, controlling, and problem solving; use leadership for change and movement through direction, alignment, motivation, and inspiration. Organizations need both.

## Key Concepts
- Leadership as a process
- Influence as the sine qua non of leadership
- Followers and mutual purpose
- Assigned leadership
- Emergent leadership
- Position power and personal power
- Coercive power versus coercion
- Management: order and stability
- Leadership: adaptive and constructive change

## Mental Models
- **Use** the process test: identify the influencer, the group, the mutual goal, and the reciprocal interaction before calling an action leadership.
- **Think** title and influence are separate variables: an appointed manager may lack followership, while an informal contributor may lead.
- **Use** the power audit: identify which base creates influence, then ask whether the resource is being used relationally for shared goals or over followers for private ends.
- **Think** order and change are complementary: leadership without management can become directionless change, while management without leadership can become bureaucracy.

## Anti-patterns
- Treating leadership as an inborn status excludes learnable leadership and ignores follower interaction.
- Equating a formal title with real leadership overlooks emergent influence and acceptance.
- Treating power as domination, especially through threats and punishment, confuses coercion with ethical leadership and centers the leader's private goals.
- Creating a false leadership-versus-management choice neglects their overlap and the organizational need for both stability and change.

## Worked Example
**Kotter's functions applied to a change initiative:** A hospital introducing a new service would need management to set budgets and timetables, allocate staff, establish procedures, and correct operational problems. It would also need leadership to explain the future direction, communicate the big picture, align clinicians and administrators, build commitment, and energize staff through the transition. A tightly controlled implementation with no shared meaning may be orderly but bureaucratic; an inspiring vision without staffing, procedures, or corrective action may produce enthusiasm without execution. The artifact demonstrates why the two processes overlap but should not be collapsed into one.

## Key Takeaways
1. Leadership is influence exercised by a person with a group toward a common goal.
2. The process view makes leadership learnable and recognizes followers as co-participants.
3. Assigned authority and emergent influence can belong to different people in the same group.
4. Power is potential influence; its ethical value depends on how it is used in the relationship.
5. Coercion is not ideal leadership because it substitutes force for mutual purpose.
6. Management creates order and consistency; leadership creates change and movement, and effective organizations require both.
