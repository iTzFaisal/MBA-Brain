# Chapter 14: Team Leadership
## Core Idea
Teams are interdependent people coordinating toward common goals, often across distance and technology. Team leadership is a functional, process-oriented form of leadership: the leader or any member monitors the team's internal and external conditions, builds a mental model of the problem, and takes whatever action meets unmet needs. Effectiveness has two outcomes, performance (quality task accomplishment) and development (cohesion and member needs). The approach is strong because it gives real teams a practical diagnostic map and legitimizes shared leadership; it is also complex, incomplete, timing-sensitive, and assumes substantial group-process and action skills.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Hill Model for Team Leadership (Susan E. Kogler Hill):** Use as a monitor-diagnose-intervene loop. Form a mental model of the team and context, then decide: (1) monitor or take action, (2) address task or relational needs, and (3) intervene internally or externally. If action is needed, select a requisite behavior and monitor results. Internal task actions are goal focusing, structuring for results, facilitating decisions, training, and maintaining standards. Internal relational actions are coaching, collaborating, managing conflict and power, building commitment, satisfying needs, and modeling ethical principles. External environmental actions are networking, advocating, negotiating support, buffering distractions, assessing indicators, and sharing information.
- **Hackman's enabling conditions and Larson-LaFasto team excellence:** Use as a benchmark before blaming members. Hackman asks whether there is a real team, a compelling purpose, the right people, clear conduct norms, organizational support, and team-focused coaching. Larson and LaFasto's eight criteria are a clear, elevating goal; results-driven structure; competent team members; unified commitment; collaborative climate; standards of excellence; external support and recognition; and principled leadership. Assess with observation, feedback, surveys, and performance indicators.
- **McGrath's critical leadership functions:** Plot the problem by internal versus external and monitoring versus executive action. The four functions are diagnosing group deficiencies, taking remedial action, forecasting environmental changes, and preventing deleterious changes. Use it to distinguish information search and structuring from intervention.
- **Shared or distributed leadership:** Treat leadership capacity as the team's repertoire, not the formal leader's property. Members step forward when their expertise is needed and step back when another member should lead. This is especially useful for complex virtual teams; legitimate power shifts (heterarchy) can improve trust, consensus, cohesion, and response speed.
- **Team Excellence Survey and Collaborative Team Leader Instrument:** Use the combined Team Excellence and Collaborative Team Leader Questionnaire for diagnosis and collaborative action planning. Team members and the leader rate the same dimensions; compare views because leaders often overestimate effectiveness. Low ratings identify priorities such as goal focus, collaborative climate, confidence building, technical know-how, priorities, or performance management.

## Key Concepts
- **Team interdependence:** Members must coordinate contributions rather than work as isolated individuals.
- **Team effectiveness:** The joint outcome of performance and development.
- **Mental model:** The leader's structured conception of team problems, constraints, resources, and possible solutions.
- **Requisite variety:** A behavioral repertoire complex enough to match the team's situation.
- **Leader mediation process:** Choosing monitoring or action, intervention level, and specific function.
- **Shared leadership:** Distributed influence enacted by members as conditions warrant.
- **Heterarchy:** Legitimate, fluid shifts in power within a team.
- **Collaborative climate:** Trust, openness, safe risk-taking, listening, and coordinated mutual support.
- **Virtual team:** A geographically dispersed team dependent on communication technology and deliberate relational maintenance.

## Mental Models
- **Use** the sequence monitor, classify, act, and re-monitor; do not jump from a visible symptom to a favorite intervention.
- **Think** in two simultaneous outcomes: a team that delivers but damages cohesion, or bonds but misses its goal, is not excellent.
- **Use** the task/relational and internal/external distinctions to locate the actual need; a team fight may reflect unclear roles or missing organizational autonomy.
- **Think** shared leadership is an adaptive resource: formal leaders should fill gaps, not monopolize every function.

## Anti-patterns
- Calling a collection of individuals a team while leaving goals, roles, norms, and accountability vague.
- Intervening hastily with incomplete information, or delaying until a manageable problem becomes unmanageable; timing and team life stage matter.
- Assuming technical competence guarantees interpersonal or teamwork competence.
- Focusing only on internal conflict while ignoring resources, recognition, competing priorities, or environmental threats.
- Overcontrolling mature teams, avoiding inadequate performance, diluting effort with too many priorities, or rewarding individuals when the work is collective.
- Treating virtual teams like co-located teams; distance increases the need for explicit communication and relationship building.

## Worked Example
Jim Towne leads a newly formed 20-person information-technology team spanning Canada, the United States, Europe, South America, Africa, and Australia. Members are technically competent and excited by the global innovation task, but they have never worked together, travel at least two weeks each month, serve several projects, and cannot finish work. A half-time New York secretary mainly handles travel and meetings; one member has 500 unread messages because everyone copies everyone. Using the Hill Model, Jim should act rather than merely monitor. Internally, he needs task interventions: focus and prioritize a limited goal set, structure roles and decision channels, and facilitate information sharing. Relationally, he should establish norms that make collaboration and shared leadership possible. Externally, he should negotiate adequate coordination support and protect the team from incompatible demands. The team should then reassess performance, workload, communication, and commitment through member and leader feedback.

## Key Takeaways
1. Team leadership is diagnosis and action in service of performance and development, not simply formal authority.
2. The Hill Model's three decisions determine whether, where, and how to intervene.
3. Hackman and Larson-LaFasto criteria turn vague team health into observable benchmarks.
4. Effective action can be task, relational, external, or shared across members.
5. Team leadership models guide judgment but do not supply instant answers; training, timing, and feedback remain essential.
