## Core Frameworks & Mental Models
Use this as a practitioner toolkit, not as a summary. Preserve the named model and its attribution, diagnose before prescribing, and state when evidence or context limits the claim.

### Foundation: influence, power, and change
- **Use Northouse's process definition of leadership** when deciding whether an activity is leadership: an individual influences a group of individuals to achieve a common goal. Check process, influence, group, and common purpose; include reciprocal leader-follower interaction and the ethical responsibility created by influence.
- **Use French and Raven's six bases of social power** (French & Raven, 1959; Raven, 1965) to audit influence: referent, expert, legitimate, reward, coercive, and information power. Treat referent and expert as personal power; legitimate, reward, coercive, and information as position power. Prefer mutual purpose over coercion because threats and punishment substitute force for shared influence and can serve private ends.
- **Use Kotter's management-leadership distinction** (Kotter, 1990) to separate functions, not people: management creates order and consistency through planning, organizing, staffing, controlling, and problem solving; leadership creates direction, alignment, motivation, and inspiration for change. Prefer both over a false choice: order without direction becomes bureaucracy, while inspiration without execution becomes drift.

### Person, capability, and behavior
- **Use the Trait Approach** when selecting, coaching, or building self-awareness for a particular role. Compare intelligence, self-confidence, determination, integrity, and sociability with situational demands; use Stogdill's trait-situation findings (1948, 1974), the Big Five model (Goldberg, 1990; Judge et al., 2002), and emotional-intelligence work (Mayer, Salovey, & Caruso; Goleman) as evidence about tendencies, not destiny. Prefer a role-fit hypothesis over Great Man theory or a universal trait checklist because traits alone do not establish effectiveness.
- **Use Katz's Three-Skill Approach** (Katz, 1955) when the development need is concrete: technical skill for specialized work, human skill for cooperation, and conceptual skill for systems, meaning, vision, and strategy. **Use the Mumford et al. Skills Model** (Mumford, Zaccaro, Harding, Jacobs, & Fleishman, 2000) for complex problems: problem-solving skills, social judgment skills, and knowledge produce performance, shaped by individual attributes, career experiences, and environmental influences. Prefer skills for development planning because they are learnable; do not treat the broad, partly trait-like model as a precise prediction equation.
- **Use the Behavioral Approach** when the question is what a leader does. Keep task behavior and relationship behavior as independent dials: Ohio State's initiating structure and consideration, Michigan's production and employee orientations, and Blake and Mouton's Leadership Grid (1964; 1978; 1985; Blake & McCanse, 1991). Use follower feedback and outcomes to decide whether to add structure, support, or both. Treat team management (9,9) as a development hypothesis, not a universal best style; the evidence does not establish one behavior combination for every context.

### Contingency and relationship matching
- **Use Situational Leadership II (SLII)** (Hersey and Blanchard; refined by Blanchard) when a follower's competence and commitment differ by goal or project phase. Diagnose goal-specific development, then match **D1 -> S1 directing** (low competence/high commitment), **D2 -> S2 coaching** (some competence/low commitment), **D3 -> S3 supporting** (moderate-to-high competence/variable commitment), and **D4 -> S4 delegating** (high competence/high commitment). Treat directive and supportive behavior as separate dials and reassess after setbacks or new tasks. Prefer individual diagnosis over group averages; the model is practical but research support is limited and inconsistent.
- **Use Path-Goal Theory** (House & Mitchell) when effort is blocked, motivation is weak, or the route to a valued outcome is unclear. Check Vroom's expectancy, instrumentality, and valence, then use directive behavior for missing structure, supportive behavior for frustration or repetitive work, participative behavior for control and input, and achievement-oriented behavior for credible challenge. Remove obstacles and clarify valued payoffs, but do not duplicate structure already supplied by clear tasks, rules, authority, or norms. Prefer path-goal over SLII when the diagnosis centers on motivation, task characteristics, or obstacle removal rather than follower development; its broad, complex causal claims have only partial support.
- **Use Leader-Member Exchange (LMX) and Vertical Dyad Linkage (VDL)** when group averages hide unequal trust, information, influence, access, or opportunity. Audit each dyad, distinguish in-group expanded roles from out-group contractual roles, and use **leadership making** (Graen & Uhl-Bien) to offer every follower a genuine chance to move from stranger to acquaintance to mature partnership through reciprocal trust, respect, obligation, support, and recognition. Prefer transparent opportunity over a permanent clique; high-quality LMX is associated with positive outcomes, but bias, measurement, context, and unrewarded extra-role labor remain risks.

### Meaning, morality, service, and change
- **Use transformational leadership** (Burns) when compliance is insufficient and followers need shared meaning, moral purpose, growth, innovation, or collective commitment. Use Bass's **Full Range of Leadership**: the Four Is are idealized influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation, and individualized consideration; contingent reward and active or passive management-by-exception add transactional clarity; laissez-faire is nonleadership. Prefer a full-range diagnosis over treating transformation as the only behavior. Apply the **pseudotransformational warning** (Bass; Bass & Steidlmeier): a compelling vision is not legitimate when it is personalized, exploitative, intolerant of dissent, or indifferent to follower welfare.
- **Use authentic leadership** for distrust, inconsistency, or leadership blind spots. Apply Walumbwa et al.'s four components: self-awareness, internalized moral perspective, balanced processing, and relational transparency. Use George's practical approach (George, 2003; George & Sims, 2007) for purpose, values/True North, relationships, self-discipline, and heart/compassion; use life-story reflection, feedback, and the ALQ as diagnostic aids. Prefer appropriate transparency and dissent-seeking over unfiltered self-expression; authenticity does not guarantee competence, ethics, or results, and causal evidence remains incomplete.
- **Use servant leadership** (Greenleaf) when follower growth, autonomy, well-being, least-privileged effects, and community value are central. Apply the seven Liden et al. behaviors: conceptualizing, emotional healing, putting followers first, helping followers grow and succeed, behaving ethically, empowering, and creating value for the community. Use Spears's ten characteristics as a behavior checklist and test follower receptivity. Prefer service with accountability and shared authority over paternalistic helping; care that removes autonomy is intrusion, and the model's dimensions and causal evidence remain contested.
- **Use adaptive leadership** (Heifetz; Heifetz, Grashow, & Linsky) when values, identity, roles, habits, or behavior must change. Diagnose a technical challenge (known problem and solution), technical-and-adaptive challenge (clear goal plus learning), or adaptive challenge (ambiguous, value-laden, and not solvable by authority or expertise). Use the six behaviors: get on the balcony, identify the adaptive challenge, regulate distress, maintain disciplined attention, give the work back to the people, and protect leadership voices from below. Build a **holding environment** with enough safety for honesty and enough tension for learning. Prefer adaptive work over a technical fix when ownership must change; do not overheat, overprotect, silence dissent, or treat this prescriptive model as settled empirical law.
- **Use the Psychodynamic Approach's Clinical Paradigm** when repeated irrationality, anxiety, idealization, scapegoating, or collusion resists ordinary explanations. Treat inner theatre and Core Conflictual Relationship Themes as hypotheses about recurring wishes, expected reactions, and response patterns; scan Bion's dependency, fight-flight, and pairing assumptions plus social defenses such as splitting, projection, displacement, denial, mirroring, and idealizing. Use coaching and 360-degree feedback to examine constructive versus reactive narcissism. Prefer a tested relational hypothesis over casual diagnosis, and combine psychodynamic insight with roles, incentives, power, technology, and structure.
- **Use ethical lenses** before high-power decisions: teleology (ethical egoism, utilitarianism, altruism) asks about consequences; deontology asks about duties, rights, promises, truth, fairness, and respect; virtue ethics asks what character and habits the action develops. Apply the five principles of ethical leadership as a behavioral audit: respect, service, justice, honesty, and community. Use the **toxic triangle** (Padilla, Hogan, & Kaiser, 2007) to inspect destructive leaders, susceptible followers, and conducive environments, including weak checks and balances. Prefer multiple lenses over a single shortcut because favorable outcomes do not excuse rights violations and rules do not remove responsibility for harm.

### Teams, inequality, and culture
- **Use the Hill Model for Team Leadership** as a monitor-diagnose-intervene loop. Build a mental model, decide whether to monitor or act, locate the need as internal or external and task or relational, choose the requisite behavior, and re-monitor. Internal task actions include goal focusing, structuring, decision facilitation, training, and standards; internal relational actions include coaching, collaboration, conflict and power management, commitment, needs, and ethical modeling; external actions include networking, advocacy, negotiation, buffering, assessment, and information sharing. Prefer shared or distributed leadership when expertise is fluid. Judge both performance and development; use Hackman's enabling conditions and Larson-LaFasto's team criteria before blaming members.
- **Use the leadership labyrinth** (Eagly & Carli, 2007) when gender gaps appear in advancement or selection. Map leaks at entry, development, promotion, and elite selection, including assignments, mentoring, visibility, negotiation, work-home structures, and opaque criteria. Pair role congruity theory with structured evidence, diverse reviewers, sponsorship, equitable leave, and individual, interpersonal, organizational, and societal remedies. Prefer a system audit over a deficit story about women; findings vary by role, evaluator, culture, and intersectionality.
- **Use GLOBE cultural calibration** (House, Hanges, Javidan, Dorfman, & Gupta, 2004) when leading across national, organizational, or virtual cultural boundaries. Treat the nine dimensions, 10 clusters, and six global leadership behaviors - charismatic/value-based, team-oriented, participative, humane-oriented, autonomous, and self-protective - as hypotheses about expectations, not labels for individuals. Ask followers about participation, autonomy, status, directness, humane support, and face. Prefer adapting expression while retaining integrity, trust, fairness, dignity, and competence; avoid ethnocentrism, stereotypes, and treating perception-centered GLOBE measures as a complete theory.

### Cross-theory selection rules
- Start with the process and power test when the issue is whether influence, authority, management, or coercion is being mistaken for leadership.
- Start with traits for role fit and self-awareness, skills for capability development, and behavior for observable task/relationship change.
- Prefer SLII for goal-specific competence and commitment; prefer Path-Goal for expectancy, motivation, task structure, and obstacle removal.
- Prefer LMX when unequal dyadic access is the problem; transformational, authentic, or servant leadership when meaning, trust, moral purpose, development, or service is central.
- Prefer adaptive leadership for learning and value change, psychodynamic analysis for hidden relational and emotional patterns, and ethical lenses for rights, duties, consequences, character, and common-good trade-offs.
- Prefer Hill when an interdependent team needs diagnosis and action, the labyrinth when advancement systems may be biased, and GLOBE when cultural expectations may alter how leadership is perceived or expressed.
- Across theories, distinguish association from causation, use instruments for feedback unless the chapter identifies stronger validation, and test claims against followers, outcomes, context, and current evidence.
