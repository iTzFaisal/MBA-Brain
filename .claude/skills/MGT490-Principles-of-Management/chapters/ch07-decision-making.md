# Chapter 7: Individual and Group Decision Making: How Managers Make Things Happen

## Core Idea
Decision making is identifying and choosing among alternatives. Managers should use a rational process when the situation permits, but recognize bounded rationality, intuition, bias, ethics, data limits, AI limits, and group dynamics when conditions are uncertain.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Rational decision-making model**: (1) identify the problem or opportunity, (2) generate alternatives, (3) evaluate alternatives and select a solution, (4) implement and evaluate the solution.
  - Evaluate alternatives for cost, quality, ethics, feasibility, and effectiveness.
- **Nonrational models**: Satisficing selects an acceptable solution when time or information makes optimization impractical; intuition relies on expertise, hunches, or automated experience.
- **Ethical decision tree**: Ask whether the action is legal, whether it maximizes shareholder value, whether it is ethical considering stakeholders, and whether not taking a non-value-maximizing action would itself be unethical.
- **Evidence-based management**: Translate the best available evidence into organizational practice. Treat the organization as an unfinished prototype, use facts rather than hype, see the organization from the outside, involve all levels, sell the approach, slow bad practices, and ask what happens when people fail.
- **Four decision-making styles**: Directive (facts and action), analytical (information and alternatives), conceptual (intuition and long-term possibilities), and behavioral (people and relationships). Styles combine value orientation with tolerance for ambiguity.
- **Ten biases**: Availability, representativeness, confirmation, sunk cost, anchoring and adjustment, overconfidence, hindsight, framing, escalation of commitment, and categorical thinking.
- **Group problem-solving techniques**: Brainstorming, devil's advocacy, the dialectic method, and the project post-mortem.

## Key Concepts
- **Problem**: A difficulty that inhibits goal achievement.
- **Opportunity**: A situation that permits performance beyond existing goals.
- **Bounded rationality**: Limits on information, time, and cognitive capacity.
- **Big data**: Large, varied data sets including transaction, web, social, sensor, and surveillance data.
- **Data analytics**: Examining data to find hidden patterns and correlations.
- **Artificial intelligence**: Computer systems performing tasks that normally require human intelligence.
- **Groupthink**: Pressure for unanimity that prevents accurate assessment.
- **Consensus**: Agreement to support a decision after members express views; it is not necessarily unanimity.

## Mental Models
- Diagnose the gap between the actual and desirable state before jumping to solutions.
- Use intuition for pattern-rich situations where relevant expertise exists, not as a substitute for evidence in unfamiliar problems.
- Treat dissent as information: ask what a critic, outsider, or minority view sees that the group is missing.

## Anti-patterns
- **Rational-model fantasy**: Assuming complete information, unlimited time, and unemotional optimization.
- **Bias confirmation**: Searching for support for a preferred answer while ignoring contrary evidence.
- **Sunk-cost escalation**: Continuing a failing project because resources have already been spent.
- **Data worship**: Treating data volume as evidence quality.
- **Groupthink**: Silencing dissent for the appearance of agreement.
- **AI without governance**: Deploying systems without reliable data, bias checks, human oversight, or cost analysis.

## Worked Example
Howard Schultz returned to Starbucks in 2008 after the company had lost its "soul," extended existing products instead of creating new ones, and suffered during recession. He closed 800 stores, laid off 4,000 employees, replaced executives, and brought 10,000 store managers to New Orleans for strategy work and community service. Starbucks later improved operations, technology, staffing, products, mobile payments, and store offerings. The case shows diagnosis, difficult alternatives, implementation, feedback, and values-based decision making.

## Key Takeaways
1. Separate diagnosis, alternatives, selection, implementation, and evaluation.
2. Match decision effort to complexity: strategic, significant, or quick decisions.
3. Use evidence, ethical tests, and competing viewpoints to counter bias.
4. Give groups a clear decision process, minority voice, and an explicit way to challenge assumptions.
5. Review failures without blame and convert them into process improvements.
