# Chapter 3: The Manager's Changing Work Environment and Ethical Responsibilities: Doing the Right Thing

## Core Idea
Managers operate inside a network of stakeholders and environmental forces. Exceptional management therefore evaluates people, planet, and profit together, makes ethical choices using explicit standards, and builds governance systems that protect owners and stakeholders.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Triple bottom line**: Evaluate organizational success through people, planet, and profit rather than financial results alone.
  - When to use: When a decision creates social or environmental effects that financial accounting alone hides.
  - How: Identify affected stakeholders, estimate social and environmental impacts, compare them with financial outcomes, and use a social audit to track performance.
- **Stakeholder environments**: Internal stakeholders are employees, owners, and the board. The external task environment includes customers, competitors, suppliers, distributors, strategic allies, employee organizations, local communities, financial institutions, government regulators, and special-interest groups. The general environment includes economic, technological, sociocultural, demographic, political-legal, and international forces.
- **Four approaches to ethical dilemmas**: Utilitarian (greatest good), individual (long-term self-interest), moral-rights (protect fundamental rights), and justice (fairness and equality).
- **Kohlberg's moral development**: Preconventional reasoning follows punishment and self-interest; conventional reasoning follows social expectations and rules; postconventional reasoning uses internal principles.
- **Carroll's social-responsibility priorities**: Be a good global corporate citizen, act ethically, obey the law, and make a profit. The approaches can conflict and require judgment.
- **Corporate governance**: The system for governing a company so owners' and other stakeholders' interests are protected.

## Key Concepts
- **Ethics**: Standards of right and wrong that influence behavior.
- **Values**: Deeply held beliefs and attitudes that shape behavior.
- **Social responsibility**: A manager's duty to take actions benefiting society as well as the organization.
- **Task environment**: External stakeholders presenting daily operational demands.
- **General environment**: Broad forces that influence the organization indirectly.
- **Kohlberg's moral development**: Preconventional, conventional, and postconventional levels of moral reasoning.
- **Ethical climate**: Shared perceptions about what conduct is expected, supported, and rewarded.
- **Sarbanes-Oxley (SarbOx)**: U.S. legislation responding to white-collar scandals through stronger financial reporting and control responsibilities.

## Mental Models
- Treat ethical analysis as a four-lens check, not a personal feeling: consequences, self-interest, rights, and justice.
- Separate the immediate task environment from broad environmental trends; they require different monitoring routines.
- Make ethics structural: screen for values, train, reward ethical conduct, and hold leaders accountable.

## Anti-patterns
- **Shareholder-only tunnel vision**: Ignoring stakeholders creates hidden costs, legal exposure, and loss of trust.
- **Code-only ethics**: Publishing a code without incentives, training, reporting channels, or leadership modeling.
- **Moral outsourcing**: Treating law or a consultant's opinion as a substitute for ethical judgment.
- **Governance theater**: Appointing a board or ethics officer without independence, accountability, reporting protection, or incentive alignment.

## Worked Example
In the college-admissions scandal, wealthy parents, coaches, consultants, universities, students, regulators, and the public were all stakeholders. A utilitarian analysis weighs access and harm across the system; a rights analysis asks whether honest applicants were denied fair opportunity; a justice analysis examines unequal treatment; and a governance analysis asks why controls and incentives failed. The case shows that a private gain can be unethical even when a participant believes no one will notice. It also illustrates why codes and formal policies are insufficient without reporting channels, oversight, and consequences.

## Key Takeaways
1. Map stakeholders before deciding; those absent from the meeting may bear the largest cost.
2. Use all four ethical approaches when a dilemma is high-impact or contested.
3. Measure social and environmental performance rather than treating it as public-relations language.
4. Governance must connect authority, oversight, incentives, and consequences.
