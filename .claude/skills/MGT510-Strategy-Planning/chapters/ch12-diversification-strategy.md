# Chapter 12: Diversification Strategy

## Core Idea
Diversification creates value only when this firm can own and manage a new business more profitably than the business could operate independently or under another owner. Growth and risk reduction are not sufficient motives; the source of synergy, the need for ownership, and the cost of complexity must be explicit.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Economies of scope and internalization**: Identify shared tangible resources, intangible assets, capabilities, or demand-side benefits. Compare ownership with licensing, contracting, partnership, or leasing; internalize when market contracting is costly or cannot protect tacit know-how.
- **Porter's three essential tests**:
  1. **Attractiveness**: The target industry is attractive or can be made attractive.
  2. **Cost of entry**: Entry cost does not capitalize all future profits.
  3. **Better-off**: The new unit gains advantage from the corporation, or the corporation gains from the unit.
- **Parenting advantage**: Ask whether this parent adds more value than any realistic alternative parent, not merely whether the businesses are better together.
- **Strategic relatedness**: Test similarity in resource allocation, investment horizons and risk, management skills, KSFs, life-cycle stages, competitive positions, performance indicators, and target horizons. Product similarity alone is weak evidence.
- **Internal markets**: Evaluate whether internal capital and labor markets reduce financing, hiring, and information costs, while accounting for political allocation, cross-subsidies, and retention problems.

## Key Concepts
- **Economies of scope**: Fewer resources are required when one resource is used across multiple activities.
- **Better-off test**: The corporate relationship must create competitive advantage or a corporate gain.
- **Parenting advantage**: The parent can add more value than alternative owners.
- **Strategic relatedness**: Similarity in management tasks and decision systems, not just products.
- **Dominant logic**: Shared corporate view of identity and purpose that helps integrate businesses.
- **Internal capital market**: Corporate allocation of funds among businesses.
- **Conglomerate discount**: Lower valuation associated with a diversified portfolio when complexity or misallocation destroys value.
- **Refocusing**: Divesting businesses to restore focus and performance.

## Mental Models
- **Three-gate screen**: Attractive industry, affordable entry, and a better-off relationship.
- **Parenting auction**: Compare the current parent with the best alternative parent.
- **Synergy causal chain**: Shared resource or capability -> cost or demand benefit -> advantage -> measurable incremental cash flow.
- **Inverted-U portfolio**: Additional businesses help until complexity and coordination costs exceed synergy.

## Anti-patterns
- Diversifying to satisfy executive growth or empire-building ambitions.
- Treating smoother cash flows as automatic shareholder value.
- Paying an acquisition premium that captures all expected synergy.
- Using "synergy" without naming the resource, mechanism, and financial effect.
- Assuming related products automatically create economies of scope.
- Owning a business when licensing or partnership would capture the benefit more cheaply.
- Protecting weak divisions through internal capital markets or political influence.
- Retaining a business whose best value-creating parent is someone else.

## Worked Example
A cable company considering home security should test industry attractiveness, acquisition price, integration cost, and measurable benefits from its network, billing system, customer base, and technicians. It should compare acquisition with licensing or distribution. Ownership is stronger if service quality depends on tacit coordination; contracting is better if the security provider has the critical capability. Proceed only if synergy and parenting value exceed premium, complexity, and execution risk.

## Key Takeaways
1. Diversification is an ownership decision, not a growth objective.
2. Name the specific economy of scope, internalization benefit, demand synergy, or parenting capability.
3. Apply the three essential tests and then compare alternative parents.
4. Assess relatedness through strategic management similarities and causal mechanisms.
5. Treat portfolio breadth as costly and refocusing as a legitimate value-creation option.
