# Chapter 2: Goals, Values, and Performance

## Core Idea
Strategy is a quest to create and capture value over the long term. Profit is necessary for survival, but enterprise value depends on future free cash flow, the cost of capital, customer value, stakeholder relationships, and the options created by current investments.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Stakeholder theory**: Map value and obligations across customers, employees, suppliers, owners, society, and the natural environment; make tradeoffs explicit.
- **Economic Value Added (EVA)**: Calculate economic profit as `operating profit - (WACC x capital employed)` to test whether returns exceed the cost of capital.
- **Discounted Cash Flow (DCF)**: Value the firm as the present value of future free cash flows discounted at WACC; use it to avoid short-term performance distortion.
- **Disaggregating Return on Assets**: Diagnose `ROA = profit margin x asset turnover`, then trace margins and asset use to operational drivers.
- **Balanced Scorecard**: Combine financial, customer, internal-process, and learning measures so targets support strategy rather than invite gaming.
- **Strategic profit drivers and obliquity**: Pursue the operational and customer drivers of profit rather than making profit the only direct target.
- **Strategic CSR / shared value**: Prefer social initiatives that reinforce products, value-chain productivity, or local competitive clusters.
- **Real options / strategy as options management**: Stage commitments so the firm can continue, abandon, modify, scale, or defer as uncertainty resolves.

## Key Concepts
- **Value creation**: Total customer value minus the real resources required to produce an offering.
- **Value capture**: The share of created value retained by the firm after customers, suppliers, employees, and rivals claim their portions.
- **Enterprise value**: The present value of future cash flows, broadly reflecting equity and debt value.
- **Economic profit**: Accounting profit after charging for capital employed.
- **Performance diagnosis**: Moving from an observed result to the operational causes that explain it.
- **Target dysfunction**: Narrow measures can trigger gaming, manipulation, quality reduction, and short-termism.
- **Option value**: The value of preserving the right, but not the obligation, to make a larger future commitment.

## Mental Models
- **Profitability tree**: Start with ROA or ROCE, split margin from turnover, then trace each branch to actionable causes.
- **Medical diagnosis**: Assess performance, diagnose causes, then prescribe strategy; do not jump from a symptom to an intervention.
- **Obliquity**: Improve the drivers of profit and long-run trust rather than pressuring the organization to maximize profit directly.
- **Option ladder**: Use small, staged commitments to buy information before committing irreversible capital.

## Anti-patterns
- Maximizing quarterly earnings or share price at the expense of enterprise value.
- Treating accounting profit as proof that capital is productive.
- Relying on one ratio without industry, competitor, trend, and cost-of-capital comparisons.
- Setting precise targets that reward gaming rather than customer or strategic outcomes.
- Listing stakeholder goals without priorities, tradeoffs, or accountability.
- Committing fully to an uncertain project when staged investment is available.

## Worked Example
The UPS-FedEx comparison illustrates diagnosis. UPS had higher ROA because it combined a stronger operating margin with higher asset turnover. The useful question was not simply whether to cut labor, but which integrated ground-delivery choices produced lower purchased-transportation, fuel, maintenance, depreciation, and administrative costs while using assets more effectively. A balanced target set would combine ROCE, turnover, delivery reliability, customer satisfaction, and service quality.

## Key Takeaways
1. Anchor strategic evaluation in long-run value creation and capture.
2. Use economic profit to test whether returns exceed the cost of capital.
3. Decompose performance until the diagnosis reaches controllable drivers.
4. Balance financial targets with customer, process, and learning measures.
5. Use strategic CSR and real-options thinking when immediate profit is an incomplete guide.
