# Chapter 13: Implementing Corporate Strategy: Managing the Multibusiness Firm

## Core Idea
Corporate headquarters creates value only when it adds more value than it destroys through overhead, complexity, coordination costs, and managerial interference. Its main roles are managing the portfolio, exploiting linkages, improving individual businesses, and enabling strategic change.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Parenting advantage / Ashridge Portfolio Display**: Plot the parent's potential to add value against its potential to destroy value through overhead or misfit.
  - **Heartland**: High value-adding potential and low destruction risk; own and actively parent.
  - **Edge of heartland**: Test interventions carefully.
  - **Ballast**: Good fit but little parenting upside; maintain efficiently.
  - **Value trap**: Apparent upside with serious misfit risk; repair or exit.
  - **Alien territory**: Little upside and high destruction risk; do not acquire or divest.
- **GE/McKinsey nine-box**: Screen businesses on industry attractiveness and competitive advantage; use market size, growth, margins, international potential, market share, quality, technology, and cost.
- **BCG growth-share matrix**: Use market growth and relative market share as rough proxies for attractiveness and advantage. Treat quadrants as an initial screen, not an automatic decision.
- **Resource-sharing and capability-transfer framework**: Compare value chains, centralize common services where scale or learning economies are real, and actively sponsor transfer across businesses.
- **Input versus output control**: Use strategic planning control for related, technology-intensive businesses with long investments; use financial control for mature, less-related businesses where autonomy and output accountability matter.
- **M-form and holding-company governance**: Use multidivisional structures for corporate allocation and accountability; use holding companies when ownership and financial autonomy matter more than operating integration.

## Key Concepts
- **Parenting advantage**: Value created through management expertise, resource sharing, capability transfer, or transaction-cost savings.
- **Vertical value-added**: Improvement in an individual business independent of interbusiness synergy.
- **Internal capital market**: Corporate allocation of funds using opportunity and performance rather than equal shares or politics.
- **Strategic inflection point**: External shift requiring fundamental redirection.
- **Dynamic corporate capability**: Sensing change, reallocating resources, launching businesses, and exiting declining ones.
- **M-form**: Corporate headquarters separated from relatively autonomous divisions.
- **Holding company**: Parent control through ownership and boards while subsidiaries retain operational and financial independence.

## Mental Models
- **HQ as investor, orchestrator, coach, and catalyst**: Match the corporate role to the source of potential value.
- **Net parenting test**: Parent-added value minus overhead, complexity, misfit, and autonomy costs.
- **Control tradeoff**: Either control decisions and accept responsibility, or control outcomes and grant decision freedom.
- **Capital follows opportunity**: Reallocate resources toward changing prospects rather than preserving equal shares.

## Anti-patterns
- Acquiring without a credible parenting advantage.
- Using BCG or GE/McKinsey classifications as automatic investment decisions.
- Centralizing services merely because duplication exists.
- Assuming cross-selling or best-practice transfer happens automatically.
- Demanding entrepreneurial performance while micromanaging decisions.
- Applying short-term financial targets to businesses with long investment horizons.
- Retaining declining businesses because of attachment or organizational politics.

## Worked Example
Suppose a corporate group owns cloud security, bottled water, and industrial sensors. Cloud security may be heartland: invest with strategic planning and shared engineering. Bottled water may be ballast: use financial control and avoid intervention. Industrial sensors may be a value trap until a time-limited test shows whether engineering and industrial-sales capabilities transfer. Capital should follow expected value, while the board independently reviews risk, incentives, and exit decisions.

## Key Takeaways
1. Own businesses where the parent can add distinctive net value.
2. Match portfolio tools, resource sharing, and controls to business context.
3. Treat synergy as an actively managed process with measurable benefits.
4. Reallocate capital, develop new businesses, and exit when the parenting case disappears.
5. Governance must constrain self-interest without destroying entrepreneurial autonomy.
