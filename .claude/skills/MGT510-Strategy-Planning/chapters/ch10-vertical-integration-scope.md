# Chapter 10: Vertical Integration and the Scope of the Firm

## Core Idea
Vertical integration is a governance choice: own multiple stages of a value chain or coordinate them through external relationships. Internalize when avoided market transaction costs exceed internal administration costs; outsource when specialization, incentives, flexibility, and scale make markets or hybrids superior.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Transaction-cost framework**: Compare searching, negotiating, monitoring, enforcement, and hold-up costs in markets with administration, coordination, incentive, capability, and risk costs inside the firm.
- **Make-or-buy criteria**: Integration is more attractive with few trading partners, transaction-specific investment, uncertainty, and high coordination need. Outsourcing is more attractive with many suppliers, scale mismatch, strategic dissimilarity, volatile demand, specialist capability, and high-powered incentive needs.
- **Vertical-relationship framework**: Choose along two dimensions - resource commitment and formality - from spot contracts through long-term contracts, partnerships, franchising, and ownership.
- **System-capability test**: Integrate when advantage depends on continuous coordination across stages, such as Zara's design-to-retail cycle; retain architectural knowledge even when components are outsourced.
- **Hybrid governance ladder**: Use spot contracts, long-term contracts, relational partnerships, franchising, collaborative networks, partial integration, or full ownership according to commitment and coordination needs.

## Key Concepts
- **Bilateral monopoly**: Buyer and seller become mutually dependent, so bargaining replaces a competitive price.
- **Transaction-specific investment**: An asset whose value depends heavily on one trading relationship.
- **Hold-up**: Opportunistic exploitation after a specific investment has reduced the other party's alternatives.
- **Incomplete contract**: An agreement unable to specify every future event or adaptation.
- **High-powered incentives**: Market-based rewards tied to winning and retaining business.
- **System integrator**: Lead firm that coordinates specialists while retaining system-level control.
- **Architectural capability**: Knowledge of how components fit together.
- **Virtual integration**: Coordinated external relationships that achieve system benefits without common ownership.

## Mental Models
- **Governance substitution**: Integration substitutes administration for contracts; outsourcing substitutes contracts for hierarchy.
- **Lock-in test**: Specific investment plus uncertainty plus dependence creates the strongest case for integration or protected partnership.
- **Coupling test**: Integrate tightly coupled activities; outsource generic and independent ones.
- **Three-way tradeoff**: Evaluate coordination, specialization, and incentives together.

## Anti-patterns
- Outsourcing everything that is not presumed to be a core competence.
- Assuming physical co-location requires common ownership.
- Treating make-or-buy as a binary decision.
- Integrating for monopoly leverage without testing partner retaliation and added risk.
- Integrating a dissimilar activity below efficient scale.
- Outsourcing system-critical knowledge until architectural control is lost.
- Ignoring how the arrangement allocates risk and incentives.

## Worked Example
For an EV battery pack, standardized cells from many suppliers favor flexible contracts because demand is uncertain and switching is easy. A proprietary pack requiring specialized tooling and continuous engineering coordination favors integration or a protected long-term partnership. A hybrid may retain architecture, software, and system integration internally while sourcing standardized cells and specialized manufacturing externally.

## Key Takeaways
1. Compare total governance costs, not supplier price with internal production cost alone.
2. Specific investment, uncertainty, and coordination favor ownership or relational governance.
3. Scale differences, dissimilarity, specialist capability, volatile demand, and incentives favor outsourcing.
4. Hybrid relationships often outperform pure markets and full integration.
5. Outsourcing requires enough retained knowledge to coordinate and improve the system.
