# Chapter 11: Global Strategy and the Multinational Corporation

## Core Idea
Internationalization expands markets and access to resources but multiplies competitive complexity. Global strategy must coordinate activity location, entry mode, standardization, local adaptation, and multinational organization while matching firm-specific capabilities with national conditions.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Porter's National Diamond**: Analyze factor conditions, related and supporting industries, demand conditions, and domestic strategy/structure/rivalry to explain national competitive advantage.
- **Location of value-chain activities**: For each activity, compare input cost and availability, policy and risk, firm-specific capabilities, strategic fit, and coordination benefits. Locate country-dependent activities where resources exist and firm-dependent activities where capabilities transfer.
- **Foreign-market entry ladder**: Move from spot sales and agents to licensing/franchising, joint ventures, and wholly owned subsidiaries as resource commitment and control rise.
- **Five-factor entry test**: Match mode to source of advantage, tradability, capability completeness, appropriability, and transaction costs.
- **CAGE framework**: Diagnose cultural, administrative/political, geographic, and economic distance before transferring a home-market formula.
- **Global integration versus national differentiation**: Standardize where scale economies are high and differences are low; adapt where regulation, culture, distribution, or preferences matter.
- **Ghemawat's AAA triangle**: Pursue **aggregation** for scale, **adaptation** for local fit, and **arbitrage** for favorable resource or institutional differences. Each requires different organizational solutions.
- **Transnational corporation / integrated network**: Use when the firm must combine global scale, local responsiveness, and distributed learning through a network of differentiated subsidiaries.

## Key Concepts
- **Internationalization**: Extension of business across national borders.
- **Trading industry**: Internationalized mainly through exports.
- **Multidomestic industry**: Requires direct investment because products or services are nationally differentiated.
- **Global industry**: High trade and high foreign direct investment.
- **Sheltered industry**: Protected by regulation, trade barriers, perishability, or localized demand.
- **Value-chain fragmentation**: Locating different activities in different countries.
- **Liability of foreignness**: Disadvantages from unfamiliarity with a host environment.
- **Globalization penalty**: Coordination and administrative costs that can make a multinational less effective than a strong local firm.

## Mental Models
- **Activity-specific location**: Ask which country is best for each activity, not which country is best overall.
- **Control-commitment ladder**: Increase foreign commitment only when control value exceeds risk and transaction cost.
- **Selective globalization**: Standardize, adapt, or arbitrage by activity, product, and market.
- **MNC as network**: Subsidiaries are local operators and potential global sources of knowledge.

## Anti-patterns
- Assuming home capabilities transfer automatically.
- Treating low wages as the only location advantage.
- Using one entry mode everywhere.
- Forcing a single global product despite cultural, regulatory, and channel differences.
- Fragmenting activities without coordination and resilience discipline.
- Preserving historical administrative structures after conditions change.

## Worked Example
McDonald's standardizes brand values, franchising, training, core operating practices, and supplier relationships while adapting menus and practices to local culture and regulation. Local units can generate innovations that diffuse through the network. The rule is to globalize activities with scale and brand benefits and localize choices where distance makes adaptation valuable.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use the National Diamond to explain how environments support international advantage.
2. Locate each value-chain activity according to resources, strategy, coordination, and risk.
3. Match entry mode to the source of advantage and required control.
4. Apply CAGE before assuming a successful formula travels.
5. Build an integrated network that combines scale, responsiveness, arbitrage, and learning.
