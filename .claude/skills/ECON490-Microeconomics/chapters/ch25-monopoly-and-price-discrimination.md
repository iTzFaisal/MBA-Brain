# Chapter 25: Monopoly and Price Discrimination

## Core Idea
A monopolist is a price maker protected by a barrier to entry, so it faces the market demand curve and must lower price to sell more. It maximizes profit at `MR = MC`, then uses demand to set price; relative to competition, this produces a higher price, lower quantity, rent-seeking waste, and deadweight loss, although patents may trade temporary monopoly for innovation.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Monopoly output-and-price rule**: Use when one firm serves a market without close substitutes. Find the quantity where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, move up to the demand curve to find the price, and compute profit as `(price - average cost) * quantity`. Because lowering price applies to previous customers as well as the new customer, `MR < P` beyond the first unit.
- **Marginal-revenue good-news/bad-news decomposition**: Use to explain why monopoly MR lies below demand. Selling one more unit adds the new price but loses revenue on all earlier units whose price must be cut. For a linear demand curve, `MR = new price + (slope of demand curve * old quantity)`.
- **Monopoly-versus-competition surplus test**: Use to measure social cost. Competition expands output until price equals marginal cost; monopoly stops earlier at `MR = MC`. The monopoly profit is a transfer from consumers, but the surplus from units not produced is a deadweight loss.
- **Rent-seeking framework**: Use when firms spend resources to obtain or preserve a barrier to entry. Lobbying, political payments, and exclusive privileges can consume the monopoly profit without producing additional value.
- **Patent trade-off**: Use when evaluating innovation policy. A patent raises price and restricts output, but may make a product worth developing when research and development costs would otherwise exceed the temporary monopoly profit. If the product would have been developed without a patent, the patent may merely prolong an inefficient monopoly.
- **Segmented price-discrimination rule**: Use when a firm has market power, can identify groups with different willingness to pay or elasticity, and can prevent resale. Apply `MR = MC` separately in each segment; charge the higher price to the group with less elastic demand and the lower price to the more elastic group.

## Key Concepts
- **Monopoly**: A market in which one firm sells a product without close substitutes.
- **Market power**: Ability to affect the price of a product.
- **Barrier to entry**: Something that prevents firms from entering a profitable market.
- **Marginal revenue**: Change in total revenue from selling one more unit.
- **Rent seeking**: Using resources or public policy to gain monopoly profit.
- **Deadweight loss from monopoly**: Decrease in market surplus caused by monopoly's restricted output.
- **Patent**: Exclusive right to sell a new good for a specified period.
- **Network externalities**: Product value that increases with the number of other users.
- **Price discrimination**: Selling the same good at different prices to different consumers.
- **Natural monopoly**: A market in which scale economies are so large that one firm can serve the market at lower average cost.

## Mental Models
- **Use `MR = MC`, not `P = MC`, for the monopoly quantity**: The price is the reward on the demand curve, but the marginal benefit of expanding output is the lower marginal revenue after price cuts.
- **Use the elasticity segmentation rule**: If groups can be separated and resale prevented, discount the more elastic group and charge more to the less elastic group.
- **Use the innovation counterfactual for patents**: Compare the product with no patent, not the monopoly outcome with the competitive outcome alone; the relevant alternative may be no product.
- **Use the rent-seeking screen**: Count lobbying, exclusive licenses, political payments, and defensive spending as real resource costs, not merely transfers.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming monopoly inefficiency is only a transfer from consumers to the firm**: The unproduced units that would have generated positive net benefits create a separate deadweight loss.
- **Setting price where demand equals marginal cost**: That is not the monopolist's profit rule when demand slopes downward.
- **Calling a patent automatically socially beneficial**: It is beneficial only when the innovation would otherwise not be developed or would be materially delayed.
- **Interpreting a discount as generosity**: Price discrimination is a profit-maximizing response to different elasticities and willingness to pay.
- **Ignoring resale and identification**: Without separable groups and resale barriers, different posted prices will be arbitraged away.

## Worked Example
Curall, a patented cold medicine, faces a downward-sloping market demand curve and has the cost data shown in the textbook. The marginal-revenue curve intersects marginal cost at 900 doses per hour. The demand curve says consumers will buy those 900 doses at $15 each, while average cost is $8. Profit is therefore `($15 - $8) * 900 = $6,300` per hour. Producing 600 doses would leave marginal revenue at $12 and marginal cost at $4, so expanding output adds profit. At 1,000 doses, marginal revenue falls to $4 while marginal cost rises to $6.70, so the extra dose reduces profit. The firm must first choose the quantity with `MR = MC`; it cannot choose a price independently of the quantity on demand.

## Key Takeaways
1. Identify the entry barrier and the firm's market demand before analyzing pricing.
2. Choose the monopoly quantity at `MR = MC`, then read the corresponding price from demand.
3. Separate transferred consumer surplus, deadweight loss, and rent-seeking resource costs.
4. Evaluate patents against the no-innovation alternative and the likely duration of monopoly profit.
5. For price discrimination, verify market power, identifiable demand differences, and no resale before segmenting prices.
6. Charge relatively lower prices to more elastic groups when marginal cost is shared across segments.
