# Chapter 14: The Federal Reserve and Monetary Policy

## Core Idea
The Federal Reserve controls the supply of money and influences short-term interest rates through the money market. Expansionary monetary policy lowers rates, raises investment and interest-sensitive consumption, can depreciate the currency and raise net exports, and increases short-run GDP. Long lags, expectations, bond prices, exchange rates, and international spillovers make the transmission powerful but uncertain.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Money-market demand and supply**: Use a downward-sloping money-demand curve because the interest rate is the opportunity cost of holding noninterest-bearing money. Money demand rises with the price level and real GDP. With a Fed-determined vertical money supply, their intersection determines the nominal short-term interest rate.
- **Federal Reserve policy tools**: Use open market purchases to increase reserves and money supply, lower rates, and stimulate; use sales for the reverse. Reserve requirements, interest on reserves, the discount rate, and quantitative easing are additional tools. Open market operations are the primary conventional tool.
- **Bond-price/present-value rule**: A bond's price equals the present value of promised future payments: `price = promised payment / (1 + interest rate)` for a one-period payment. Rates and bond prices move inversely; Fed purchases raise bond demand and prices while lowering yields.
- **Monetary transmission mechanism**: Trace `open-market purchase -> money supply rises -> interest rates fall -> investment and durable-goods spending rise -> AD rises -> short-run GDP and prices rise`. In the long run, price adjustment limits the real-output effect.
- **Open-economy channel and expectations**: Lower domestic rates send funds abroad, depreciate the currency, make domestic exports cheaper and imports more expensive, increase net exports, and add to GDP. Long-term rates depend on expected future short-term rates, so Fed communication and quantitative easing affect expectations.
- **Policy-lag framework**: Inside lags are relatively short because the FOMC can act frequently; outside lags can be long, often around two years for most effects. Forecast errors can make a timely-looking decision destabilizing.

## Key Concepts
- **Money market**: Market in which money demand and supply determine the nominal interest rate.
- **Transaction demand**: Demand for money to facilitate purchases.
- **Liquidity demand**: Demand for readily accessible funds for transactions on short notice.
- **Speculative demand**: Demand for safer money-like assets when stocks or bonds seem risky.
- **Open market operations**: Fed purchases or sales of government securities.
- **Discount rate**: Rate banks pay when borrowing directly from the Fed.
- **Federal funds market/rate**: Market/rate for banks lending reserves to one another.
- **Quantitative easing**: Fed purchases of longer-term securities to lower long-term rates.
- **Depreciation/appreciation**: Decrease/increase in a currency's value.
- **Monetary policy**: Fed actions intended to influence GDP or inflation.

## Mental Models
- **Use opportunity cost to predict money demand**: Higher interest rates make idle money more expensive to hold, so quantity demanded falls.
- **Use the transmission chain before claiming stimulus**: Identify the money, rate, investment, exchange-rate, net-export, and AD links that must work.
- **Use present value to read bond news**: A fixed future payment is worth less today when the market discount rate rises.
- **Use expectations as a second policy instrument**: A short-term rate cut has larger long-term effects when markets believe future short-term rates will also remain low.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating the Fed as directly controlling every long-term borrowing rate**: Traditional operations directly target very short-term rates; long-term rates depend on expectations and risk.
- **Saying bond prices and interest rates move together**: A fixed promised payment means their present-value relationship is inverse.
- **Ignoring the exchange-rate channel in an open economy**: Monetary policy affects GDP through net exports as well as domestic investment.
- **Assuming a reserve injection automatically becomes circulating money**: Banks may hold excess reserves and borrowers may not demand loans.
- **Ignoring outside lags**: A policy that is correct for today's data can be wrong when its full effects arrive.

## Worked Example
In quantitative easing, the Fed purchased long-term government bonds and mortgage-backed securities by writing checks on itself. A seller deposited the check, raising bank reserves. With a 10 percent reserve ratio, a $1 million increase in reserves could support $900,000 of new loans under the simplified textbook mechanism. The purchases also raised demand and prices for the securities, lowering their yields directly. The textbook reports that the Fed's balance sheet grew from less than $1 trillion to about $4.5 trillion by the end of the 2014 program, with a much larger mortgage-security share. The policy illustrates both channels: reserve creation and direct influence on long-term asset prices.

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat the interest rate as the opportunity cost of holding money.
2. Use open market purchases for expansionary policy and sales for contractionary policy, then trace the full chain to GDP.
3. Remember that bond prices are present values and move inversely with market rates.
4. Add depreciation/appreciation and net exports when analyzing an open economy.
5. Evaluate monetary policy with lags, expectations, loan demand, excess reserves, and forecast uncertainty in view.
