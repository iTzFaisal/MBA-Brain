# Chapter 16: The Dynamics of Inflation and Unemployment

## Core Idea
At full employment, money growth determines long-run inflation rather than real GDP. In the short run, unexpected inflation can move unemployment below or above the natural rate, producing the expectations Phillips curve; once expectations adjust, unemployment returns to its natural rate. Credible central banks, stable fiscal finance, and controlled money growth prevent inflation from becoming self-reinforcing or hyperinflationary.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Steady-state money-growth framework**: Use when inflation and expectations have settled. If the Fed grows money at 5 percent and real output is fixed at potential, inflation is 5 percent; nominal wages and money demand also grow 5 percent, while real wages and real interest rates remain unchanged. The nominal interest rate is approximately `real interest rate + expected inflation`.
- **Money-growth transition**: Use for a change in monetary-policy growth. A sudden reduction in money growth while inflation expectations remain high initially raises real and nominal interest rates, lowers investment and GDP, and raises unemployment. In the long run, inflation and nominal rates fall to match the lower money-growth rate and real output returns to potential.
- **Expectations Phillips curve**: Use to relate unemployment and inflation in the short run. If actual inflation exceeds expected inflation, workers may mistake nominal wage gains for real gains, unemployment falls below the natural rate, and a boom results. Once expectations catch up, unemployment returns to the natural rate; there is no permanent inflation-unemployment trade-off.
- **Rational-expectations and credibility framework**: Use when policy changes are anticipated. Rational expectations mean the public uses available information and forecasts correctly on average; a credible central bank can deter wage and price escalation because firms and unions expect it not to accommodate inflation.
- **Quantity equation and velocity**: Use to connect money to nominal spending: `M * V = P * y`; `velocity = nominal GDP / money supply`. In growth rates, `money growth + velocity growth = inflation + real-output growth`.
- **Hyperinflation and fiscal finance**: Use when money growth becomes extreme. `government deficit = new borrowing from the public + new money created`; if a government cannot borrow and monetizes persistent deficits, money loses its store-of-value function, velocity rises, and hyperinflation follows. Ending it requires closing the deficit and stopping excessive money creation.

## Key Concepts
- **Nominal wage**: Wage expressed in current dollars.
- **Real wage**: Wage adjusted for purchasing power.
- **Money illusion**: Confusion between nominal and real magnitudes.
- **Expectations of inflation**: Public beliefs about future inflation.
- **Expectations Phillips curve**: Short-run relationship between unemployment and inflation after accounting for expected inflation.
- **Natural rate of unemployment**: Rate consistent with full employment and no cyclical unemployment.
- **Rational expectations**: Theory that expectations use available information and are correct on average.
- **Velocity of money**: Rate at which money turns over, equal to nominal GDP divided by money supply.
- **Quantity equation**: `M * V = P * y`, linking money, velocity, prices, and real output.
- **Seignorage/hyperinflation**: Revenue from money creation / inflation exceeding 50 percent per month.

## Mental Models
- **Use the nominal-real decomposition**: When wages, rates, or money grow, subtract inflation to identify the purchasing-power or real-return effect.
- **Use expected versus actual inflation to predict employment**: Unexpectedly high inflation temporarily lowers perceived real wages and unemployment; expected inflation does not create a lasting employment gain.
- **Use credibility as a private-sector incentive**: A central bank that will not accommodate wage-price increases can reduce the wage demands and price increases that create inflation.
- **Use the quantity-equation accounting identity**: Given money and velocity growth, solve for the inflation rate after subtracting real-output growth.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating nominal wage growth as real wage growth**: A 5 percent wage increase with 5 percent inflation leaves purchasing power unchanged.
- **Expecting a permanent Phillips-curve trade-off**: Workers and firms revise expectations, returning unemployment to the natural rate.
- **Assuming lower money growth is painless immediately**: Expectations can remain high, so real rates rise and output falls before inflation adjusts.
- **Explaining hyperinflation only with careless pricing**: Persistent fiscal deficits financed by money creation are the core mechanism.
- **Ignoring velocity**: Money growth alone does not map one-for-one into inflation when money turnover changes.

## Worked Example
Suppose money grows 10 percent per year, velocity is constant, and real output grows 3 percent. The growth form of the quantity equation gives `10% + 0% = inflation + 3%`, so inflation is 7 percent. If velocity instead grows 1 percent, inflation is 8 percent. In the textbook's steady-state logic, the same money growth also raises nominal wages and expected nominal interest rates, but it does not permanently raise real wages, real interest rates, or output. To reduce inflation durably, policymakers must reduce money growth and keep fiscal deficits from forcing new money creation.

## Key Takeaways
1. Separate nominal growth from real growth before interpreting wages, interest rates, or money balances.
2. Expect a money-growth reduction to raise real rates and unemployment temporarily, then lower inflation and nominal rates in the long run.
3. Use the expectations Phillips curve only for short-run surprises, not a permanent unemployment target.
4. Anchor inflation expectations with credible, independent, and consistent central-bank policy.
5. Apply `M * V = P * y` and its growth version before attributing inflation to money alone.
6. Stop hyperinflation by closing the fiscal deficit and ending its monetization.
