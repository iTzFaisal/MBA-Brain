# Chapter 10: Fiscal Policy

## Core Idea
Fiscal policy changes government spending and taxes to influence aggregate demand, output, and employment. Expansionary policy can close a recessionary gap and contractionary policy can cool an overheated economy, but multipliers, inside and outside lags, forecast uncertainty, budget structure, and long-run crowding out make real-world stabilization much harder than a graph suggests.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Expansionary and contractionary fiscal policy**: Use expansionary policy (higher government purchases or lower taxes) when output is below potential; it shifts AD right. Use contractionary policy (lower purchases or higher taxes) when output exceeds potential; it shifts AD left. The same tax can also affect long-run aggregate supply through work, saving, and investment incentives.
- **Fiscal multiplier framework**: Use the multiplier to size a policy rather than equating the policy amount with the GDP effect. Spending increases AD directly and has multiplier `1/(1 - MPC)` in the simple model. A tax increase first reduces disposable income, so the tax multiplier is `-MPC/(1 - MPC)` and is smaller in absolute value.
- **Stabilization-policy lag test**: Use inside lags for recognizing and legislating a response, and outside lags for the response to affect spending and GDP. A policy that arrives after an economy has self-corrected can amplify rather than dampen fluctuations.
- **Federal-budget architecture**: Classify spending as discretionary, entitlement and mandatory, or net interest; distinguish federal purchases, which enter GDP, from transfer payments, which affect income but do not directly represent current production. Distinguish a one-year deficit or surplus from accumulated debt.
- **Automatic stabilizers and supply-side/Laffer analysis**: Use taxes and transfers that move automatically with income to cushion recessions and booms. Use supply-side analysis when tax rates alter labor, saving, investment, or output; the Laffer curve is plausible only where high rates strongly discourage the taxable activity.

## Key Concepts
- **Fiscal policy**: Changes in government taxes and spending that affect GDP.
- **Expansionary policy**: Government action intended to increase aggregate demand.
- **Contractionary policy**: Government action intended to decrease aggregate demand.
- **Fiscal multiplier**: Total change in GDP divided by the initial spending or tax change.
- **Stabilization policy**: Action intended to move GDP toward potential output.
- **Inside lag**: Time needed to recognize a problem and formulate or pass policy.
- **Outside lag**: Time from policy implementation to its economic effect.
- **Automatic stabilizer**: Tax or transfer mechanism that dampens GDP fluctuations without new legislation.
- **Budget deficit/surplus**: Spending above/below revenue in a given year.
- **Laffer curve**: Relationship in which sufficiently high tax rates can reduce the taxable base and revenue.

## Mental Models
- **Use the output-gap test before choosing direction**: Compare actual GDP with potential GDP; do not choose expansion merely because growth is slow in a single month.
- **Use the multiplier-adjusted dose**: Divide the desired GDP change by the relevant multiplier, then consider leakages and implementation delays.
- **Use automatic stabilizers as the first line of defense**: Let falling taxes and rising transfers cushion a downturn before adding discretionary policy.
- **Use the deficit time-horizon test**: A recession deficit can stabilize short-run demand; a persistent full-employment deficit can crowd out investment and future growth.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating every deficit as evidence of bad policy**: Recessions automatically lower tax revenue and increase transfers; the resulting deficit can be stabilizing.
- **Ignoring the sign and size of the tax multiplier**: A tax cut does not raise GDP dollar-for-dollar because households save part of the additional income.
- **Assuming permanent and temporary tax changes have identical effects**: Households may save a temporary rebate when they base spending on permanent income.
- **Confusing spending, revenue, deficit, and debt**: Spending and revenue are flows; debt is the accumulated stock of borrowing.
- **Calling a tax cut purely demand-side or supply-side**: Actual tax packages can affect consumption, labor supply, saving, investment, and expectations simultaneously.

## Worked Example
Suppose GDP is $6,000 billion, `MPC = 0.6`, and policymakers want to raise GDP by $60 billion, or 1 percent. The government-spending multiplier is `1/(1 - 0.6) = 2.5`, so an increase in purchases of `$60 / 2.5 = $24 billion` meets the target. The tax multiplier is `-0.6/(1 - 0.6) = -1.5`, so a tax cut of `$60 / 1.5 = $40 billion` is required. If policymakers raise government spending and taxes by the same $60 billion, the balanced-budget multiplier is one and GDP rises by $60 billion, but only if the policy arrives before the gap closes and the model's assumptions are reasonable.

## Key Takeaways
1. Match expansionary or contractionary policy to the sign of the output gap.
2. Size spending and tax changes with their different multipliers, not with a one-for-one rule.
3. Check inside and outside lags before interpreting a policy's timing as its effect.
4. Separate government purchases from transfers when linking the budget to GDP.
5. Distinguish useful short-run deficits from persistent deficits that reduce capital formation through crowding out.
