# Chapter 30: Public Goods and Public Choice

## Core Idea
Public goods generate benefits that markets cannot easily charge for because they are nonrival and nonexcludable. Voluntary contributions invite free riding, so taxes and collective decision making may produce efficient quantities. But government is not automatically benevolent: median-voter competition, self-interest, and concentrated special interests shape which public projects are chosen.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Public-good classification**: Use to distinguish a good's economic properties from who provides it. A public good is nonrival in consumption and nonexcludable: one person's benefit does not reduce another's, and excluding nonpayers is impractical. A private good is rival and excludable even if government provides it for free.
- **External-benefit and social-benefit framework**: Use for education, trees, theft-prevention systems, and research. `marginal social benefit = marginal private benefit + marginal external benefit`. Private choice stops where private benefit equals marginal cost, producing too little when external benefits are positive; a subsidy or public provision can move quantity toward social efficiency.
- **Free-rider and collective-finance framework**: Use when people can receive a public good without paying. Each individual prefers others to finance the good, so voluntary contributions can fall short even when total benefits exceed cost. Taxes, matching contributions, private rewards, and moral appeals are ways to overcome, but not fully eliminate, the problem.
- **Median-voter rule**: Use for majority-rule choices on a single-dimensional issue such as an education budget. Candidates move toward the preference of the voter in the middle because that position can capture the decisive voters.
- **Vote-with-your-feet framework**: Use for local public services. Households can sort among communities offering different tax and service bundles, disciplining inefficient local governments through migration.
- **Self-interest and special-interest models**: Use as alternatives to the benevolent-government model. Officials may seek larger budgets or prestige; concentrated beneficiaries have stronger incentives to lobby than the many taxpayers who each bear a small cost.

## Key Concepts
- **External benefit**: Benefit experienced by someone other than the buyer.
- **Public good**: Good that is nonrival and nonexcludable.
- **Private good**: Good that is rival and excludable.
- **Free rider**: Person who receives a benefit without paying.
- **Chump problem**: Reluctance to contribute when others can free ride.
- **Marginal social benefit**: Private marginal benefit plus marginal external benefit.
- **Public-choice economics**: Rational-choice analysis of public-sector decisions.
- **Median-voter rule**: Government choice tends to match the middle voter's preference.
- **Special-interest group**: Small group seeking concentrated benefits financed by dispersed costs.
- **Means of exit**: Moving to another community to choose a different tax-service bundle.

## Mental Models
- **Use the rivalry-exclusion test before calling something public**: A government-funded private good, such as a meal or apartment, remains rival and excludable.
- **Use social rather than private MB for spillovers**: Add benefits received by neighbors, coworkers, voters, or future victims before deciding the efficient quantity.
- **Use the free-rider test for financing**: If noncontributors cannot be excluded, voluntary funding will tend to underprovide the good.
- **Use the concentrated-benefit test for political risk**: Expect lobbying and inefficient policy when a small group gains a lot while each taxpayer loses only a little.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming a public good must be produced by government**: The defining issue is rival/nonrival and excludable/nonexcludable, not ownership.
- **Calling free cheese a public good**: Each piece is consumed by one household; it is a private good distributed free by government.
- **Assuming majority rule always selects the social optimum**: The median voter's preference may ignore external benefits, tax costs, minority interests, or information gaps.
- **Ignoring incentives to not participate**: Free riders and voters with small individual stakes can make socially valuable contributions or oversight disappear.
- **Treating special interests as representative of total benefits**: Concentrated lobbying can support projects whose aggregate cost exceeds aggregate benefit.

## Worked Example
The flood-control dam example shows why collective finance can be efficient. The dam protects 1,000 people, each receiving a $50 benefit, so total benefit is `$50 * 1,000 = $50,000`. Its construction cost is $20,000, so building it is socially efficient. No individual will build it alone because the private $50 benefit is less than the full cost. A tax of $20 per person raises exactly $20,000, and each taxpayer receives a net expected benefit of $30, so the proposal can receive unanimous support under the simplified assumptions. With voluntary contributions, each person can hope others pay while still receiving protection; the resulting free-rider problem can leave the dam unbuilt despite positive total surplus.

## Key Takeaways
1. Classify goods by rivalry and excludability before choosing a financing mechanism.
2. Add external benefits to private benefits when comparing marginal social benefit with marginal cost.
3. Expect voluntary contributions to underprovide nonexcludable goods unless contributors receive private rewards or strong social incentives.
4. Use the median-voter rule for one-dimensional majority choices, but test its assumptions.
5. Compare voting, migration, bureaucratic incentives, and special-interest pressure when explaining actual policy.
6. Make concentrated benefits and dispersed costs visible before treating a public project as efficient.
