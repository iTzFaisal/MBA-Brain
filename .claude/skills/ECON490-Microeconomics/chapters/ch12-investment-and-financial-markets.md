# Chapter 12: Investment and Financial Markets

## Core Idea
Investment exchanges costs today for uncertain future benefits, so it is both a source of long-run capital and a volatile component of short-run GDP. Present value and real interest rates provide the comparison rule for projects, while financial intermediaries pool savings, diversify risk, monitor borrowers, provide liquidity, and transform loans into tradable securities. Leverage and poor underwriting can turn those efficiencies into systemic risk.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Accelerator and multiplier-accelerator theories**: Use the accelerator theory when expected future real GDP growth drives plant and equipment spending. Firms invest more when demand is expected to grow. The multiplier-accelerator model explains how a GDP downturn can reduce investment, which further reduces GDP through the multiplier. Keynes's "animal spirits" capture abrupt optimism and pessimism.
- **Present-value rule**: Use to compare cash flows at different dates: `present value = K / (1 + i)^t`. Invest when today's cost is less than or equal to the present value of future benefits. A higher interest rate lowers the present value because it raises the opportunity cost of funds.
- **Real-nominal interest framework**: Use real returns for economic decisions: `real interest rate = nominal interest rate - inflation rate`; expected real interest is nominal interest minus expected inflation. Nominal and real calculations are both valid if future cash flows and the discount rate use the same price basis.
- **Investment decision theories**: Use the neoclassical theory when taxes and real interest rates affect investment; use Q-theory when high stock prices reduce the cost of financing new projects and signal high expected future dividends. Investment falls as the real interest rate rises, other factors fixed.
- **Financial intermediation and risk transformation**: Use intermediaries to explain how banks, savings and loans, insurers, brokers, and mutual funds connect savers to investors. Pooling, monitoring, liquidity, diversification, securitization, and leverage lower some costs but can amplify correlated losses.

## Key Concepts
- **Investment**: An action with a cost today and an expected future benefit.
- **Accelerator theory**: Investment depends positively on expected future real GDP growth.
- **Present value**: Maximum amount worth paying today for a future payment.
- **Nominal interest rate**: Market-quoted interest rate before adjusting for inflation.
- **Real interest rate**: Nominal interest rate minus actual inflation.
- **Expected real interest rate**: Nominal interest rate minus expected inflation.
- **Q-theory of investment**: Theory linking investment spending to stock prices.
- **Financial intermediary**: Organization that receives funds from savers and channels them to investors.
- **Securitization**: Buying, packaging, and selling loans in financial markets.
- **Leverage**: Use of borrowed funds to purchase assets, increasing both potential return and risk.

## Mental Models
- **Use the present-value gate before debating a project**: Discount every future payoff, then compare its value with today's cost.
- **Use a real-return dashboard**: Subtract expected inflation from nominal interest and compare the real cost of funds with the project's real return.
- **Use expectations as a volatility trigger**: Ask whether an investment change reflects revised future demand, uncertainty, or financing conditions.
- **Use the intermediation risk checklist**: Check diversification, liquidity mismatch, leverage, underwriting, monitoring, and whether apparently separate assets share a common shock.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating investment as certain future profit**: The defining feature is uncertainty, not merely a delayed payment.
- **Discounting nominal benefits with a real rate**: Mixing price bases produces a misleading present value.
- **Reading high nominal rates as high real rates**: Inflation can make a high nominal rate a low or negative real return.
- **Assuming stock prices cause investment automatically**: Both may reflect shared expectations about GDP, profits, and technology.
- **Confusing diversification with elimination of risk**: Independent risks can be pooled, but a common recession or housing collapse remains correlated.

## Worked Example
A friend offers $105 one year from now in exchange for money today. At a 5 percent interest rate, the present value is `$105 / 1.05 = $100`; lending $100 is no better or worse than leaving it in a savings account, so $100 is the maximum rational price. At a 10 percent rate, the same payment is worth `$105 / 1.10 = $95.45`; at 2 percent, it is worth `$105 / 1.02 = $102.94`. The payment did not change, but its present value did because the opportunity cost of funds changed. The same rule decides whether a plant, dam, education, or housing project is worth undertaking.

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat investment as a present cost traded for an uncertain future payoff.
2. Discount future benefits with `K/(1+i)^t` before comparing them with current cost.
3. Use expected real interest rates, not nominal rates alone, for investment decisions.
4. Expect investment to be procyclical and amplified by the multiplier-accelerator process.
5. Evaluate financial intermediaries for the services they provide and the leverage, liquidity, and correlated risks they create.
