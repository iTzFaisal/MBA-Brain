# Chapter 32: The Labor Market and the Distribution of Income

## Core Idea
Labor demand is derived from demand for the goods workers produce, and a competitive firm hires labor until the wage equals the marginal-revenue product of labor. Wages then reflect demand, skills, training, amenities, risk, discrimination, and barriers to entry. The chapter connects those labor-market forces to rising income inequality, poverty, taxes, transfers, and work incentives.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Marginal-revenue-product labor demand**: Use when deciding how many workers a competitive firm hires. `MRP = marginal product of labor * price of output`; hire labor while MRP is at least the wage. With a fixed facility, diminishing returns make MRP and the short-run labor-demand curve downward sloped.
- **Short-run versus long-run labor demand**: Use when a wage changes. In the long run, the output effect lowers total output because higher labor costs raise product prices and reduce quantity demanded; the input-substitution effect replaces labor with capital or other inputs. Both reduce labor demand, so long-run demand is flatter than short-run demand.
- **Income-and-substitution labor-supply framework**: Use for a worker's hours decision. A higher wage raises the opportunity cost of leisure, encouraging more work, but also raises real income; if leisure is normal, the income effect encourages more leisure and less work. Individual hours can rise, fall, or stay constant, while occupational choice and migration make market supply generally upward sloped.
- **Compensating-wage and human-capital framework**: Use to explain wage differences. Wages are higher when required skills are scarce, training is costly, working conditions are undesirable, risk is high, or licensing/unions restrict supply. Education can raise productivity through a learning effect and signal unobserved ability through a signaling effect.
- **Distributional-quintile framework**: Use to measure inequality. Rank households by market income, divide them into quintiles, and compare each group's income share before and after taxes and transfers. Between 1979 and 2007 the top quintile's market-income share rose from about 49.6 percent to 59.9 percent, while the top 1 percent's share rose from 10.5 to 21.3 percent.
- **Work-incentive transfer framework**: Use when evaluating antipoverty policy. A high base benefit can reduce work incentives; a high benefit-reduction rate can make additional earnings yield little net income. The EITC instead subsidizes earnings, phases in with work, has a flat maximum, and then phases out.

## Key Concepts
- **Derived demand**: Demand for labor generated by demand for the goods labor produces.
- **Marginal product of labor**: Output change from one additional unit of labor.
- **Marginal-revenue product (MRP)**: Extra revenue generated by one additional unit of labor.
- **Short-run labor demand**: Wage-quantity demand when the facility is fixed.
- **Output effect**: Labor-demand change from a wage-induced change in total output.
- **Input-substitution effect**: Labor-demand change from replacing labor with other inputs after a relative wage change.
- **Human capital**: Skills and knowledge acquired through education and experience.
- **Learning effect**: Wage increase from skills acquired through education.
- **Signaling effect**: Education's signal of otherwise hard-to-observe ability.
- **Means-tested program**: Assistance available when income falls below a threshold.
- **Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC)**: Earnings subsidy for low-income households.

## Mental Models
- **Use `wage = MRP` for competitive hiring**: Compare the cost of the next worker with the revenue generated by that worker, not with average productivity.
- **Use the two-effect labor-demand test**: In the long run, ask both what happens to output and what inputs substitute for labor.
- **Use the leisure-price/income split for wage changes**: A higher wage makes leisure more expensive but also makes the worker richer; do not predict individual hours without knowing which effect dominates.
- **Use the incentive schedule for transfers**: Inspect the base payment, benefit reduction rate, phase-in, plateau, and phase-out rather than judging a program from its headline benefit.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming higher wages always increase hours worked**: Income and substitution effects work in opposite directions for an individual.
- **Using average product instead of MRP to set a competitive wage**: The relevant hiring benefit is the revenue from the next worker.
- **Explaining every wage gap with productivity alone**: Occupational access, discrimination, risk, amenities, training costs, and institutions also shift labor supply and demand.
- **Treating inequality statistics as self-explanatory**: Distinguish market income from after-tax-and-transfer income and check whether changes are concentrated at the top.
- **Designing aid without marginal work incentives**: A 100 percent benefit reduction can make additional earnings yield no net cash gain.

## Worked Example
The Major League Baseball application applies the MRP rule to players. A team will pay a free agent's salary only when the player's expected MRP, the additional ticket and broadcast revenue generated by the player's contribution to winning, is at least that salary. Competition among teams for free agents pushes salaries close to MRP. Players restricted to one team receive less: the source reports that journeymen's average MRP is about 17 percent above their average salary, while apprentices' MRP is about 3.6 times their average salary. The difference is not evidence that the MRP rule failed; it reflects institutional limits on mobility and bargaining. Once those restrictions are removed, competing employers can bid closer to the player's marginal revenue contribution.

## Key Takeaways
1. Derive labor demand from product demand, output price, worker productivity, and diminishing returns.
2. Hire until the next worker's MRP equals the wage, subject to the relevant time horizon.
3. Use output and input-substitution effects to explain why long-run labor demand is more elastic.
4. Separate an individual's ambiguous hours response from market supply responses through occupational choice and migration.
5. Explain wage differences with scarcity, human capital, compensating differentials, discrimination, and institutional barriers.
6. Measure inequality using quintiles and distinguish market income from after-tax-and-transfer income.
7. Prefer antipoverty designs that support work while reducing poverty, such as an earnings subsidy with transparent phase-in and phase-out incentives.
