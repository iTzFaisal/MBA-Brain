# Chapter 7: The Economy at Full Employment

## Core Idea
In the classical full-employment model, flexible wages and prices allow the labor market to clear. The production function converts the equilibrium amounts of capital and labor into potential output, while labor demand and supply determine the real wage and employment. Full employment does not mean zero unemployment; it means no cyclical unemployment.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Classical full-employment model**: Use as a long-run benchmark when wages and prices adjust freely and quickly. Frictional and structural unemployment remain, but cyclical unemployment is absent. The model is not a claim that short-run recessions never occur.
- **Production function**: Use to connect inputs to output: `Y = F(K, L)`, where `Y` is GDP, `K` is the stock of capital, and `L` is labor. With capital fixed, more labor raises output at a decreasing rate. More capital shifts the short-run production function upward.
- **Labor demand and supply**: Use the real wage, not the nominal wage, as the price of labor. Firms' downward-sloping labor demand reflects the marginal benefit of labor; workers' upward-sloping supply reflects the assumed response of desired work to purchasing power. Their intersection determines employment and the real wage.
- **Full-employment output and potential output**: Use the equilibrium employment level as an input into the production function. Potential output is what the economy produces when labor supply equals labor demand at the prevailing real wage, not the absolute maximum possible output.
- **Tax and technology shocks / real business cycle theory**: Use a labor-demand shift to trace effects. A labor tax raises the marginal cost of hiring and shifts demand left; a positive technology shock can shift demand right and raise both wages and employment. Real business cycle theory emphasizes technology shocks as sources of fluctuations in potential output.

## Key Concepts
- **Classical model**: A model assuming wages and prices adjust freely to demand and supply.
- **Production function**: The relationship between output and the factors used to produce it.
- **Stock of capital**: All machines, equipment, and buildings available to the economy.
- **Real wage**: The wage rate adjusted for changes in the price level.
- **Labor demand**: The quantity of labor firms want to hire at each real wage.
- **Labor supply**: The quantity of labor workers want to provide at each real wage.
- **Full-employment output/potential output**: Output produced when the labor market is in equilibrium.
- **Natural rate of unemployment**: Frictional plus structural unemployment when cyclical unemployment is zero.
- **Crowding out**: Reduction in one GDP component caused by increased government spending at fixed output.
- **Real business cycle theory**: A theory emphasizing technology shocks as causes of economic fluctuations.

## Mental Models
- **Use the real-wage lens when comparing labor markets**: Ask what the wage buys and what it costs firms after the price level is considered.
- **Use the production-function bridge**: Find equilibrium employment from labor demand and supply, then map that employment through `Y = F(K,L)` to obtain potential output.
- **Use slope to predict tax effects**: A labor tax shifts demand left; the labor-supply elasticity determines how much adjustment appears in employment versus wages.
- **Use the capital-complement test for immigration and investment**: More labor tends to lower the real wage, while more capital raises labor productivity and labor demand.

## Anti-patterns
- **Defining full employment as zero unemployment**: Efficient economies need time for search and reallocation, so natural unemployment remains.
- **Using nominal wages to infer labor-market conditions**: Inflation can raise dollar wages without increasing real purchasing power.
- **Treating potential output as the physical maximum**: Potential output reflects the labor people choose to supply at equilibrium, not unlimited overtime.
- **Claiming immigration has only one effect**: A larger labor supply can lower incumbent wages while increasing employment, lowering input prices, and expanding output.
- **Applying diminishing returns after all inputs have adjusted**: The short-run fixed-capital assumption is essential.

## Worked Example
The Black Death shows how a labor-supply shock affects living standards. In medieval England, real wages were about the same around 1200 and 1800, but from 1350 to 1550 they were temporarily much higher, roughly 75 percent above the 1200 level by 1450. The bubonic plague reduced population and therefore labor supplied to the market. With fewer workers, the labor-supply curve shifted left: the real wage rose, but total output fell because fewer people were producing. The example separates wage welfare from aggregate production and shows why a smaller labor force need not make every outcome worse for remaining workers.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use flexible wages and prices as a long-run benchmark, not as a description of every short-run outcome.
2. Keep `K` fixed when applying short-run diminishing returns to labor.
3. Determine real wages and employment at the labor-market intersection, then use the production function to obtain potential output.
4. Trace taxes and technology through labor demand before drawing conclusions about GDP.
5. At full employment, higher government spending must crowd out another use of fixed output; in an open economy, net exports can be crowded out too.
