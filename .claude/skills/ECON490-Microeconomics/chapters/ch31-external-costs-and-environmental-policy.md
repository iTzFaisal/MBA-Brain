# Chapter 31: External Costs and Environmental Policy

## Core Idea
Pollution, congestion, and collisions impose costs on people outside the transaction, so unregulated markets produce too much of the polluting activity. Efficient policy internalizes those costs while preserving flexibility: choose abatement where marginal benefit equals marginal cost, then use pollution taxes or tradable permits to make the lowest-cost reductions first.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Optimal-abatement marginal framework**: Use when deciding how clean is efficient. Increase abatement while its marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost, and stop where `MB of abatement = MC of abatement`. A pristine environment is not automatically optimal because the final units of cleanup may cost more than the damage they prevent.
- **Coase bargaining**: Use when there are few affected parties, clear property rights, and low transaction costs. The harmed party can pay the polluter to abate, or the polluter can pay the rights holder to tolerate some pollution; bargaining reaches the efficient quantity even though the distribution of payments depends on who owns the rights.
- **Pigouvian pollution-tax framework**: Use when many parties make bargaining impractical. Set a tax equal to the external cost per unit of pollution, shifting private incentives toward social cost. Firms abate when the avoided tax exceeds marginal abatement cost and consumers reduce demand for the polluting product.
- **Command-and-control comparison**: Use to compare uniform standards with market incentives. Requiring each firm to abate the same amount ignores differences in marginal abatement costs; specifying one technology can raise production costs and weaken incentives for innovation. The advantage is predictable emissions.
- **Marketable-permit or cap-and-trade framework**: Use when policymakers need a quantity target. Set a cap, issue just enough permits, and allow trading. High-cost abaters buy permits from low-cost abaters; the permit price reflects the avoided marginal abatement cost. A tighter cap raises the permit price, while cheaper abatement technology lowers demand and price.
- **Automobile-externality pricing**: Use when designing transport policy. Separate pollution per mile, congestion by time and location, and collision risk by driver and vehicle. A mileage or congestion tax is more targeted than a gasoline tax, though gasoline taxes can be practical proxies.

## Key Concepts
- **External cost of production**: Cost imposed on someone other than the producer.
- **Private cost of production**: Producer's cost of labor, capital, materials, and other inputs.
- **Social cost of production**: `private cost + external cost`.
- **Pollution abatement**: Reduction in pollution from a starting level.
- **Pollution tax**: Charge equal to external cost per unit of pollution.
- **Coase bargaining**: Side-payment solution among affected parties with low transaction costs.
- **Command-and-control policy**: Government-mandated pollution level or abatement technology.
- **Marketable pollution permit**: Tradable right to emit under a fixed pollution cap.
- **Cap-and-trade**: System that fixes total permits and lets firms trade them.
- **Vehicle-miles-traveled tax**: Charge per mile intended to internalize collision or other travel costs.

## Mental Models
- **Use `MB = MC` for environmental quantity, not "zero pollution"**: Compare the next unit of damage avoided with the next unit of cleanup cost.
- **Use the least-cost-abater rule**: For a fixed reduction target, shift abatement toward firms with the lowest marginal abatement cost.
- **Use a tax when behavior should adapt; a cap when emissions quantity must be fixed**: Taxes reveal the cost through price, while permits fix the total quantity and let the market discover the price.
- **Use the externality-specific tax test for automobiles**: Match the charge to miles, congestion, emissions, time, location, or risk rather than using one coarse proxy for every harm.

## Anti-patterns
- **Demanding zero pollution without comparing marginal costs**: Elimination can require abandoning valuable production or replacing one environmental harm with another.
- **Treating the legal polluter as the only economic party**: External costs are borne by neighbors, workers, consumers, ecosystems, and future generations.
- **Requiring equal abatement from unequal firms**: Uniform standards can cost more than a tax or tradable-permit system for the same reduction.
- **Assuming a pollution tax fixes total emissions exactly**: The tax gives incentives but the final quantity depends on demand and abatement costs.
- **Confusing clean technology per mile with total emissions**: If cleaner cars induce more driving, total pollution may not fall as expected.

## Worked Example
The lake example has a steel mill and a fishing firm. Abating the first ton costs $3 and yields a $21 fish-harvest benefit; marginal abatement cost rises to $13 for the last ton, while marginal benefit falls to $1. The efficient point is 300 tons of abatement, leaving 200 tons of waste, where marginal benefit equals marginal cost. If the mill owns the lake, the fishing firm can pay for abatement: for the first ton it can offer up to $21 while the mill needs only $3, so a split-the-difference payment of $12 benefits both. Bargaining continues until the 300th ton. If the fishing firm owns the lake, the mill instead pays to permit pollution, and the same 200 tons of waste emerge. Property rights affect who receives payments, but with only two parties and low bargaining costs they need not affect the efficient pollution level.

## Key Takeaways
1. Define private, external, and social cost before evaluating an environmental policy.
2. Choose abatement where marginal benefit equals marginal cost, allowing some pollution when cleanup is too costly.
3. Use Coase bargaining only when rights are clear and the number of affected parties is small.
4. Set pollution taxes to internalize external costs and let firms choose the cheapest abatement method.
5. Use tradable permits to guarantee a cap while exploiting differences in abatement costs.
6. Price automobile pollution, congestion, and collision risk separately where measurement permits.
