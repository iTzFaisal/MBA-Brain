# Chapter 1: Introduction: What Is Economics?

## Core Idea
Economics is the study of choices made under scarcity. Because resources are limited while wants are unlimited, every choice has a trade-off; economic analysis makes the alternatives, incentives, and consequences visible so individuals, firms, and governments can choose more intelligently.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Scarcity and the three key economic questions**: Use whenever a person, organization, or society must allocate limited resources. Ask: (1) What products do we produce? (2) How do we produce them? (3) Who consumes the products? The five factors of production are natural resources, labor, physical capital, human capital, and entrepreneurship.
- **Positive versus normative analysis**: Use positive analysis to answer "What is?" or "What will be?" by predicting consequences. Use normative analysis to answer "What ought to be?" Policy judgment comes after the empirical trade-offs are understood; economists can estimate the effects of a minimum-wage increase without deciding whether it should occur.
- **Economic models**: Use a simplified representation of an economic environment to isolate the features relevant to a question. A model is an abstraction, not a complete replica. Judge a simplifying assumption by whether it changes the conclusion for the problem at hand.
- **The economic way of thinking**: Use four moves in sequence: simplify with assumptions, isolate a relationship with `ceteris paribus` (other variables held fixed), think at the margin about a small change, and assume rational people respond to incentives. This is a method for drawing conclusions, not a fixed list of policy answers.

## Key Concepts
- **Scarcity**: Resources used to produce goods and services are limited, so not every want can be satisfied.
- **Economics**: The study of choices made when scarcity forces trade-offs.
- **Factors of production**: Natural resources, labor, physical capital, human capital, and entrepreneurship.
- **Positive analysis**: Analysis that predicts outcomes and describes what is or will be.
- **Normative analysis**: Analysis concerned with what ought to be.
- **Economic model**: A deliberately simplified representation of an economic environment.
- **Variable**: A measure that can take different values.
- **Ceteris paribus**: The assumption that other relevant variables are held fixed.
- **Marginal change**: A small, one-unit change used to examine an incremental effect.
- **Microeconomics and macroeconomics**: Microeconomics studies household, firm, and government choices in markets; macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole.

## Mental Models
- **Use the scarcity test when a proposal sounds free**: Identify the resources it consumes and the best alternative use that must be sacrificed.
- **Use the three-question test when evaluating a policy**: Identify what is produced, how it is produced, and who receives it before debating whether the outcome is desirable.
- **Use ceteris paribus when isolating causation**: Hold income, related prices, technology, or other drivers fixed before attributing a change to one variable.
- **Use the incentive test when predicting behavior**: Find how the payoff or cost changed, then ask which actions become more attractive.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating a desire as demand**: Demand requires both willingness and ability to sacrifice resources; wanting a product alone does not allocate it.
- **Confusing positive and normative claims**: A prediction about job losses is not a recommendation about whether a policy is good.
- **Rejecting a model because an assumption is unrealistic**: An assumption is useful or harmful according to whether it obscures the mechanism being studied.
- **Changing several variables at once and calling the result causal**: Without ceteris paribus, the direction or size of an effect cannot be identified cleanly.
- **Ignoring marginal effects**: A decision about one more hour, worker, or unit should compare its incremental benefit with its incremental cost, not its average or total alone.

## Worked Example
London's congestion problem illustrates the full method. Each driver entering a busy rush-hour highway slows other vehicles, but an individual driver considers only the driver's private travel cost. If 900 other commuters each spend two extra seconds because of one marginal driver, total travel time rises by 1,800 seconds, or 30 minutes. If commuter time is valued at $16 per hour, the marginal external cost is $8. A congestion tax of about $8 per trip makes the driver face more of the social cost. Some travelers then shift to off-peak hours, ride-sharing, mass transit, or less congested routes. The policy is not justified merely because congestion is unpleasant: the economic case is that an incentive corrects a gap between private and total cost.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start every economic problem by naming the scarce resources and the alternatives they could serve.
2. Separate positive predictions from normative judgments so evidence and values are not conflated.
3. Simplify deliberately, state what is held fixed, and inspect whether the assumption matters for the conclusion.
4. Predict behavior from changed marginal payoffs rather than from intentions alone.
5. Use macroeconomics for growth, inflation, unemployment, and fluctuations; use microeconomics for markets and specific decisions.
