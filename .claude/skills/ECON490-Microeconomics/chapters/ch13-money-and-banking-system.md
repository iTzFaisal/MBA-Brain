# Chapter 13: Money and the Banking System

## Core Idea
Money is whatever buyers and sellers regularly accept in exchange, and modern money consists mainly of currency and deposits. Banks transform deposits into loans, creating a multiple expansion or contraction of checking deposits. The Federal Reserve supplies currency, clears payments, regulates banks, conducts monetary policy, and acts as lender of last resort when financial intermediation threatens the economy.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three functions of money**: Use money as a medium of exchange to avoid barter's double coincidence of wants, as a unit of account to quote and compare prices, and as a store of value between transactions. Inflation makes the store-of-value function less reliable.
- **M1/M2 measurement framework**: Use M1 for currency held by the public, demand deposits, other checkable deposits, and traveler's checks. Use M2 for M1 plus savings deposits, retail money-market mutual funds, and small time deposits. Credit cards are borrowing instruments, not money; debit cards access money already in checking accounts.
- **Bank balance-sheet and reserve framework**: Use the liabilities-and-equity side for sources of funds, including deposits and owners' equity; use assets for loans and reserves. `Total assets = total liabilities + owners' equity`. Required reserves are the legal fraction of deposits; excess reserves are additional holdings.
- **Deposit-creation multiplier**: Under the simplified assumptions that banks lend all excess reserves and borrowers redeposit all loans, `increase in checking deposits = initial deposit * 1/reserve ratio`. A 10 percent reserve ratio creates a tenfold gross deposit expansion, but cash holdings and excess reserves make the real multiplier smaller.
- **Central-bank and crisis framework**: Use the Fed as a banker's bank, supplier of currency, check-clearing system, bank regulator, monetary-policy authority, and lender of last resort. Its Board of Governors, 12 regional banks, and FOMC distribute formal authority, while long terms support independence.

## Key Concepts
- **Money**: An item regularly used and accepted in economic exchanges.
- **Medium of exchange**: An item buyers give sellers in payment for goods and services.
- **Unit of account**: Standard unit for stating prices and comparing values.
- **Store of value**: Ability to preserve purchasing power until a future exchange.
- **Fiat money**: Money with no intrinsic commodity value, made legal tender by government.
- **M1/M2**: Narrow and broad measures of money, with M2 including selected savings assets.
- **Reserves**: Deposits or cash held by banks rather than lent out.
- **Reserve ratio**: Ratio of reserves to deposits.
- **Money multiplier**: Ratio of total checking-deposit increase to an initial cash deposit.
- **Lender of last resort**: Central bank lending to banks during an emergency when other sources fail.

## Mental Models
- **Use the function test to classify money**: Ask whether an asset is used directly for exchange, quoted as the account unit, and held as value; do not classify by whether a card is plastic.
- **Use the balance-sheet identity before discussing bank creation**: Track the change in deposits, reserves, loans, and currency on both sides.
- **Use the multiplier as a ceiling, not a promise**: Reduce the textbook result for cash leakage, excess reserves, weak loan demand, and defaults.
- **Use lender-of-last-resort logic during a panic**: Separate a liquidity problem, where funds are temporarily unavailable, from insolvency, where assets are worth less than liabilities.

## Anti-patterns
- **Calling credit-card balances money**: A credit card creates a loan; it is not itself a medium of exchange, account unit, or store of value.
- **Saying one bank creates money from nothing**: The banking system expands deposits through sequential lending and redepositing, subject to reserves and repayment.
- **Assuming a 10 percent reserve ratio guarantees a tenfold money multiplier**: Public cash holdings and bank excess reserves interrupt the rounds.
- **Treating a bank run as harmless because a bank is profitable**: A solvent bank can fail if depositors demand cash simultaneously while most funds are lent.
- **Ignoring the trade-off in deposit insurance**: Insurance prevents panic but can encourage institutions and depositors to tolerate excessive risk.

## Worked Example
Suppose a $1,000 cash deposit enters a bank and the reserve ratio is 0.10. The first bank keeps $100 and lends $900. The next bank receives $900, keeps $90, and lends $810; the following bank keeps $81 and lends $729. The infinite sequence of deposits is `$1,000 + $900 + $810 + ... = $1,000 * 1/0.10 = $10,000`. Because the public gave up $1,000 of currency, the net M1 increase in the simplified example is $9,000: checking deposits rise by $10,000 while currency held by the public falls by $1,000. If borrowers retain cash or banks hold excess reserves, the actual expansion is smaller.

## Key Takeaways
1. Classify money by its economic functions, not its physical form.
2. Distinguish M1's transaction assets from M2's less-liquid savings assets.
3. Track bank assets and liabilities together whenever a deposit, loan, withdrawal, or reserve changes.
4. Treat `1/reserve ratio` as a simplified deposit-creation benchmark and inspect its assumptions.
5. Understand the Fed's lender-of-last-resort role as a way to contain liquidity panics, with risk and moral-hazard trade-offs.
