# Chapter 20: Elasticity: A Measure of Responsiveness

## Core Idea
Elasticity turns the laws of demand and supply into quantitative decision tools. It measures percentage responsiveness, predicts quantity and price changes, connects price changes to total revenue, and forces analysis to account for substitutes, time, budget shares, production costs, and the relevant side of the market.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Percentage elasticity framework**: Use when the question asks how strongly quantity responds to a price, income, or related-good change. Price elasticity of demand is `Ed = |percentage change in quantity demanded / percentage change in price|`; price elasticity of supply is `Es = percentage change in quantity supplied / percentage change in price`. Use the midpoint method when a two-way comparison must give the same percentage change in either direction.
- **Elasticity-determinants test**: Use before assigning an elasticity value. Demand is more elastic when there are many substitutes, consumers have more time to adjust, the good takes a large budget share, or it is a luxury rather than a necessity. Supply is more elastic when firms can expand capacity and when marginal costs rise slowly.
- **Total-revenue rule**: Use to evaluate a price change. `total revenue = price per unit * quantity sold`. If `Ed > 1`, a price increase reduces total revenue; if `Ed < 1`, it increases total revenue; if `Ed = 1`, total revenue is unchanged locally.
- **Linear-demand midpoint rule**: Use with a straight-line demand curve. Slope is constant, but elasticity is not: demand is elastic above the midpoint, unit elastic at the midpoint, and inelastic below it. Total revenue is maximized at the midpoint.
- **Cross-market elasticity framework**: Use to classify related products and income responses. Positive income elasticity identifies a normal good and negative income elasticity an inferior good. Positive cross-price elasticity identifies substitutes; negative cross-price elasticity identifies complements.
- **Equilibrium-price response formula**: Use for a small or specified shift when both sides of the market respond. For a demand increase, `percentage change in equilibrium price = percentage change in demand / (Es + Ed)`. For a supply change, `percentage change in equilibrium price = - percentage change in supply / (Es + Ed)`.

## Key Concepts
- **Price elasticity of demand**: Responsiveness of quantity demanded to a percentage change in price.
- **Price elasticity of supply**: Responsiveness of quantity supplied to a percentage change in price.
- **Elastic demand**: Demand with elasticity greater than one.
- **Inelastic demand**: Demand with elasticity less than one.
- **Unit elastic demand**: Demand with elasticity equal to one.
- **Total revenue**: Price multiplied by quantity sold.
- **Income elasticity of demand**: Percentage change in quantity demanded divided by percentage change in income.
- **Cross-price elasticity of demand**: Percentage change in quantity of good X divided by the percentage change in the price of good Y.
- **Perfectly inelastic supply**: Supply with zero quantity response to price.
- **Perfectly elastic supply**: Supply with an infinite quantity response at one price.

## Mental Models
- **Use elasticity rather than slope when comparing different units or scales**: Percentage changes make responsiveness comparable across products and markets.
- **Use the time-horizon switch**: Expect demand and supply to be less elastic in the short run because consumers and firms cannot immediately change cars, locations, plants, or crops.
- **Use the revenue sign test**: Observe whether price and total revenue move together or in opposite directions to infer inelastic or elastic demand.
- **Use both elasticities for incidence and equilibrium changes**: A change in demand or supply creates a smaller price movement when buyers and sellers can adjust more readily.

## Anti-patterns
- **Confusing a steep curve with a fixed elasticity**: Slope uses absolute units; elasticity uses percentage changes and can vary along a straight line.
- **Applying the law of demand without a magnitude**: Knowing quantity falls is not enough to predict revenue, tax receipts, or the size of a price change.
- **Using long-run estimates for an immediate policy effect**: Gasoline demand, coffee supply, and housing supply all become more responsive with time.
- **Looking only at demand when forecasting a supply shock**: A pipeline failure's price effect depends on alternative suppliers and supply elasticity as well as consumers' response.
- **Treating a brand and the whole product category as the same market**: A specific coffee brand has more substitutes and therefore typically more elastic demand than coffee overall.

## Worked Example
The broken-pipeline application explains why a 30 percent gasoline-supply reduction did not require the 150 percent price increase implied by demand alone. Short-run gasoline demand had elasticity `Ed = 0.20`; if supply were fixed, consumers would need a very large price rise to cut quantity by 30 percent. But sellers could divert gasoline from the West Coast pipeline and compete for available fuel. Taking supply elasticity as `Es = 0.55`, the equilibrium-price formula gives `percentage change in price = -(-30%) / (0.55 + 0.20) = 30% / 0.75 = 40%`. The higher Phoenix price pulled supplies from other cities, so the market adjusted through both lower demand and higher supply rather than through demand contraction alone.

## Key Takeaways
1. Define the percentage-change convention before computing an elasticity.
2. Use the midpoint method when direction should not change the measured percentage change.
3. For pricing, compare `Ed` with one before predicting total revenue.
4. Expect substitutes, time, budget share, and luxury status to raise demand elasticity.
5. Expect fixed capacity and diminishing returns to make short-run supply less elastic than long-run supply.
6. For equilibrium effects, divide the demand or supply shift by the sum `Es + Ed` rather than using one elasticity alone.
