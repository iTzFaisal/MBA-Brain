# Chapter 18: International Trade and Public Policy

## Core Idea
International trade expands consumption possibilities when nations specialize according to comparative advantage, not absolute productivity. Protectionist policies can preserve particular jobs or industries, but they raise domestic prices, destroy some gains from exchange, invite retaliation, and shift costs among consumers, workers, importers, and exporters.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Comparative advantage and the terms of trade**: Use by calculating each nation's opportunity cost for each good. The nation with the lower opportunity cost specializes in that good. Mutually beneficial terms of trade lie between the two nations' opportunity costs, allowing both consumption possibilities curves to exceed their production possibilities curves.
- **Production versus consumption possibilities**: Use to distinguish self-sufficiency from trade. A production possibilities curve shows what a nation can make with its own resources; after specialization, the consumption possibilities curve shows what it can consume by exchanging output with another nation.
- **Protectionist-policy incidence**: Use to evaluate an import ban, quota, voluntary export restraint, or tariff. Restricting imports shifts total supply left, raises the domestic price, decreases total consumption, and creates gains for protected producers at the expense of consumers and lost mutually beneficial trades. A tariff creates government revenue; a quota or VER creates quota rents for importers or foreign producers.
- **Protectionist-rationale test**: Use when a policy is defended as job protection, infant-industry policy, or strategic trade policy. Ask whether workers can transition, whether learning by doing is genuine and temporary, whether the government can withdraw protection, and whether taxpayers gain more than foreign rivals lose.
- **Trade-institutions framework**: Use when a dispute involves dumping, environmental rules, or trade agreements. GATT and the WTO lower barriers and resolve disputes, but rules constrain discriminatory restrictions and cannot eliminate the political conflict between national regulation and global production.

## Key Concepts
- **Comparative advantage**: The ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another producer.
- **Terms of trade**: The rate at which units of one product exchange for units of another.
- **Consumption possibilities curve**: Combinations a nation can consume after specializing and trading.
- **Autarky**: Self-sufficiency without international trade.
- **Import quota**: A government-imposed limit on the quantity imported.
- **Voluntary export restraint (VER)**: An exporting country's agreement to reduce its exports.
- **Tariff**: A tax on imported goods.
- **Learning by doing**: Knowledge and skills gained through production that raise productivity and lower cost.
- **Dumping**: Selling in a foreign market below the home-market price or production cost.
- **Outsourcing**: Producing components or services in another country.

## Mental Models
- **Use opportunity cost before debating national competitiveness**: A country can have an absolute advantage in both goods and still gain by specializing in the good with the lower relative sacrifice.
- **Use the consumer-producer split for protectionism**: Trace the higher price, the protected domestic output, the lost imports, the tariff revenue or quota rent, and the retaliation risk.
- **Use the adjustment-time test for job claims**: Separate the long-run gains from lower prices and expanded trade from the short-run costs of displaced workers, regions, and skills that cannot move quickly.
- **Use the policy-substitute test for infant industries**: Prefer time-limited, measurable support or worker training over permanent protection that rewards lobbying and shelters inefficient production.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating trade as zero-sum**: Specialization and voluntary exchange can increase the amount each nation can consume; one nation's exports are not the other nation's permanent loss of money.
- **Protecting an industry without counting downstream users**: A tariff on inputs can harm firms that use those inputs and reduce exports in other sectors.
- **Confusing a tariff with a quota**: Both can produce the same price and quantity, but the tariff sends rent to the government while a quota may send it to license holders or foreign firms.
- **Assuming every low foreign price is predatory**: Price discrimination can explain a low export price; predation requires a below-cost strategy intended to remove rivals and later raise price.
- **Ignoring retaliation and smuggling**: A restriction can provoke a trade war and create a profitable gap for bribery and illegal imports.

## Worked Example
The textbook's Shirtland-Chipland reconstruction makes the gains from trade concrete. Shirtland can produce either 108 shirts or 36 chips per day, so one chip costs three shirts and one shirt costs one-third of a chip. Chipland can produce either 120 shirts or 120 chips, so one unit of either good costs one unit of the other. Chipland therefore has comparative advantage in chips, while Shirtland has comparative advantage in shirts, even though Chipland can produce more shirts in absolute terms. If each country initially divides resources evenly, Shirtland consumes 24 shirts and 28 chips while Chipland consumes 60 of each. With specialization, Shirtland produces 108 shirts and Chipland produces 120 chips. At terms of trade of two shirts per chip, Chipland trades 40 chips for 80 shirts and consumes 80 chips and 80 shirts; Shirtland trades 80 shirts for 40 chips and consumes 28 shirts and 40 chips. Each country moves beyond its autarky consumption options.

## Key Takeaways
1. Compute opportunity costs and comparative advantage before choosing a trade policy.
2. Check that the proposed terms of trade fall between the two opportunity costs.
3. Use production and consumption possibilities separately to show why specialization expands choice.
4. Expect free trade to create aggregate gains but also concentrated short-run losses for workers in shrinking industries.
5. Compare a tariff, quota, and VER by who receives the rent and who bears the higher price.
6. Treat training, mobility, and adjustment assistance as more targeted responses to worker losses than broad import restrictions.
