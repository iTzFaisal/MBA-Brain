# Chapter 23: Production Technology and Cost

## Core Idea
Economic cost is the opportunity cost of all inputs, including explicit payments and implicit costs of owned time and capital. In the short run, a fixed facility creates specialization followed by diminishing returns, shaping marginal and average costs; in the long run, input flexibility produces economies, constant returns, or diseconomies of scale.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Economic-cost framework**: Use when measuring a firm's true profitability or comparing alternatives. `economic cost = explicit cost + implicit cost`; economic profit is total revenue minus economic cost. Accounting cost and accounting profit omit unpaid opportunity costs and therefore can overstate profitability.
- **Short-run production-and-cost framework**: Use when at least one input, such as a factory or mold, is fixed. The total-product curve maps labor into output. Specialization can raise marginal product for early workers; once the facility becomes a bottleneck, diminishing returns lower marginal product and raise marginal cost.
- **Short-run cost decomposition**: Use to interpret cost curves. `TC = FC + VC`, `AFC = FC / Q`, `AVC = VC / Q`, `ATC = AFC + AVC`, and `MC = change in TC / change in Q`. The ATC curve is U-shaped because spreading fixed cost and specialization initially lower average cost, while diminishing returns eventually raise AVC.
- **Marginal-average relationship**: Use to read a cost graph. If MC is below ATC, the next unit pulls average cost down; if MC is above ATC, it pushes average cost up. MC crosses ATC at ATC's minimum.
- **Long-run scale framework**: Use when all inputs and the production facility can change. Replication gives constant returns over a range; indivisible inputs and specialization generate economies of scale at low output; coordination problems and rising input prices can generate diseconomies at high output. The minimum efficient scale is where economies are exhausted.
- **First-copy and replication logic**: Use for information goods and large capital equipment. A large first-copy or setup cost with a tiny marginal reproduction cost makes average cost fall as output expands; a large turbine can also lower unit electricity cost by spreading installation and maintenance costs over much greater capacity.

## Key Concepts
- **Economic cost**: Opportunity cost of all inputs used in production.
- **Explicit cost**: Monetary payment for an input.
- **Implicit cost**: Opportunity cost without a monetary payment.
- **Fixed cost (FC)**: Cost that does not vary with output.
- **Variable cost (VC)**: Cost that varies with output.
- **Marginal product of labor**: Output change from one additional unit of labor.
- **Short-run marginal cost (MC)**: Cost change from one additional unit of output.
- **Short-run average total cost (ATC)**: Total cost divided by output.
- **Economies of scale**: Falling long-run average cost as output increases.
- **Minimum efficient scale**: Output at which scale economies are exhausted.

## Mental Models
- **Use the opportunity-cost ledger before calling a firm profitable**: Include forgone wages, interest on invested funds, owner time, and alternative land or facility uses.
- **Use the fixed-input check to predict cost direction**: Ask whether the next worker shares a fixed bottleneck; if so, diminishing returns will eventually raise MC.
- **Use MC against ATC like a running average**: A marginal cost below the current average lowers it; a marginal cost above it raises it.
- **Use the scale-up/scale-down asymmetry**: Replication may make doubling output proportionate, while indivisible inputs and lost specialization make small operations expensive.

## Anti-patterns
- **Using accounting profit as economic profit**: An owner can earn a positive cash surplus while receiving less than the opportunity cost of time and capital.
- **Applying diminishing returns to the long run**: The principle requires at least one fixed input; a flexible firm can expand its facility.
- **Assuming average cost falls forever with size**: Coordination problems, higher input prices, and less productive inputs can create diseconomies of scale.
- **Dividing total fixed cost by zero output**: AFC and ATC are not meaningful at zero output; fixed cost is incurred even when production stops.
- **Thinking a lower output automatically halves cost**: Indivisible molds, tracks, machines, and facilities may remain fully necessary.

## Worked Example
The fake-killer-whale application isolates an indivisible input. The producer spends $11,000 on a mold and $5,000 on labor and materials for the first whale, so the first unit costs $16,000. Once the mold exists, each additional whale costs only $5,000. Two whales cost $21,000 in total, or $10,500 each; three cost $26,000, or about $8,667 each. The average cost falls because the mold's fixed cost is spread over more units, not because the marginal cost of labor and materials changed. If output is cut from two whales to one, the mold cannot be cut in half, so average cost rises. The example applies both opportunity cost and the long-run scale logic.

## Key Takeaways
1. Count implicit costs whenever an owner supplies time, capital, land, or an owned facility.
2. Separate fixed, variable, average, and marginal costs before making a production decision.
3. Expect specialization to lower early MC and fixed bottlenecks to generate diminishing returns later.
4. Use the MC-ATC relationship to locate minimum average cost.
5. In the long run, evaluate replication, indivisible inputs, coordination, and input-price effects together.
6. For information goods, compare first-copy cost with the scale of distribution rather than treating every copy as a full production cost.
