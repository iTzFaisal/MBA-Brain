# Chapter 9: Aggregate Demand and Aggregate Supply

## Core Idea
Short-run economic fluctuations arise because wages and prices are sticky, so demand changes alter output before prices fully adjust. The aggregate demand and aggregate supply model separates that short-run coordination problem from the long run, where factor supplies and technology determine potential output and demand changes primarily affect the price level.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Sticky-price short-run model**: Use when contracts, custom pricing, or slow wage adjustment prevent immediate market clearing. In the short run, firms meet demand with relatively small price changes, so weak demand lowers output and employment while strong demand raises them.
- **Aggregate demand curve (AD)**: Use to relate the price level to total real GDP demanded by households, firms, government, and the foreign sector: `AD = C + I + G + NX`. It slopes downward through the wealth effect, interest-rate effect, and international-trade effect. Changes in taxes, government spending, money supply, expectations, or foreign demand shift AD; a price-level change moves along it.
- **Multiplier and consumption function**: Use to estimate how an autonomous spending change is amplified. `C = Ca + bY`, where `Ca` is autonomous consumption and `b` is the marginal propensity to consume. `MPC + MPS = 1`, and the simple multiplier is `1 / (1 - MPC)`.
- **Short-run and long-run aggregate supply**: Use SRAS, relatively flat, when prices are sticky and firms adjust production to demand. Use LRAS, vertical at `yp`, when wages and prices have adjusted and output is determined by capital, labor, and technology.
- **Supply-shock and adjustment framework**: Use for external changes in oil prices, wages, materials, technology, taxes, regulations, or subsidies. An adverse shock shifts SRAS upward, raising prices and lowering output; over time, wage and price adjustment shifts SRAS until the economy returns to potential.

## Key Concepts
- **Sticky prices/wages**: Prices or wages that adjust slowly to changes in demand and supply.
- **Short run in macroeconomics**: Period in which prices change little.
- **Aggregate demand (AD)**: Total demand for currently produced real GDP.
- **Wealth effect**: More real purchasing power from a lower price level increases spending.
- **Interest-rate effect**: A lower price level reduces money-market interest rates and increases borrowing-sensitive spending.
- **International-trade effect**: A lower domestic price level makes domestic goods cheaper abroad, raising net exports.
- **Multiplier**: Ratio of the total AD shift to the initial autonomous spending shift.
- **Aggregate supply (AS)**: Relationship between the price level and total output firms supply.
- **Supply shock**: External event that shifts aggregate supply.
- **Stagflation**: Falling real output together with rising prices.

## Mental Models
- **Use the movement-versus-shift rule at the macro level**: A price-level change moves along AD; taxes, money, expectations, or foreign demand shift AD.
- **Use SRAS for timing and LRAS for capacity**: Ask whether wages and input prices can adjust before deciding whether output or prices absorb a shock.
- **Use the three-channel AD test**: For a price-level change, check purchasing power, interest rates, and relative prices of domestic versus foreign goods.
- **Use output-price directions to diagnose a shock**: Demand shocks move output and prices together; adverse supply shocks move them in opposite directions.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating the short run as instant market clearing**: Sticky contracts mean demand can determine production temporarily.
- **Shifting AD because the price level changed**: The price-level effect is already represented by movement along AD.
- **Assuming every recession is a demand recession**: Oil and other supply shocks can cause recession with inflation.
- **Using LRAS to predict immediate output changes**: LRAS describes the eventual factor-and-technology constraint, not short-run capacity utilization.
- **Applying the simple multiplier without checking leakages**: Taxes, imports, financial effects, and supply constraints reduce the textbook multiplier.

## Worked Example
Suppose government spending increases by $10 million while the marginal propensity to consume is `0.6`. The first round raises demand, income, and output by $10 million. Households spend $6 million of that income, creating a second round; they then spend $3.6 million, followed by $2.16 million, and so on. The total increase approaches `$10 million * 1/(1 - 0.6) = $25 million`, so the multiplier is 2.5. In the short run, the relatively flat SRAS converts much of this demand shift into higher output and only a small price increase. Near full employment, the same shift creates more inflation and less additional real output.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use sticky prices to explain why demand shocks affect short-run output and unemployment.
2. Remember `AD = C + I + G + NX` and identify whether a determinant moves along or shifts the curve.
3. Use the wealth, interest-rate, and trade channels to explain why AD slopes downward.
4. Use vertical LRAS to separate long-run potential output from short-run demand-driven GDP.
5. Treat an adverse supply shock as a possible source of stagflation, not merely a price increase.
