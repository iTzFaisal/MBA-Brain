# Chapter 28: Controlling Market Power: Antitrust and Regulation

## Core Idea
Some markets are naturally served by one firm because fixed infrastructure creates enormous economies of scale; others become concentrated through mergers, exclusion, or strategic conduct. Policy must distinguish natural monopoly from artificial market power, then choose among average-cost regulation, breakup, merger review, business-practice rules, or deregulation while accounting for cost-saving efficiencies and incentives.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Natural-monopoly test**: Use when one network or facility can serve the market at lower cost than duplicate facilities. A steeply declining long-run average-cost curve means a second firm cannot cover its fixed infrastructure cost, so a single supplier may be efficient even though it has market power.
- **Average-cost pricing**: Use to regulate an unavoidable natural monopoly. Set the price where market demand intersects long-run average cost, producing the lowest price that allows the firm to break even. The policy raises output and lowers price relative to unregulated monopoly, but weakens incentives to control costs because higher costs can be passed into the regulated price.
- **Three-part antitrust framework**: Use to classify enforcement. Governments can break up a dominant firm, block mergers that would reduce competition, and regulate business practices such as price fixing, tie-in sales, exclusionary contracts, or predatory pricing.
- **Merger-efficiency and price-data test**: Use when a merger reduces the number of firms. Do not count firms mechanically; use substitution patterns, scanner data, and marginal pricing evidence to predict whether the merged firm can raise prices, while allowing demonstrated production or administrative efficiencies to offset anticompetitive effects.
- **Predatory-pricing credibility test**: Use when a firm is accused of pricing below cost to remove rivals. The strategy is profitable only if future monopoly profits can recover the losses and entry can be kept out; if new firms can enter after the price rises, predation may require repeated losses.
- **Deregulation test**: Use when regulation itself restricts entry or competition. Compare the original regulatory benefit with the price, quantity, quality, and innovation effects of allowing more firms to compete.

## Key Concepts
- **Natural monopoly**: Market where scale economies make one firm the lowest-cost provider.
- **Average-cost pricing**: Regulation setting price equal to average cost.
- **Trust**: Arrangement in which several firms transfer decision-making power to trustees.
- **Antitrust policy**: Government policy intended to promote competition.
- **Merger**: Combination of two or more firms.
- **Horizontal merger**: Merger between firms producing similar products.
- **Vertical merger**: Merger between firms at different production stages.
- **Tie-in sale**: Requiring a buyer of one product to purchase another product.
- **Predatory pricing**: Pricing below cost to drive out rivals and later raise price.
- **Deregulation**: Removal of rules that restrict competition or entry.

## Mental Models
- **Use the duplicate-infrastructure test**: Ask whether a second network, pipe system, or transmission line would add capacity more cheaply than expanding the existing one.
- **Use `P = ATC` as the regulation floor**: A regulated natural monopoly cannot sustainably charge below average cost without an external subsidy.
- **Use the counterfactual merger test**: Compare prices and quantities with and without the rival, not merely the merged firm's market-share percentage.
- **Use the entry-after-predation test**: If competitors can return when the predator raises price, below-cost pricing is an expensive and weak exclusion strategy.

## Anti-patterns
- **Breaking up every monopoly automatically**: Splitting a natural monopoly can duplicate fixed infrastructure and raise average cost.
- **Assuming average-cost regulation is costless**: The regulated firm may lose incentives to reduce costs, allowing cost creep and higher future regulated prices.
- **Blocking every merger that reduces firm count**: Integration can create economies of scale, better products, or lower costs; assess the net price effect.
- **Relying only on market-share thresholds**: Actual substitutability and observed pricing responses can reveal competitive pressure that simple counts miss.
- **Calling every low price predatory**: Below-cost pricing must be tied to a credible plan to exclude rivals and recoup losses.

## Worked Example
The water-utility example shows why a natural monopoly can need regulation. A single water firm faces long-run marginal cost of $0.80 per cubic meter and declining long-run average cost because the pipe network is an indivisible fixed input. Unregulated profit maximization gives `MR = MC` at 70 million cubic meters, a price of $2.70, and average cost of $2.10, so the firm earns $0.60 per unit. If a second firm enters, each firm serves only part of the market and its demand lies below average cost, so neither can cover a duplicate network. Under average-cost pricing, the regulator chooses the demand/ATC intersection at $1.20 and 150 million cubic meters. Consumers gain lower prices and higher output, but the firm has less incentive to find cost savings because the allowed price follows its average cost.

## Key Takeaways
1. Diagnose natural monopoly through scale economies and infrastructure duplication before prescribing breakup.
2. Use average-cost pricing to achieve the lowest feasible regulated price, while monitoring cost-control incentives.
3. Apply antitrust through breakup, merger review, and business-practice enforcement rather than a single rule.
4. Use real price and substitution data to predict merger effects.
5. Treat predatory pricing as a dynamic claim requiring loss recovery and durable exclusion.
6. Consider deregulation when rules protect incumbents more than consumers or block efficient entry.
