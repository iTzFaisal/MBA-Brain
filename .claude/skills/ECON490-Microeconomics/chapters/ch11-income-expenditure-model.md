# Chapter 11: The Income-Expenditure Model

## Core Idea
The income-expenditure model, or Keynesian cross, explains short-run GDP when prices are sticky and firms produce what is demanded. Equilibrium output is where planned expenditures equal production; inventory changes push firms toward that point. The consumption function, multipliers, taxes, government spending, trade, and the 45-degree line make short-run fluctuations operational.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **45-degree equilibrium model**: Use when prices are treated as fixed and firms supply all demanded output. Plot planned expenditures on the vertical axis and output/income on the horizontal axis. The 45-degree line marks equality; equilibrium is where planned spending crosses it. Excess inventories cause production to fall; depleted inventories cause production to rise.
- **Consumption function**: Use `C = Ca + by`, where `Ca` is autonomous consumption and `b` is the marginal propensity to consume. Wealth and consumer confidence shift the function; a change in MPC changes its slope. `MPC = additional consumption / additional income`, and `MPS = 1 - MPC`.
- **Saving-investment equilibrium**: In a simple closed economy without government, `S = y - C` and equilibrium `y = C + I`, so `S = I`. Use this as an alternative diagnostic for equilibrium income and for understanding why desired saving can reduce income.
- **Multiplier framework**: Use `multiplier = 1/(1 - MPC)` for an autonomous spending, investment, or government-purchase change. With taxes, the tax multiplier is `-MPC/(1 - MPC)`; equal changes in government purchases and taxes have a balanced-budget multiplier of one.
- **Open-economy income-expenditure model**: Add exports as autonomous demand and imports as income-dependent leakage: `M = my`. The adjusted slope is `b - m`, so a higher marginal propensity to import lowers the multiplier and equilibrium domestic output.

## Key Concepts
- **Planned expenditures**: Total desired spending on goods and services.
- **Equilibrium output**: GDP at which planned expenditures equal production.
- **45-degree line**: Locus where output equals expenditures on the graph.
- **Consumption function**: Relationship between consumption spending and income.
- **Autonomous consumption**: Consumption independent of current income.
- **Marginal propensity to consume (MPC)**: Fraction of additional income spent.
- **Savings function**: Relationship between saving and income.
- **Multiplier**: Ratio of the total output change to the initial autonomous spending change.
- **Marginal propensity to import**: Fraction of additional income spent on imports.
- **Income-expenditure model**: Short-run model in which demand determines output at fixed prices.

## Mental Models
- **Use inventories as the adjustment signal**: Excess stock means production exceeded planned spending; depleted stock means firms must increase production.
- **Use the intercept-slope decomposition**: Changes in wealth, confidence, or autonomous spending move the consumption line; changes in MPC rotate it.
- **Use saving equals investment as a cross-check**: At equilibrium in the simple closed model, calculate `S = y - C` and verify it equals planned `I`.
- **Use leakages to assess amplification**: Saving, taxes, and imports interrupt spending rounds and reduce the multiplier.

## Anti-patterns
- **Using the model for long-run price analysis**: It holds prices fixed and is designed for short-run fluctuations.
- **Assuming every increase in inventories signals stronger demand**: Inventories can rise because production exceeded sales and may trigger a cutback.
- **Treating a tax cut as equivalent to government purchases**: Government spending enters demand directly; households save part of a tax cut.
- **Forgetting imports in an open economy**: Domestic consumption can rise while demand for domestic output rises less because some spending leaks abroad.
- **Equating wealth with income**: A house or stock-price change can shift autonomous consumption even when current income is unchanged.

## Worked Example
Let the consumption function be `C = 100 + 0.6y` and fixed investment be `I = 40`. Equilibrium is `y* = (Ca + I)/(1 - MPC) = (100 + 40)/0.4 = 350`. The associated savings function is `S = y - C = -100 + 0.4y`; at `y = 350`, saving is `-100 + 140 = 40`, exactly equal to investment. If investment rises by $20 million and `MPC = 0.8`, the multiplier is 5 and equilibrium income ultimately rises by $100 million, not merely $20 million, because each income round induces further consumption.

## Key Takeaways
1. Locate short-run equilibrium where planned expenditures cross the 45-degree line.
2. Read the consumption function through its autonomous intercept and MPC slope.
3. Use `S = I` as a closed-economy equilibrium check, not as a statement that saving is fixed.
4. Apply the multiplier only after identifying the relevant leakages and tax treatment.
5. Add exports and subtract income-driven imports when translating foreign demand into domestic GDP.
